# 26 — RAND COIN doctrine and scorecards: the enemy's instrument panel, read as a target list

Read-only briefing, 2026-10-25. Question: **the state's counterinsurgency doctrine is a list of
things it believes it must do. What are those things, what do they cost, how does the state know
whether they are working, and where does each one break — for a game in which the player is the
insurgent?**

Builds on `08-babylon-books-networks-policing-and-insurgency.md` (the Topology screen; Sparrow,
Scott, Kalyvas, Weinstein; already uses Paul/Clarke/Grill's scorecard as a warning) and
`17-serge-sparrow-rand-and-bonapartist-state-ai.md` (the state as competing cells; Long's
organizational repertoire; Davis/Cragin on local knowledge). **This briefing does not re-derive
either.** It adds what those two left on the table: (a) Long's *assumptions* as an explicit
vulnerability list, (b) the full Paul/Clarke/Grill factor inventory and its arithmetic as a
candidate dial set — and the reasons not to wire it in, (c) the 2013 expansion's time, sequence,
and durability findings, and (d) Davis/Cragin's support dynamics as the thing the scorecard
measures badly.

cannot touch, or where flipping the sign would be a category error — I say so rather than flip it.

## 0. Thesis

1. **These four books are the state's instrument panel, and every gauge on it is a place the
   player can put a thumb.**
2. Long supplies the doctrine's **assumptions** (support is purchasable, repression ratchets, the
   enemy learns, progress is measurable, bureaucracy can be unified); Paul/Clarke/Grill supply its
   **checklist** (15 good practices, 11–12 bad, one balance score); Davis/Cragin supply its
   **blind spot** (support is a relational portfolio of six commodities, and ascent is not descent).
3. **The scorecard's own arithmetic says it is a diagnostic, not a mechanism**: 30 cases, 8 wins,
   perfect separation, no rankable factor, and 16 equally sufficient QCA patterns — a measurement
   that cannot tell you which input to buy.
4. **For a player who is the insurgent the reading inverts twice**: the state's good practices are
   the player's *denial targets*, and the state's bad practices are the player's *products*.
5. **So: no scorecard in the engine.** The scorecard exists only as a document the state writes,
   argues over, and optimises against — and the player's skill is making the state's own audit say
   the wrong thing.

## 1. Method: three kinds of claim, and one operator

Each book yields three usable things, and they are not interchangeable:

- **Quantities** — what it counts, at what resolution, with what error. A quantity is a candidate
  state variable only if the book shows it *changing* and *mattering*.
- **Conditions** — "X present → Y more likely," with the sample and the comparison stated. These
  are candidate rules. Most are correlational; some are explicitly bivariate and uncontrolled.
- **Failures and sequences** — the named ways the programme breaks, and the order in which things
  are said to happen. These are the most valuable and the least numeric: they give a game its
  failure states and its phase structure.

**The inversion operator.** For a claim about the COIN force, the player-side reading is usually
one of four things, and only the first is a simple sign flip:

| Inversion | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Deny** | the state needs it; the player's job is that it be absent or late | "COIN force intelligence was adequate to disrupt insurgent processes" (V p.72) |
| **Induce** | it is a state *failure*; the player's job is to cause it | "COIN force or government actions contributed to substantial new grievances" (V p.86) |
| **Substitute** | the state's action shifts the player to a different input, at a price | losing population tolerance → looting/crime/external patron (D p.120) |
| **Cannot flip** | a state-capacity or structural fact the player cannot reach | air dominance, terrain, "the government was at least a partial democracy" (V p.xvii) |

I mark every design reading **OURS**. I mark claims **Observed** (a sentence or table in the book)
or **Derived** (my inference from it). §10 is the audit table.

## 2. Long (2006): doctrine as a list of assumptions

Long is the shortest and the most useful, because he is summarising five decades of RAND work for
the Office of the Secretary of Defense and therefore states the doctrine's premises plainly, in the
course of recommending them. **The recommendations are the state's to-do list; the premises are the
player's attack surface.**

### 2.1 Quantities Long actually reports

| Quantity | Value / structure | Where | Game use |
|---|---|---|---|
| HES pacification index | **18 indicators in 6 categories**, each hamlet scored **1–5 (or A–E)**, each *category* given an assessor **confidence rating** | L p.39 | The state's progress number is a survey with a self-reported error bar. Both are corruptible. |
| HES aggregation | "aggregate scores of hundreds of villages were used to measure progress in the war as a whole"; pre-Tet trend upward; post-Tet "the measure was worthless" | L p.39–40 | A metric that is locally valid and globally misleading — exactly the shape of a player-farmable number. |
| Chieu Hoi amnesty | peak 1969: **>47,000 ralliers**, **$350 per capita** (~$1,875 in 2005 dollars) | L p.46 | Price of a defection, and a volume. |
| Malaya reward scale | soldier **$875**; platoon leader **$2,300**; province secretary **$16,000** (2005 dollars); information worth **~75% of surrender value, cumulative** | L p.48 | A price list by rank — i.e., a defection market with a rank gradient. |
| Morice Line | "during the second half of 1960, only **40 men and 40 rifles** were able to enter Algeria"; mobile reserve eventually **40,000 soldiers** | L p.50 | The seal is a **cost ratio**: near-total interdiction bought with a field army. |
| VC force structure | **Main Force / Regional Force / Local Force**, mutually supporting, "had to be balanced as much as possible, leading to difficult resource allocation problems" | L p.37 | The player's own force ladder, with a real trade-off. |
| VC learning cycle | helicopters and APCs arrive early 1960s → manuals, gunlaying tables, anti-APC technique → **Ap Bac, January 1963**: 5 of 15 helicopters down, all but one damaged | L p.37–38 | Time-to-adapt after a state technology shock: roughly two years, and it is *documented and disseminated*, not heroic. |

### 2.2 Conditions: the three theories, as rules

**(a) Hearts and minds (HAM).** Modernisation outruns institutions → institutional gap → disorder →
insurgents offer alternatives and build "counterinstitutions" (taxation, services) → government
isolated → collapse or defeat in battle (L p.21–23). The prescription is security + development +
political rights + less corruption (L p.23). **Assumption:** the state can close the gap it opened.

**(b) Cost/benefit (Wolf, Leites).** "What an insurgent movement requires … is not popular support,
in the sense of attitudes of identification and allegiance, but rather a supply of certain inputs …
at a reasonable cost, interpreting cost to include expenditure of coercion as well as money"
(Wolf 1965, quoted L p.24). Corollary that matters most: **development can lower the insurgent's
input cost** — "programs designed to reduce popular support for insurgents could actually reduce the
insurgent cost for inputs such as food" (L p.25). And the quid pro quo: benefits must be exchanged
for cooperation, not given (L p.25). **Assumption:** the population responds to marginal prices, and
the state can set them.

**(c) The ratchet and the judo.** Repression can stimulate insurgency; the state escalates; the
limit of repression is unavailable to a modern democracy, so the state is "trapped by the ratchet,
having escalated but unable to make the final repressive effort" (L p.26–28). Melnik's judo: "the
counterinsurgent resembles an old boxer, huge but awkward, who becomes the toy of a small and
youthful judo artist" (quoted L p.29). Ellsberg's sharpening: repression changes *preferences*, so
"insurgents would only have to pay x for an input while government might have to pay more for the
same input" (L p.29). **Assumption:** the state's coercion is a neutral instrument. It is not; it
is a price signal that can move against the sender.

**(d) Legitimacy as a termination condition.** Wohlstetter: force must not undercut claims to
legitimacy, because "without legitimacy, there could never be a termination of repression and the
costs associated with it," costs that "could ultimately bankrupt even a powerful empire" (L p.30).
**Assumption:** legitimacy is a constraint the state can satisfy while coercing. Long's own case
says no: Thieu "had more or less successfully followed the recommendations of the cost/benefit
theory, but without building sufficient legitimacy. When stressed by conventional invasion in 1975,
it fell apart" — a **"brittle" regime, "reasonably strong until highly stressed"** (L p.33).

### 2.3 The failure modes Long names

1. **Metric misuse.** HES was a decent hamlet instrument and a catastrophic war instrument; Sweetland
   found it "reasonably reliable … if one accepted that no 'objective' criteria for measuring
   pacification existed" (L p.40). Sweetland's proposed additions are the tell: **VC taxation, VC
   recruitment, and a "freedom of speech" metric** — "the willingness to speak freely would be the
   best possible single index of pacification" (quoted L p.40). The state's best indicator is a
   *behaviour it cannot compel*.
2. **Organizational repertoire.** Komer: the military ran "firepower-intensive 'search-and-destroy'";
   State treated Saigon "as if it were any other government"; USAID used "its normal, peacetime
   methods"; and "below Presidential level everybody and nobody was responsible" (L p.41–42).
   Long's prescription — a unified civil-military council with one senior official — is resisted
   because "bureaucracies, civilian and military … have no interest in performing for COIN in a way
   inconsistent with their values and practices" (L p.58).
3. **Amnesty without follow-up.** Chieu Hoi had "lack of follow-up on ralliers"; they "could be
   re-recruited by the VC," making it "potentially nothing more than a rest-and-recreation program
   for the insurgents"; and it "never attracted any ralliers above the lowest echelon" (L p.47–48).
4. **Protection beats price.** "Above a certain level the amount of money was often less important
   than the defector or informer's estimate of which side could protect him" (Komer, quoted L p.48–49).
5. **Barriers that impede, not prevent.** The Morice Line was "designed to impede and warn, rather
   than to prevent"; effectiveness came from the reserve and fires it cued, not the wire (L p.50).
   Schilling's model conclusion: absent a system that "at least hinders or deters the enemy from
   determining freely his desired infiltration rates, **no model solution leads to conflict
   termination**" (quoted L p.51).
6. **Development that dispossesses.** Border systems threaten smuggling livelihoods: "depriving them
   of their livelihood … is unlikely to gain their loyalty" (L p.71).
7. **Resettlement and identification as double-edged.** Census + ID + resettlement + food control is
   the Malayan package (L p.54), and it is also the package that manufactures grievance.

### 2.4 The sequences Long describes

- **Insurgent adaptation:** state technology shock → observation → experiment → **written manual,
  widely distributed** → battlefield test (Ap Bac). The mechanism is organisational learning
  "inculcated by both Party and Army discipline" (L p.37–38).
- **State adaptation:** the opposite — a repertoire that resists change, and a reform that requires
  overriding existing organisations (L p.58–62).
- **Pacification:** security and development "intimately related," but "efforts to provide one can
  potentially help or hinder the other" (L p.52); progress is local first — "all politics is local"
  (L p.53), RIDA-style local financing (L p.53), local militia (L p.54), then census/ID as the
  precondition of population control (L p.54).

### 2.5 The state's doctrine, stated as assumptions — and the exploit

**This is the core deliverable from Long.** Twelve assumptions, each with the player-side reading
(**OURS**).

| # | The doctrine assumes (Observed) | Where | Player exploit (OURS) |
|---|---|---|---|
| A1 | The institutional gap can be closed faster than it opens | L p.21–23 | Make the state's delivery visible and late; the gap is a schedule, not a level |
| A2 | Support is either attitudes (HAM) or input prices (Wolf) — either way the population is the variable | L p.23–25 | Offer a third thing neither theory prices: **the pleasure of agency** (08 §2.3, Wood) |
| A3 | Benefits can be conditioned on cooperation (quid pro quo) | L p.25 | Make cooperation unverifiable — the state cannot audit a favour |
| A4 | Repression is available in increments and stoppable at the right one | L p.26–28 | Push the state up one increment; the ratchet only turns one way |
| A5 | Coercion is a neutral instrument | L p.29 | Force the state to pay more than you for the same input (Ellsberg's marginal-cost flip) |
| A6 | Legitimacy is a constraint that can be satisfied while coercing | L p.30, 33 | Build a **brittle** state: high compliance, low legitimacy, then stress it once |
| A7 | The enemy is a three-phase automatic process | L p.38 | Be a learning organisation; the state's model of you is a stage, not a loop |
| A8 | Hamlet-level progress can be aggregated into war-level truth | L p.39–40 | Farm the hamlet score; let the aggregate lie for you |
| A9 | Bureaucracies can be unified by command | L p.58 | Work the seams between agencies (17 §4.8); unity is the state's most expensive purchase |
| A10 | Amnesty works if the offer is attractive | L p.45–49 | Make **protection** the binding constraint, not money — then punish defectors credibly |
| A11 | A border can be sealed at acceptable cost | L p.50–51 | Cost the state a field army per border; make the seal the budget |
| A12 | Census + ID + local militia = population control | L p.54, 71–73 | Be the reason the census is wrong; the ID is a node attribute the state can inscribe (08 §1.4) |

**The assumption that fails hardest.** A4/A5 together. Long's own ratchet is not "the state is
weak"; it is "the state's coercive instrument has a **negative marginal return** past a threshold it
cannot locate." The player's job is not to survive repression but to **move the threshold** — to make
each increment buy less and cost more. That is a mechanic, not a mood: the state's repression yield
should be a function of the population's prior, and the player's actions should be able to change
the prior without being attributable (08 §2.2, Kalyvas).

## 3. Paul/Clarke/Grill, *Victory Has a Thousand Fathers* (2010): the scorecard as a parts list

### 3.1 What it measures

30 resolved insurgencies (1978–2008); 86 phases; **76 factors** scored per phase; outcome from the
COIN force's perspective; **8 COIN wins, 22 losses** (V p.xiv–xix, p.93). Phases are analyst-defined
"macro-level … sea-change" breaks, 2–5 per case, and the **decisive phase** is scored, which need not
be the terminal phase (V p.91, p.113–115). **This is the single most important methodological fact
for a game:** the dataset records the *conditions at the point of decision*, not the whole trajectory.

### 3.2 The 15 good and 12 bad practices

V p.xvii (Table S.1), repeated with discussion at p.85–86.

**Good (15):** strategic communication (≥2 factors); tangible insurgent support significantly reduced;
government legitimacy established/maintained; at least partial democracy; adequate intelligence;
sufficient strength to force insurgents to fight as guerrillas; competent government/state; avoided
excessive collateral damage or illegitimate force; sought positive relations with the population;
short-term investment/infrastructure/property reform in COIN-controlled areas; majority of the
population supported the COIN force; established and expanded secure areas; uncontested air
dominance; provided basic services in controlled areas; perception of security created/maintained.

**Bad (12):** collective punishment **and** escalating repression; primary COIN force an external
occupier; COIN/government actions created substantial new grievances; militias working at
cross-purposes; resettlement/removal of civilians; collateral damage perceived as worse than the
insurgents'; COIN force perceived as worse than the insurgents; failed to adapt; more coercion than
the insurgents; insurgents individually superior (more professional or better motivated); COIN force
or allies reliant on looting; COIN force and government had different goals or commitment.

**The separation.** Good minus bad: every win positive, every loss ≤ 0; **highest-scoring loss 0,
lowest-scoring win +5** (V p.86). V's own conclusion is a recommendation to **keep the scorecard and
add it up**: "assess the presence or absence of the good and bad factors … and add them up. … A blank
scorecard can be found at the end of this volume" (V p.96).

### 3.3 What each approach actually did

| Approach | Rule | Losses / Wins implementing | Verdict | Where |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Development | both factors | 0 / 4 | strong support | V p.37–38 |
| Pacification | ≥1 of 3 | 1 / 8 | strong support | V p.39–40 |
| Government legitimacy | ≥2 of 5 | 3 / 7 | strong support | V p.41–42 |
| Legitimate use of force | ≥3 of 5 | 4 / 6 | *some* support | V p.42–44 |
| Democracy | ≥1 of 3 | 7 / 8 | some support; **direction of change matters more than level** (6 of 8 wins had a democracy factor go absent→present; 1 loss did) | V p.44–45 |
| Resettlement | present | **8 / 1** | strong evidence against | V p.47–48 |
| Cost/benefit | ≥2 of 6 | 2 / 8 | strong support | V p.49–50 |
| Border control | flow reduced/absent | 1 / 8 | strong support | V p.50–51 |
| "Crush them" | both factors | **18 / 2** | strong evidence against | V p.51–53 |
| Amnesty/rewards | both factors | 0 / 5 | **cannot be tested** — partly tautological, causal order unknown | V p.53–55 |
| Strategic communication | ≥2 of 7 | 2 / 8 | strong support | V p.55–58 |
| FM 3-24 | ≥3 of 9 | 1 / 7 | strong support | V p.58–61 |
| Beat cop | ≥2 of 6 | 4 / 8 | strong support | V p.61–63 |
| Boots on the ground | ≥2 of 4 | 2 / 6 | strong support | V p.64–65 |
| Local face / cultural awareness | — | — | **untestable** (external COIN force in only 3 of 30 cases) | V p.66–69 |
| Tangible support reduction | ≥3 of 10 | **0 / 8** | the only stack that perfectly predicts | V p.69–71 |
| Intelligence | ≥1 of 2 | 0 / 6 | strong; but all predictive power is in **"disrupt processes,"** not kill/capture | V p.72–73 |
| Flexibility/adaptability | single factor | 6 / 8 | strong, and **necessary**: no case or phase was won by a force that failed to adapt | V p.73–74 |
| Insurgent support strategies | ≥1 of 3 | 22 / 7 | **strong evidence against** | V p.76–77 |
| Continuation & contestation | all 4 | **18 / 0** | strong support — "if insurgents can hang on until the end, they appear to prevail" | V p.77–79 |

**Two facts in that table are worth more than the rest.** (1) **Repression wins phases, not cases**:
in 19 intermediate phases the COIN force had the upper hand and still lost the case; in **17 of
those 19** both "crush them" factors were present (V p.53). (2) **Tangible support beats popular
support**: in 25 of 30 cases the two covaried; in the three cases where the government had popular
support but did not cut tangible support, **the insurgents won all three**; in the two cases where
the COIN force cut support without gaining popularity, it won both (V p.70–72).

### 3.4 Failure modes named

- **Resettlement without compensation** is the paradigm failure: 9 cases used it, 1 won (Turkey);
  the analyst notes the "relocated populations were sufficiently compensated" factor was present in
  **no phase of any case** (V p.47–48). The approach was never actually implemented as specified.
- **Militias**: used in 25 cases, 6 wins; **15 of 25 worked at cross-purposes, and 13 of those 15
  were losses** (V p.63).
- **Amnesty**: cannot separate "offer causes win" from "winning causes offer" (V p.54–55).
- **"Not worse than the insurgents"** is necessary-ish, not sufficient: present in most wins and in
  nine (collateral) / seven (perception) losses (V p.43–44).
- **The good-practice pack has no ranking**: QCA returns **16 equally plausible minimally sufficient
  patterns** and cannot discriminate (V p.89–91). This is V's own admission that the scorecard is
  underdetermined as a causal model.

## 4. *Paths to Victory* (2013): the same programme at 71 cases — time, sequence, durability

### 4.1 The larger dataset and the doctrinal split

71 resolved insurgencies (WWII–2010), **204 phases**, **~289 factors per phase**, ~58,000 cells
(P p.xix, p.150, p.179). 59 "core" cases after removing the "against the tide of history" cases
(end of colonialism, end of apartheid) and one indeterminate outcome (P p.xix–xx). 24 concepts
tested; **17 strong support, 1 strong evidence against** ("crush them": 33 implementers, **23 lost**)
(P p.xxi–xxiii).

The new organising distinction is a **2×2** (P p.7, Fig. 1.1; p.xxx):

- axis 1, **target**: insurgent support vs active insurgents;
- axis 2, **action**: diminish motives vs kinetic.

Quadrants: *legitimacy/development/politics* (support × motives); *population and border control*
(support × kinetic); *reform and redress* (insurgents × motives); *traditional enemy-centric COIN*
(insurgents × kinetic). "Iron fist" ≈ lower-right; "motive-focused" ≈ upper-left, usually balanced
with kinetic. **Result: iron fist won 32%, motive-focused/mixed won 73%** (P p.xxix, p.180).

### 4.2 Quantities that are new relative to V

| Quantity | Value | Where |
|---|---|---|
| Duration, all 71 | median **118 months** (~9.8 yr); mean 128; range given as 3–420 months in the footnote but "roughly nine months (Bangladesh)" to 35 years in the text | P p.158 and n.11 | the 3-vs-9 discrepancy is in the source |
| Duration by outcome, all 71 | wins median **132**, losses **95** | P p.158, p.223 |
| Duration, 59 core | median **105**; wins **132**, losses **72** | P p.223 |
| Iron fist vs motive-focused duration | **94** vs **126** months (medians); iron fist wins **138.5** (median) vs iron fist losses **60** ("on average" — source mixes the two statistics) | P p.223 |
| Time from positive scorecard to victory | median **69 months** (~5.75 yr); mean 101, s.d. 95 | P p.162 |
| Peace intervals | 35 of 71 ended; mean **7.33 years** | P p.164 |
| Durable-peace factors | government reform; ethical/professional/human-rights military reform; **significant economic disruption** — each ~4–5× more likely to endure | P p.165 |
| Effective police/paramilitaries | 23 cases, **69% COIN win** | P p.152 |
| Counter-gangs / scouts / ferret forces | **7 of 7 COIN wins** | P p.152 |
| External professional military on the *insurgent* side | **13 of 14 COIN losses** | P p.151 |

### 4.3 The revised scorecard (15 good / 11 bad)

P p.xxvi, p.141–142. Changes from V are instructive: "uncontested air dominance" and "external COIN
actor" **drop out**; "unity of effort" and "reconstruction above historical baseline" come in; on the
bad side, **"corrupt and arbitrary personalistic government rule," "host-nation elites had perverse
incentives to continue the conflict," "an external professional military fighting for the insurgents,"
"host nation economically dependent on external supporters," and "fighting was primarily initiated by
the insurgents"** are added (P p.142).

Scores: 59 core cases, **highest loss −1, lowest win +2** — a three-point gap, perfect separation
(P p.142, Table 5.1).

**Seven factors explicitly *not* needed to discriminate** the 59 cases: air dominance; external COIN
actor; terrain; Islamic insurgency; communist insurgency; grievances substantially resolved; ethnic
or religious violence (P p.147). The authors' gloss: each may matter in a narrative, none is
essential at this level of analysis. **For the game this list is the set of things the player will
believe matter and the state's model says do not** — which is itself a fog mechanic.

### 4.4 The three "priority" concepts

QCA on the 59 core cases returns a two-factor prime implicant — **tangible support reduction +
commitment and motivation** — and then a four-factor set present in every win and no loss:
**commitment and motivation, tangible support reduction, flexibility and adaptability, and at least
two of {unity of effort, initiative, intelligence}** (P p.149–150). P therefore names three
priorities: **tangible support, commitment, adaptability** (P p.xxiii, p.150).

**Observed caution:** "commitment and motivation" is a composite of eight *absence* factors
(no looting, no kleptocracy, no perverse elite incentives, no external dependency, etc.) (P p.129).
It is a **negative** index — the state scores well by not being predatory. That is a very different
kind of quantity from "tangible support," and it should not be implemented as one meter.

### 4.5 Sequence — the most directly gameable finding

P p.156–158, under an explicit caveat that the data are presence-per-phase, not event-timed.

- **Foundational, usually before the decisive phase:** commitment/motivation (26 of 28 wins);
  overmatch forcing guerrillas (22 of 28); flexibility (18 of 28); insurgents not superior (17 of 28).
- **Intermediate:** intelligence (21 wins, before decisive in 10); popular support (16, before in 11);
  government legitimacy (17, before in 14).
- **Late, rarely before the decisive phase:** beat cop (15 wins, 11 late); initiative (21, 15 late);
  strategic communication (11, 9 late); perception of security (14, 10 late).

**The monotonicity finding.** All 28 wins had a scorecard ≥ 2 by the decisive phase, and "almost all
28 had scorecard scores that increased monotonically … This was not the case for losing cases. Many
of these cases saw a scorecard peak in an intermediate phase, which fell off dramatically toward the
end of the case (**Vietnam being the strongest example**)" (P p.156). **A state can get better and
then worse; the shape of the curve, not its level, is the outcome.**

**Transition paths** from negative to positive (20 cases that were not positive throughout):
force revamp + strategy change (9); experience-based progression (4); external actor holds while
indigenous forces improve (4); external actor takes over (2); "back into" a positive score by
insurgent error + withdrawal of insurgent external support (1) (P p.163–164). **The last path is the
player's nightmare and should be a real ending: you lose without being defeated.**

### 4.6 External actors — the player's best and worst lever

- "You can't want it more than they do": **no externally supported government prevailed without
  commitment** (P p.175, p.xxix).
- Insurgents with an external professional military: **13 of 14 COIN losses**, even if the COIN force
  also had one (P p.151).
- Coordination of diverse COIN forces with significant external ground troops: present in **7 of 7
  wins**, absent in **7 of 8 losses** (P p.176).
- Indigenous willingness to take casualties: present in **13 of 14** winning external-actor cases
  (P p.177).
- External actor as primary COIN force: 7 stayed, 4 won; 6 drew down, 2 won (P p.177). Small n;
  P says the inference is only indicative.

### 4.7 The questions P leaves open — i.e., the design space

P's final chapter asks, verbatim: **"What Factors Lead to Insurgent Success? … What are the best
practices for overthrowing and defeating governments, and what efforts to support insurgents are most
highly correlated with success?"** and **"How Do Insurgencies End? … What conditions are necessary
for, or increase the likelihood of, negotiated settlements? What factors make cease-fires durable?
What DDR approaches are effective?"** (P p.191). **The corpus's own authors say the insurgent-side
model has not been built.** That is the gap this briefing is allowed to fill only by inversion and

## 5. Davis & Cragin (2009): support dynamics, the thing the scorecard measures badly

### 5.1 Support is not sympathy, and not one thing

- The literature conflates **"sympathetic of"** (expressions, polls) with **"supporting"** (material
  aid, abetment); Paul uses "support" only for the latter (D p.115–116). The gap is not noise: under
  a penetrated polling entity, "a group's most ardent financial supporters might decline to answer a
  pollster or might offer a 'cover' answer" (D p.115).
- **Six needs:** manpower (incl. skills), funding, materiel, intelligence, sanctuary, **tolerance of
  activities** (D p.117–118).
- **Need magnitude** is a function of **group size, goals, nature of operations, and covertness**
  (D p.119); the two ideal types (mass guerrilla vs small covert cell) are not to be trusted, and
  covertness is contested: some say clandestine groups need least, some say most (D p.119–120).
- **Four supply methods:** self-supply, looting, purchasing, external source. External sources:
  communities, states, diasporas, charities/NGOs, organised crime, other groups (D p.120).
- **Passive support is a real commodity** — intelligence, sanctuary, tolerance — and can be enough
  (D p.118–119). Social silencing or subtle coercion produces it (D p.119).

### 5.2 The support decision, as a rule

Three chains, combined with approximate **and**, each fed by **or**-factors (D p.138–139, Fig. 4.3):

1. **felt need for resistance / action by proxy for a public good** ← normative acceptability of
   violence, charged negative emotions, desire for revenge, grievances, occupation, regime
   illegitimacy, lack of political expression/repression;
2. **identification with the group** ← shared goals, group intimacy, misperception/self-deception,
   ideology/social-movement framing, legitimacy;
3. **social pressures and incentives** ← cultural obligations, kinship/fictive kinship, intimidation,
   future benefit (prestige, bandwagon, discount parameter), group provision of social services.

Paul's own caveat: "None is strictly necessary and any could be sufficient in extremis" (D p.139).
**This is the anti-scorecard.** A support model with substitutable necessary-ish conditions and no
fixed weights is exactly what the Paul scorecard cannot represent, and exactly what a game can.

### 5.3 What the group controls, and what it cannot

D p.141 (Table 4.1) rates each factor **likely / important / amenable to policy**. The pattern:

- **Mutable:** grievances/revenge; shared goals; net incentives; provision of social services;
  group legitimacy (partly); propaganda (partly).
- **Not mutable:** kinship/fictive kinship; cultural obligations; humiliation/alienation/hatred
  (only partly); normative acceptability of violence (marked "important" but not policy-mutable).

**Group-controlled levers** (D p.130–134): intimidation (effective for *passive* support: "where
'collaborators' are punished, average citizens are much more likely to keep their heads down");
propaganda, including **"propaganda by deed"** and the **provocation of disproportionate response**;
provision of social services ("areas where a terrorist or insurgent group is the only provider of
such services contain the highest levels of support"); identification/legitimacy; and **unacceptable
group behaviour**, which produces backlash (D p.133).

### 5.4 Radicalisation is a group process, and cost selects for ties

- "Socialization processes are a necessary precondition for radicalization" (D p.xxiv).
- Recruitment runs top-down, bottom-up peer groups, or both; venues include prisons, radical families,
  religious settings, the internet (D p.76–80). Internet networks "operate at their best when they are
  backed by real social linkages in specifically localized communities" (della Porta & Diani, quoted
  D p.80–81).
- **The load-bearing sentence for a game:** "the more costly and dangerous the collective action, the
  stronger and more numerous the ties required for individuals to participate" (della Porta & Diani,
  quoted D p.76). Cost is a **topology filter**, not a morale penalty.
- Alienation produces "cognitive openings" — a crisis that "shakes certainty in previously accepted
  beliefs" (Wiktorowicz, quoted D p.81).

### 5.5 Decline: eight modes, one spiral, and one asymmetry

**Eight modes** (D p.259–260): substantial success; partial success; direct state action incl.
repression; disintegration through burnout; loss of leaders; unsuccessful generational transition;
loss of popular/external support; emergence of new alternatives.

**The support-loss spiral** (D p.272–273): support ↓ → resources ↓ and intelligence ↑ → attrition and
disruption ↑ → hiding → sense of failure → dissent/defection → isolation → poor decisions → escalation
→ backlash → further loss. "This process is often self-reinforcing, thrusting the organization into a
downward spiral."

**Three asymmetries that a game must model separately:**

1. **Ascent ≠ descent.** "Descent does not necessarily mirror ascent" (D p.387–389); root causes and
   sustaining factors overlap in only three items — perceived illegitimacy, repression, lack of
   opportunity (D p.383–384). Radicalisation is socialising; **disengagement is isolating** (D p.389).
   And **"pull factors are more effective than push factors"** (D p.xxxvii–xxxviii).
2. **Splits increase violence.** Organizational disintegration "does not put an end to terrorism when
   a sizable demand for terrorism remains"; rivals "outbid each other" (D p.267–268).
3. **Amnesty only works when support is already low** — "cannot generate decline on its own"
   (D p.273). Decapitation: evidence uncertain, network nodes unequal, and backlash conditions are
   real (D p.268–271).

### 5.6 Public support is important and not a silver bullet

Terrorists weigh at least six things when choosing: positive reaction in the relevant population;
advancement of group strategy; positive reaction *inside* the group; acceptability of risk; level of
resources committed; sufficiency of information (D p.386). A group can trade popular support for an
operational objective — AQI's Amman hotels are the example (D p.386–387). **So "popular support" is a
constraint the player prices, not a resource the player maximises.** That is the correct shape for a
Babylon meter and the opposite of a hearts-and-minds bar.

### 5.7 Davis on how to represent this without lying

D's own methodological chapter is the warrant for refusing a linear scorecard: qualitative factors
with discrete values; **factor trees** with near-hierarchical decomposition; explicit **and/or**
combining logic; **non-monotonic** effects (including the inverted-U of democratisation vs terrorism);
feedbacks; **thresholds and ceilings** (critical-mass effects); hidden variables; and the rule that
"the objective of analysis in social science should often not be reliable 'prediction,' but rather an
understanding of possibilities and perhaps of rough probabilities, or odds" (D p.409–421, quote p.421).

**Observed:** D is describing *analysis*, not game design. **Derived (OURS):** it is nonetheless the
best available specification for a rules engine that is legible without being solvable — and it comes
from inside RAND, which is why it can be cited against the scorecard without importing outside theory.

## 6. The inversion: the state's checklist as the player's production plan

### 6.1 The sign-flip table

The scorecard is **side-relative, not moral**. Six entries demonstrate it.

|---|---|---|

| "Insurgent force individually superior … more professional or better motivated" — **bad** (V p.86) | state | **Professionalism is the player's core asset**, and the thing that makes the state's amnesty offer credible or not. |

(P p.162). **The player does not need to break the pack — only to make one component late, every
period, and let the state pay for the rest.**

**Rule that follows.** Every entry above is either a **relation**, a **vector**, a **per-county**
quantity, or a **threshold comparison**. None is a national scalar. If the engine has one national
number called "COIN progress," the design has already failed D1 regardless of how the number is
computed.

### 6.4 The player's actual win condition, inverted

V's insurgent-side findings give the player's victory condition, and it is **not** the mirror
of a COIN win. "Continuation and contestation" — all four of: force maintained or grown;
COIN/government discredited; resource replenishment not significantly diminished; no critical
strategic error or obvious failure to adapt — predicts **18 of 22 COIN losses and 0 of 8 wins**
(V p.77–79). **Observed.** P drops both insurgent-side concepts from its 24 (P p.xxii) — the
insurgent model is not merely wrong, it is absent from the later dataset. The player's win condition
is therefore a **survival-and-delegitimation conjunction**, not a territorial or attritional one.

**OURS: three corollaries.**

1. **The player's "score" is the state's failure to score.** There is no insurgent points total; there
   is a state audit that must be made wrong.
2. **The player's loss states are the state's good practices, not the state's victories.** You lose by
   becoming legible (overmatch → forced guerrilla), by becoming predatory (looting → new grievances →
   popular support for the state), by becoming a client (external professional military with a
   principal), or by becoming absorbable (a seat, a legal party, a paragraph).
3. **Time is not neutral.** The state's clock is 69 months *after* it gets its pack; the median
   insurgency is 118 months; the state can lose the case while winning phases (17 of 19). The player's
   clock is cadre reproduction and legitimacy, which the state cannot see (D p.115–116). **The player
   wins on the second clock; the state thinks it is winning on the first.**

## 7. Design readings — OURS, not the authors'

Each is a proposal with a falsifier. None is a claim about what RAND said.

**D1 — The scorecard is a document, not a function.**
The state computes its own audit (15 good, 11 bad), prints a score, argues about the missing cells, and
optimises against it. The engine computes *consequences* (support, legitimacy, capacity, exposure) and
never sums points. The player can capture the audit and read the state's self-diagnosis.
*Ground:* V p.96 recommends exactly the sum; V p.89–91 shows the sum cannot rank its inputs; 08 R3
already rules the linear engine out.
*Falsifier:* if the audit's score and the engine's outcome never diverge, the document is decorative
and should be cut.

**D2 — Compliance and legitimacy are two axes, and their gap is "brittle."**
A county can be quiet and unloyal. Long's Thieu is the type case: incentives bought compliance,
legitimacy was never built, and the regime "melted away" under stress (L p.33). The state's HES-like
instrument reads the first axis and reports the second.
*Ground:* L p.33 (brittle), p.39–40 (instrument), V p.41–42 (legitimacy), V p.44–45 (direction of
democratisation matters more than level).
*Falsifier:* if no player action can raise compliance while lowering legitimacy, the second axis is
not a mechanic.

**D3 — Support is a six-commodity portfolio with substitutable sources.**
No "support" bar. Manpower / funding / materiel / intelligence / sanctuary / tolerance, each with a
source mix (population, external state, diaspora, charity, crime, self-supply). Denying one source
shifts the mix and changes the political price (D p.117–120).
*Ground:* D p.117–120; V p.69–71 (the state's best play is exactly this portfolio); P p.132.
*Falsifier:* if players never switch sources, the substitutability is fake and the portfolio collapses
to one bar.

**D4 — Recruitment is gated by tie strength, not persuasion.**
Costly action requires strong and numerous ties (D p.76, della Porta & Diani). So growth is a topology
problem: a node cannot be recruited cheaply into a high-cost role, and a dense clique is hard to
infiltrate but also hard to grow. Radicalisation is a group process with venues (prison, family,
congregation, workplace, internet) (D p.76–80).
*Ground:* D p.76, p.xxiv.
*Falsifier:* if a high-cost action can be staffed by strangers at the same price as by intimates, the
gate is not real.

**D5 — Two clocks, neither displayed.**
State clock: months since the pack was first positive (median 69; P p.162). Player clock: cadre
reproduction and the legitimacy cost of substitution. The state's public metric is a third, faster
clock that both sides can farm.
*Ground:* P p.162, p.158; L p.39–40; V p.53 (phases vs cases).
*Falsifier:* if the two clocks always expire together, one of them is redundant.

**D6 — Repression is a phase weapon that loses cases.**
Model the state as able to take the "upper hand" in a phase by repression while accumulating a
case-level bad-practice stock. 17 of 19 phases where the COIN force had the upper hand and lost had
both "crush them" factors (V p.53). The player's correct response to being hurt is to make the phase
win expensive in case terms, not to win the phase.
*Ground:* V p.51–53; P p.xxiii (33 implementers, 23 lost).
*Falsifier:* if repression is never locally effective, the ratchet has no temptation and the mechanic
is moralism.

**D7 — Amnesty is a three-price market: money, protection, prospects.**
Protection dominates money above a threshold (L p.48–49); effectiveness is partly tautological because
winning produces defections (V p.54–55); it only works when support is already low (D p.273). So the
player's counter is to make protection incredible — which requires punishing defectors — which costs
legitimacy. A genuine dilemma, not a resist-stat.
*Ground:* L p.45–49; V p.53–55; D p.273.
*Falsifier:* if the player's optimal response to amnesty is always "ignore it," the market has no
price.

**D8 — External patronage is the strongest lever and it imports a principal.**
13 of 14 cases with an external professional military on the insurgent side were COIN losses
(P p.151); but the same literature says the patron's interests are not the client's and the
host-nation-commitment rule ("you can't want it more than they do") applies symmetrically to the
player's patron.
*Ground:* P p.151, p.175, p.xxix.
*Falsifier:* if patron support never constrains the player's goals, the principal is a free resource
and the strongest lever is a cheat.

**D9 — The state's metric is farmable, and the state knows it is being farmed but cannot stop.**
HES: 18 indicators, 6 categories, 1–5 scoring, per-category confidence rating, aggregated to a war
claim (L p.39–40). The confidence rating is the tell: the assessor's uncertainty is itself a number
that can be managed.
*Ground:* L p.39–40; V p.72–73 (the predictive half of "intelligence" is disruption, not killing).
*Falsifier:* if farming the metric never changes the state's resource allocation, the metric is
cosmetic.

**D10 — Ascent and descent are different systems.**
The state's counter-recruitment actions do not demobilise; the player's decline is disengagement
(isolating, individual, pull-driven) rather than deradicalisation (collective, push-driven)
(D p.387–389, p.xxxvii–xxxviii). So the player's internal decay must be modelled as **exit**, not as
belief change.
*Ground:* D p.383–389.
*Falsifier:* if the same variable both prevents recruitment and causes exit, the asymmetry is
unmodelled.

**D11 — The player can tempt the state into iron fist.**
Iron fist wins 32%, motive-focused 73% (P p.xxix); iron fist losses are *fast* (median 60 months vs
wins 138.5; P p.223); resettlement is 8 losses to 1 win (V p.47–48); "crush them" 23 of 33 lost
(P p.xxiii). The player's highest-value action may be to make the state's repertoire look adequate
while the case is being lost — Ellsberg's marginal-cost flip as a doctrine trap (L p.29).
*Ground:* P p.xxix, p.223; V p.47–48; L p.29.
*Falsifier:* if iron fist is never locally attractive to the state's cells, there is nothing to tempt.

**D12 — The player's status is read, not displayed.**
The player learns their own position from the state's documents (arrests, rewards, patrol density,
amnesty traffic) and from population behaviour (who speaks in the meeting, who stops volunteering).
No insurgent dashboard. This is 08 §3.3's rule extended to the scorecard's own categories.
*Ground:* 08 §3.3; D p.115–116 (the poll/support gap); L p.40 (freedom of speech as the best index).
*Falsifier:* if the player cannot infer standing from behaviour, the fog is unreadable rather than
systematic (08 R1).

**D13 — "Poor beginnings" is a phase rule, not a difficulty setting.**
21 of 30 cases (V p.91–92) and 49 of 71 (P p.155) had the eventual loser ahead in at least one phase.
So the engine must permit a side to be losing the case while winning the current phase, and must not
treat a phase win as progress.
*Ground:* V p.91–92; P p.154–155.
*Falsifier:* if phase outcomes and case outcomes are highly correlated in play, the phase layer is
redundant.

**D14 — Absorption is the player's most common real ending, and it is a *win* for the state's politics.**
The state's optimal play is politics plus policing (08 §2.5), and reform is the only factor that
extends the post-conflict peace (P p.165). So the "victory" screen must be able to show a player who
won the war and lost the movement: a seat, a legal party, a paragraph, a demobilisation.
*Ground:* P p.165; V p.44–45; 08 §2.5 (Jones & Libicki base rates).
*Falsifier:* if absorption is never available after a player's military success, D14 is a scripted
ending rather than a mechanic.

## 9. Uncertainties, and what would change my mind

1. **The scorecard's separation is partly an artefact of factor selection.** V and P selected the 15
   good / 11–12 bad entries *after* bivariate screening (V p.84–85; P p.140–141) and concede "at least
   as much art as science" in which factors made it (P p.146–147). Perfect separation on
   post-selected factors is weaker evidence than it looks. **I do not treat the score as causal.**
2. **"Commitment and motivation" is a negative index** (eight absence conditions; P p.129). It is
   probably measuring "the state is not predatory," which may be a proxy for underlying state capacity.
   **Uncertain** whether it is a lever or a symptom.
3. **"Fighting initiated by the insurgents" as a bad COIN practice** (P p.142) is very likely a proxy
   for insurgent strength/initiative at onset, not a practice the COIN force chose. I use it only as an
   illustration of the scorecard's side-relativity, not as a mechanic.
4. **P's QCA prime implicants are not causal estimates.** The authors say so; the 59-case core and the
   empirical cut-points are chosen to maximise prediction (P p.209–210). Treat D1/D5/D11 as design
   hypotheses, not findings.
5. **D's claims are literature consensus, not measurement.** Where D says "the literature agrees," it
   often means "several case-study authors assert." Paul's own chapter says the empirical base "does
   not allow us to say with confidence which factors … are either causally absolutely necessary or
   absolutely sufficient" (D p.135).
6. **The 69-month figure has enormous variance** (mean 101, s.d. 95; P p.162 n.16). As a design constant
   it is a planning point, not a law. **Do not** hard-code it as a threshold (cf. ADR172 r5's ban on
   imposed functional forms, 17 §1.5).
7. **The V→P revisions cut both ways.** Air dominance and external COIN actor dropped out of the
   scorecard between 2010 and 2013 (P p.147). If a factor can leave the checklist when the sample
   grows, the checklist is a snapshot of a literature, not a structure of the world.
8. **I have not read the companion case-study volumes** (`Victory Has a Thousand Fathers: Detailed
   Counterinsurgency Case Studies`, `Paths to Victory: Detailed Insurgency Case Studies`) or the
   downloadable spreadsheets. All case-level colour here is second-hand from the analytic volumes.
9. **Long is 2006 and pre-surge.** His Iraq/Afghanistan recommendations (PRTs, ID cards, border
   barriers) are period pieces; I use his *theory* chapters (4–5) and not his policy chapter (6) except
   where the theory is restated.
10. **Nothing here measures an insurgent-side effect.** P explicitly says the insurgent-side model is
    unbuilt (P p.191). Every player-side rule in §6–7 is **Derived**.

## 12. Closing

Long's doctrine is a list of things the state must do **at once, for years, through organisations that
do not want to do them**, measured by an instrument whose local readings are decent and whose aggregate
is a lie. Paul/Clarke/Grill turn that list into a checklist and prove, on their own data, that the
checklist separates wins from losses perfectly and cannot tell you which box to tick first.
*Paths to Victory* adds the two facts a game most needs: **the state's pack takes about six years to
pay off after it is finally assembled**, and **the state can win every phase and lose the case**.
Davis and Cragin supply the thing the checklist keeps mis-measuring: support is a portfolio of six
commodities from substitutable sources, chosen under three simultaneous logics, and it decays by a
different path than the one it grew by.

**The player is not the negative image of a COIN force.** The player is the reason the state's audit
is wrong. The state's good practices are the player's denial targets; the state's bad practices are
the player's products; and the player's real victory is a conjunction the state's own researchers
measured and then filed under "questions for further research" — *maintain the force, discredit the
government, keep the supply open, and do not make the obvious mistake* (P p.191; V p.77–79).

The Okhrana drew the best network chart in Russia and lost to a bread queue (17 §2.4). The 2013 RAND
volume, briefing the Pentagon, wrote down that it did not know how insurgencies win, and asked. That
is the specification for the enemy — and the design brief for the player.
