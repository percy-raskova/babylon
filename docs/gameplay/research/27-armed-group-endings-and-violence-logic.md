# 27 — Armed group endings and the violence logic of control

Read-only briefing, 2026-10-25. Question: **how does an armed group stop, what does the county's balance
of control let each side do to civilians there, and what decides whether a movement's armed phase pays?**

**Builds on, does not repeat.** `research/08` §2.1–2.5 already reads Weinstein, Kalyvas, Wood and J/L as

---

## 0. Thesis

1. Babylon models how an armed group **grows** (`research/16`) and what its **graph** looks like (`research/08`);
   it has no model of how one **stops** — and in the historical record, almost none of them lose.
2. Armed groups are not defeated so much as **closed**: by a bargain, by a courtroom, by fragmentation into
   someone else's group. Every closure has a precondition, and the precondition is almost always administrative.
3. The county is where the whole thing becomes computable: **control decides who can inform, information decides
   whether violence can be selective, and selectivity decides whether violence pays** — in that order, with no
   step skippable.
4. **Funding selects doctrine before any leader does**, and **reputation is set by the first unpunished atrocity,
   not by the average**. Both are latches; neither is a meter, and neither can be walked back cheaply.
5. The state's real weapon is **legibility**; the movement's is the **everyday resistance legibility cannot see** —
   and armed struggle is what happens when that weapon fails, not what happens when it succeeds.

# Part I — The five ways a group ends, and what each one costs the state

## 1.1 The base rates, exactly

J/L code 648 groups active 1968–2006. Of those, 244 were still active at the close of the data, 404 had ended,
and **136 of the 404 ended by splintering** — which they exclude, "since the members still used terrorism"
(p. 19 n. 33; p. 13). The 268 remaining, grouped by *primary* reason:

| Basin | Share of 268 | Count | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Politics** (transition to nonviolent political process) | 43% | 114 | J/L p. 19, Fig. 2.1 |
| **Policing** (local police/intelligence arrest or kill key members) | 40% | 107 | J/L p. 19, Fig. 2.1 |
| **Victory** (objectives achieved) | 10% | 27 | J/L p. 19 n. 33 |
| **Military force** | 7% | 20 | J/L p. 19 n. 33 |
| **Splintering** (excluded from the above) | 28% of 404 | 136 | J/L p. 19 n. 33 |

The five-way list is stated as such at J/L p. 13: "There are at least five major ways in which terrorist groups
end: policing, military force, splintering, politics, or victory." The authors are explicit that other
instruments — aid, sanctions, diplomacy, target-hardening — "are too weak to be used in a leading role" (p. 13).

**Two findings that matter more than the headline percentages.**

- **Once a group becomes an insurgency, the mix changes.** "Nearly 50 percent of the time, groups ended by
  negotiating a settlement with the government; 25 percent of the time, they achieved victory; and 19 percent of
  the time, military forces defeated them" (J/L p. xv). Armed strength buys a seat, not a scalp.
- **Upper-income countries are a different basin entirely.** Among the 96 ended groups in upper-income states,
  **92 ended via policing or politics** (J/L p. 37). That is the regime Babylon's first epoch is set in, and it
  is the single most load-bearing number in the book for this project.

## 1.2 The basins have preconditions, and they are not interchangeable

Each basin is a **closure operation** with an entry condition, a cost paid by the *state*, and a characteristic
failure when forced early. Extracted, not inferred:

| Basin | Entry condition (quoted or near-quoted) | What the state pays | Failure signature when forced | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Politics** | Goals are narrow — "the narrower the goals … the more likely the government and terrorist group may be to agree on a settlement"; "the possibility of a political solution is inversely linked to the breadth of terrorist goals" | Concedes the policy; legitimises the interlocutor | Broad goals leave "no bargaining room": al Qa'ida's caliphate "offer[s] no bargaining room with western governments" | J/L pp. xiii, 19, 21, 64, 79 |
| | Also needs a **hurting stalemate** and/or **loss of an outside patron**: "a decline in outside support … can cut off a key source of funding and increase a group's willingness to settle"; "the inability of either side … to defeat the other over a period of years may also increase the likelihood of a negotiated settlement" | | FMLN's outside assistance "quickly begin[s] to dry up" after 1991; that is the door, not the argument | J/L pp. 21, 79 |
| **Policing** | Group has "yet to reach 1,000 members"; police have permanent local presence, human intelligence, and a **legal predicate** | Legitimacy (surveillance law, civil-liberties cost); court time | "Almost never effective when groups are larger"; also weak in "a weak state, little political will … or powerful insurgent groups" | J/L pp. 40, 60, 61 |
| **Military** | Group "more than 100 members" and actually insurgent — "large, well armed, and well organized"; 70% of military successes were against groups >100 | Civilian casualties; "a significant risk of turning the local population against the government" | Against groups under 100 it displaces rather than destroys: "once the situation in an area becomes untenable for terrorists, they will simply transfer their activity to another area" | J/L pp. xiv, 31, 32 |
| **Victory** | Peak size >10,000 (>25% victory rate); "victory is rare when groups are smaller than 1,000 members"; **no religious group that ended achieved victory** | The state itself | A group that cannot grow past 1,000 has effectively been denied this basin at birth | J/L p. xiv |
| **Splintering** | Competitive resource market: "groups that fail to secure a sufficient amount of support may break apart as members scatter to bigger and more powerful groups" | Nothing — the state's problem persists | **This is the false ending**: "the end of a group does not signal the end of terrorism by its members" | J/L pp. 13, 39 |

**The most-quoted sentence in the book is a warning about the wrong instrument**, and it belongs in the design:
"Military force has rarely been the primary reason for the end of terrorist groups (7 percent), and few groups
since 1968 have achieved victory (10 percent)" (J/L p. 19).

## 1.3 The size variable is doing almost all the work

J/L's own statistics are deflationary about everything except size:

> "The best single-factor correlation was with peak size, which alone explained 32 percent of the variance in
> outcome scores (i.e., r² = 0.32). No other single variable on its own explained more than 4 percent of the
> variance. Remarkably, the addition of all other explanatory variables together improved the explanatory power
> only from 32 to 34 percent — or hardly at all." (p. 41)

They immediately flag the obvious objection: "size may be endogenous to duration … groups that last longer may
be larger simply because they have more time to recruit new members" (p. 41). So the correct reading is
**not** "make the group big and you win." It is: **size is the state's best available proxy for whether the
group has crossed out of the policing basin**, and the threshold at 1,000 is the only sharp line in the data.

For the record on what is *not* predictive: "there is no correlation between the duration of terrorist groups
and ideological motivation, economic conditions, regime type, or the breadth of terrorist goals" (J/L p. 40).

## 1.4 Design reading — OURS

**(a) Persistence is the default state, and ending is an operation with an eligibility test.** The engine should
not run a "war weariness" bar that eventually opens a peace dialog. It should hold, per armed actor,
`peak_membership`, `goal_breadth` (ordinal: status quo / policy / territorial / regime / empire / social
revolution — J/L's own six-point scale, p. 18), `patron_flow`, `legal_predicate`, and `local_information`, and at
each closure checkpoint ask *which basin is currently eligible*. If none is, the actor simply continues — which is
the 244/648 case (38%) and the honest base rate. **A war that does not end must be the modal outcome, or the
player learns that history has a finish line.**

**(b) The endgame label is derived, never stored** — same rule as `research/16` §2.2, for the same reason. Store
the five quantities; render one word in the Wiki, and only after the fact.

**(c) Splintering must be a real ending with a real successor.** This is the mechanic the genre always skips: the
node dies and *the people do not*. Implement it as a re-parenting of membership edges into a new or existing
armed node with a different `goal_breadth` (usually narrower and more violent — the fragment is the part that
would not settle). The state's counter does not register a win; the Corporate Feed does. That gap between the
Feed's story and the engine's state is the whole point.

**(d) The state's instrument is chosen by the basin, not by the player's mood.** Because politics and policing
account for 83% of closures and military for 7%, a non-player state that reaches for the army by default is not
"aggressive," it is *incompetent*, and the engine should price it as such: military force against a sub-1,000
group should raise the group's dispersal and lower the state's local information, i.e. it should *move the group
away from the policing basin and toward persistence*. That is the J/L finding expressed as an incentive.

# Part II — Control, and which violence each level of control permits

## 2.1 The five zones, and the sentence that makes them a mechanic

Kalyvas's central claim is not "violence happens in contested places." It is nearly the opposite:

> "The prediction is that violence is most likely to occur where one actor is near hegemonic, not where this
> actor is in full control or is being contested. Violence, in other words, is most likely **where the
> organizational demand for information meets its individual supply**." (K p. 13)

The five zones (K p. 196): **1** total incumbent control; **2** dominant incumbent; **3** parity; **4** dominant
insurgent; **5** total insurgent control. Zones 1 and 5 are homogeneous, 2–4 are heterogeneous (p. 197).

Two prior definitions matter for the county model. Irregular war is "the twin processes of **segmentation** and
**fragmentation** of sovereignty: territory is divided into zones monopolistically controlled by rival actors
(segmentation) and zones where these actors' sovereignty overlaps (fragmentation)" (K p. 12). A county map
therefore needs *two* presence values, not one ownership tag.

## 2.2 What actually produces control

Kalyvas is unusually concrete, and his list is a terrain-and-administration model, not a sympathy model:

- "Control has a clear territorial foundation: rule presupposes a constant and credible armed presence" (p. 132).
- "Towns, plains, key communication lines, and accessible terrain in general tend to be associated with
  incumbent control, whereas mountains and rugged terrain are generally insurgent strongholds; the location of
  insurgents is best predicted by variables such as terrain and **distance from provincial military bases**"
  (p. 132).
- "Incumbents tend to control cities, even when these cities happen to be the social, religious, or ethnic
  strongholds of their opponents, whereas the insurgents' strongholds tend to be in inaccessible rural areas,
  even when rural populations are inimical to them" (p. 133). Hence "the city was the grave of the guerrilla"
  (Castro, quoted p. 133).
- **Administrative boundaries are terrain.** RENAMO's base locations were a function "not just of remoteness …
  but also of proximity to administrative district boundaries," because local authorities "tended to reject
  counterinsurgency jurisdiction when they could" (p. 133).
- Rural dispersion impedes policing directly: "it is easier to enforce a curfew in a town than in a large rural
  area because taxing and monitoring hundreds or even thousands of hamlets exposes small army detachments to
  ambush" (p. 136).

## 2.3 The violence table

Kalyvas's hypotheses 2–5 (p. 204), which are the operational form of the model:

| Zone | Selective violence | Indiscriminate violence | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1** (state total) | none | rival's only, if any | no defection to punish; `k(c)=0` so "most denunciations are likely false" (p. 203) |
| **2** (state dominant) | **state's** | insurgent's | defection and denunciation both occur; state can shield denouncers (H4) |
| **3** (parity) | **none, either side** | low — mass-defection risk | mutual non-denunciation equilibrium: "a logic akin to the 'mutually assured destruction'" (p. 201) |
| **4** (insurgent dominant) | **insurgent's** | state's | mirror of zone 2 |
| **5** (insurgent total) | none | state's only, if any | mirror of zone 1 |

"It is, thus, possible to think of `r_A` and `r_B` as the extent to which an organization can shield an
individual from retaliation by the other side" (p. 201) — and shielding is the entire currency of denunciation.
The counter-intuitive result is stated flatly: "the most contested areas are predicted to be oases of peace in
the midst of violence" (p. 204); "in the ideal type of irregular war, the functional equivalent of the front line
turns out to be peaceful for civilians" (p. 204).

Chapter 8 checks this against the occupied American South, where the Union created "not one occupied South but
three": garrisoned towns (zone 1) were "islands of order in a sea of violence"; the no-man's-land combined zones
2 and 3, and Unionist violence "often exploded 'after the Yankees arrived' — that is, after a zone moved to 2"
(pp. 243–244).

## 2.4 The 2×2 that decides whether atrocity pays

This is the most directly gameable object in the five books. Kalyvas asks why anyone uses indiscriminate violence
when it is generally counterproductive, and answers with a four-cell matrix (Fig. 6.1, p. 167):

| | Insurgents **protect** civilians | Insurgents **fail to protect** |
|---|---|---|
| **Incumbents selective** | indeterminate (Outcome 1) | civilians support incumbents (Outcome 2) |
| **Incumbents indiscriminate** | **civilians support insurgents** (Outcome 3) | civilians support incumbents (Outcome 4) |

> "Incumbents can afford to be indifferent about the type of violence they use when insurgents are unable to offer
> any protection to civilians. Put otherwise, costly discrimination can be dispensed with when insurgents are
> weak." (p. 167)

The confirming case is exact: after German troops shot "all 207 male inhabitants" of Ano and Kato Kerdilia in
October 1941, "Greek men from surrounding villages were allowed to form militias and set up watch posts around
their villages to prevent the partisans from entering," and "the deterrent effect in northern Greece was swift"
(pp. 168). The counter-case is the same tactic against a rival that *could* protect: Yugoslavia 1941 and Greece
1943–4, where "an important insurgent infrastructure faced German indiscriminate terror with a strategy that
mixed its own selective violence with civilian protection" (p. 168), and it failed.

**Note the direction of the causal arrow.** Indiscriminate violence is not chosen from a doctrine menu; it is what
an actor does when it lacks the information for a list: "the absence of information leads incumbents (initially,
at least) to overestimate the strength of ties between civilians and insurgents" (p. 159).

## 2.5 Design reading — OURS

**(a) Two presence values per county, and the zone derived from them.** `presence_state(c)` and
`presence_movement(c)` on [0,1]; the zone is a discrete function of the pair (dominance gap and floor), not a
sigmoid. This is a plain reading of K p. 196 plus the segmentation/fragmentation distinction at p. 12, and it is
the only per-county object the violence rule needs.

**(b) The state's violence *type* is derived from its information, not chosen.** Give each county an
`information` stock whose inflow is `denunciation_supply` — which is zero in zone 5, low and *mostly false* in
zone 1, high in zones 2 and 4, and **zero in zone 3** because counter-denunciation is credible (K pp. 201–204).
When `information` is below the threshold at which a named list is credible, the state's raid resolves as
category-based. **The player never selects "indiscriminate."** The player selects a raid, and the raid's type
falls out of what the state knows — which is exactly `research/08` §2.2's conclusion, now with the mechanism
attached.

**(c) The corollary is the best political mechanic on the shelf, and it is a *displacement*, not a shield.**
Because zone 1 purges produce "mostly false" denunciations (p. 203) and zone 3 produces none, making your own
county illegible does not protect it — it **moves the state's efficient violence to the neighbouring county where
the state is near-hegemonic**. The player's defensive play is therefore not "hide" but "widen zone 3 and move
it," and the cost is paid by whichever village is currently in zone 2.

**(d) Zone 3 must be rendered as a *safe* place, because it is one, and that is the counter-intuitive lesson.**
A game that makes the front line the deadliest place has implemented conventional war and called it
counterinsurgency. Parity is where people defect freely to both sides and nobody is killed for it (p. 204).
The player's incentive should be to *create* parity in counties they cannot hold — which is also, usefully, the
`research/16` §3.6 QRF-cost play seen from the civilian side.

**(e) Do not model control as a percentage that trends.** Kalyvas's own caveat is that the theory "exogenizes
military decisions concerning the allocation of resources across space and time" and "assumes a stable strategic
environment" (p. 207). In the engine the exogenous part is the player's and the state's *allocation decisions*;
the endogenous part is denunciation. Keep that boundary or the model stops being his.

# Part III — Funding decides doctrine before any leader does

## 3.1 The coupling, in one sentence

> "My central finding is that rebel groups that emerge in environments rich in natural resources or with the
> external support of an outside patron tend to commit high levels of indiscriminate violence; movements that
> arise in resource-poor contexts perpetrate far fewer abuses and employ violence selectively and strategically."
> (W p. 7)

The mechanism is not greed and it is not culture. It is **whether revenue can be raised without civilian
consent**, which decides what a leader can *offer*, which decides *who joins*, which decides whether orders can be
enforced:

- **Economic endowments** (lootables, contraband, patrons, extortion) let leaders **pay** → they attract
  **consumers** → "opportunistic rebellions must permit indiscipline in order to maintain their membership,
  while holding on to the reins of military strategy" (p. 10) → coercive extraction → indiscriminate violence.
- **Social endowments** (shared belief, dense prior networks) let leaders **promise** → they attract
  **investors** → "activist movements can maintain internal discipline by drawing on established norms and
  networks" (p. 10) → cooperative extraction → selective violence.

Weinstein's Figure 0.1 (p. 12) is a five-row table — recruitment, control, governance, violence, resilience —
with one column per strategy. **That table is the doctrine tree, and the player does not get to pick a column.**

## 3.2 The numbers

Responsibility for violence against noncombatants, by conflict (W Table 6.1, p. 211):

| | Uganda 1981–85 | Mozambique 1976–94 | Sendero Nacional 1980–88 | Sendero–Huallaga 1980–2000 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Government | 45% | 7% | 4% | 4% |
| **Rebel groups** | **17%** | **82%** | **36%** | **32%** |
| Unclear | 38% | 8% | 60% | 64% |
| Total incidents | 711 | 1,379 | 4,159 | 804 |

Form of violence, rebel incidents only (W Table 6.2, p. 213):

| | NRA (110) | Renamo (1,133) | Sendero Nacional (1,504) | Sendero–Huallaga (258) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Killing | 74% | 61% | 23% | 40% |
| Looting | 30% | 35% | 11% | 27% |
| **Destruction** | **7%** | **42%** | **66%** | 33% |

Scale, in the large-N extension (W Table 8.1, p. 307): contraband financing is associated with +0.96 log
combat-related deaths (t = 2.29) in wars over state control; pro-rebel intervention with +0.82 (t = 1.75). In
plain terms: "the presence of contraband resources is equivalent to nearly ten years of additional fighting"
(p. 308), in civilian deaths.

## 3.3 Reputation is a latch, not an accumulator

> "Even one incident in which a combatant kills a civilian without cause damages the reputation of the rebel
> group. When civilians are unsure of what to expect from insurgents or, alternatively, are convinced that
> rebels are likely to abuse them, they often choose to resist or flee when rebels arrive. This sets into motion
> a cycle of resistance followed by increasing coercion that leads violence to spiral out of control." (W p. 206)

And the asymmetry is structural, not moral: "Because they are held together by short-term material incentives,
combatants' access to material rewards must continue if an organizational collapse is to be prevented … and
since reputations form early on in the conflict, these groups are unable to retreat from high-violence
strategies precisely because they employ them" (p. 206).

The reverse drift also exists and is *not* symmetric: "activist movements tend toward opportunism, a change in
strategy which is difficult to reverse" (p. 11), triggered by "disruptive shocks that alter the membership of the
group or undermine its internal structures" (p. 11). The worked example is Sendero–Huallaga, where a movement
"built initially around social endowments was transformed in its new environment as its membership changed" once
it organized around coca (p. 255).

## 3.4 Design reading — OURS

**(a) One scalar, `consent_free_revenue` = the share of the actor's inflow that does not require a civilian to
agree.** Contraband, patron money, protection rackets and captured state stores score 1; taxation-with-a-bargain
and voluntary contribution score 0. Cross the threshold and the recruitment offer *becomes a wage*, which is a
change in **edge type**, not a stat: supporter edges convert from obligation to payment, and payment edges expire
when unpaid. That single conversion produces indiscipline, coercion and atrocity without any of them being
authored separately. `research/08` §2.1 already rules out a discipline meter; this is the quantity that replaces
it, and it is observable in the world (a payroll, a convoy, a patron's envoy).

**(b) Reputation is a boolean with a timestamp.** `first_unpunished_atrocity(campaign)` — set once. Before it,
civilian response to an armed node is computed from obligation edges; after it, from fear, and fear decays toward
flight rather than toward collaboration. The only way to clear it is a **public, costly punishment of the
perpetrator** — Weinstein's own description of activist practice: "Beatings, looting, destruction, and other
behaviors that potentially diminish civilian support are strictly controlled" (p. 205), and his Appendix C
reproduces the NRA Code of Conduct (p. 371). Make that an event the player can run *before* the latch sets, and a
near-impossible one after.

**(c) Atrocity is not a policy lever; it is a *payoff* with one good cell.** Combine W with K §2.4 above: the
coercion cycle is only self-sustaining where the rival cannot protect. This is the cleanest cross-book synthesis
in the briefing and it should be a single rule: *coercive extraction pays while the rival is absent, and creates
the rival's base wherever the rival is present.*

**(d) Do not implement the ±10-years-of-fighting number as a combat modifier.** It is a cross-national
association with an acknowledged rival explanation ("a greater capacity to inflict harm … through the purchase
of more effective armaments," p. 308). Use it to calibrate the *magnitude* of the funding coupling at campaign
scale, not to modify a battle.

# Part IV — Why people join, and why repression is a two-sided instrument

## 4.1 The three reasons, and the one the genre always omits

Wood interviewed ~200 campesinos in contested Usulután across 1987–96. Two-thirds of residents did **not**
support the insurgency (p. 18) — so the default is non-participation and the base is a minority by construction.
Her answer to why the other third did:

> "there were three reasons that participants supported the mobilization and insurgency, which I will term
> **participation, defiance, and pleasure in agency**. In addition, two contingent, path-dependent aspects of the
> civil war — local past patterns of violence and proximity to insurgent forces — also shaped participation."
> (p. 231)

- **Participation** — the value of taking part in realising a moral commitment, independent of outcome. "To
  struggle for the realization of the reign of God was to live a life valuable to oneself and in the eyes of God"
  (p. 232).
- **Defiance** — "supporting the insurgency despite the violence of government, a refusal to acquiesce … Its
  value was not contingent on success or even on one's contributing to the likelihood of success" (p. 233). The
  defiant "were not, however, suicidal" (p. 233).
- **Pleasure in agency** — "the pleasure in together changing unjust social structures through intentional
  action" (p. 235). Crucially, "pleasure in agency (but not participation or defiance) is a frequency-dependent
  motivation: it depends on the likelihood of success, which in turn increases with the numbers participating"
  (pp. 235–236).

The two path-dependent factors are worth their own line because they are *county* facts: support tracked "the
history of violence against family members and neighbors" (p. 230) — support was likelier where government forces
had done the killing, much less likely where the FMLN had — and "proximity to insurgent forces mattered as well.
Where there was massive government presence, supportive activities were highly unlikely (whatever residents'
private preferences)" (p. 212).

## 4.2 The threshold, in her own algebra

Wood's appendix models participation as a coordination game and derives a critical mass. With δ = value of
defiance, α = pleasure of agency, A = cost of failure:

> "β* = (A − δ)/(δ + α)" (p. 271)

> "the larger the value given to defiance, the less high is the insurgency threshold"; "the larger the value
> placed on the pleasure of agency, the less difficult to surmount is the insurgency threshold"; "the greater the
> cost of failed participation, the more demanding are the requirements for self-sustaining collective action."
> (p. 271)

And the mechanism that gets a village over the line: "strongly defiant" individuals — those who participate even
at β = 0, "perhaps as a result of the loss of a loved one to the armed forces or a death squad" (p. 270). Because
"deaths as a result of state violence tended to be clustered both spatially and temporally," and because "many of
the localized groups we are considering are quite small — a few dozen family units," the probability that a
critical mass appears is **much higher in small groups**: "a random draw is more likely to introduce a large
deviation … for small populations than for large" (p. 270).

## 4.3 Design reading — OURS

**(a) Repression is a two-sided instrument, and this is the single best mechanic Wood gives us.** Raising A (the
cost of failure) *raises* β* for the uncommitted — fewer people join. But the same repression *manufactures*
δ > A individuals by killing kin, and those are the ones who tip a village. So a repression event should be
resolved as two separate writes: it subtracts from the ordinary participation pool and adds to a small
`defiant` pool. There is no monotonic curve here and therefore no sigmoid needed — two populations, opposite
signs, and the county's size decides which dominates.

**(b) The tipping rule is a critical mass on a small denominator.** Participation flips in a county when
`defiant_share ≥ f*(A, δ, α)`, and `f*` is *lower* in small populations for purely combinatorial reasons
(p. 270). That gives the map a real texture: rural hamlets of a few dozen families tip; the city does not — which
Kalyvas reaches independently from the control side (K p. 136). Two books, one conclusion, so implement it once.

**(c) `pleasure_in_agency` is the only frequency-dependent term, and it is the one the player can actually feed.**
Because it depends on *perceived likelihood of success* (p. 235), it is raised by visible, shared, successful
action — an occupied property, a defended village, a published verdict — and it is destroyed by a defeat that
everyone sees. This is the honest home for `research/16` §I's "the front exists for the armed struggle": the
front's job in mechanical terms is to keep supplying visible wins to a term that no material incentive can buy.

**(d) Two-thirds non-participation is a design constraint, not a difficulty setting.** If the player's supporter
share in a contested county is above ~50% early, the county model is wrong. The base is a minority that the
state has not yet made safe to ignore.

**(e) Do not model Wood's three reasons as three bars.** She is explicit that these are *reasons for acting* —
"values, norms, commitments, emotions, material interests, and aversions" — not a preference ordering (p. 231
n. 4), and that people "often do not calculate risks or trade-offs over time as assumed by conventional economic
theory." The engine needs one threshold and three inputs to it; anything more is a UI for something she argues
does not exist.

# Part V — Legibility, and the phase before the rifle

## 5.1 The state's project has four necessary parts

Scott's account of why large-scale state schemes fail is a conjunction, and each term is a separate condition:

> "the legibility of a society provides the capacity for large-scale social engineering, high-modernist ideology
> provides the desire, the authoritarian state provides the determination to act on that desire, and an
> incapacitated civil society provides the leveled social terrain on which to build." (SLS p. 5; the four
> elements are introduced pp. 4–5)

"All four are necessary for a full-fledged disaster" (p. 4). When a plan is thwarted, the characteristic retreat
is **miniaturization** — "the creation of a more easily controlled micro-order in model cities, model villages,
and model farms" (p. 4). And the failure mechanism is stated in a way that is almost a work-to-rule strike:

> "Designed or planned social order is necessarily schematic; it always ignores essential features of any real,
> functioning social order. This truth is best illustrated in a work-to-rule strike, which turns on the fact that
> any production process depends on a host of informal practices and improvisations that could never be codified.
> By merely following the rules meticulously, the workforce can virtually halt production." (p. 6)

## 5.2 The operational test of illegibility

This is the most useful single sentence in the three Scott books for a map system:

> "A simple way of determining whether this margin exists is to ask if an outsider would have needed a local
> guide (a native tracker) in order to find her way successfully. If the answer is yes, then the community or
> terrain in question enjoys at least a small measure of insulation from outside intrusion." (SLS p. 54)

And the consequence, which is the bridge to Kalyvas: legibility "merely amplifies the capacity of the state for
**discriminating** interventions" (p. 78). The Amsterdam 1941 map of Jewish residence is his example — the
legibility was Dutch, the purpose was Nazi, and the map is what made the round-up efficient (pp. 78–79). "An
illegible society, then, is a hindrance to any effective intervention by the state, whether the purpose of that
intervention is plunder or public welfare" (p. 78).

## 5.3 Everyday resistance is not a tutorial

WotW's thesis is that the dramatic events are the anomaly and the quiet ones are the substance:

> "the ordinary weapons of relatively powerless groups: foot dragging, dissimulation, desertion, false
> compliance, pilfering, feigned ignorance, slander, arson, sabotage, and so on. These … forms of class struggle
> … require little or no coordination or planning; they make use of implicit understandings and informal
> networks; they often represent a form of individual self-help; they typically avoid any direct, symbolic
> confrontation with authority." (WotW, Preface, p. xvi)

Two claims follow that break the usual progression.

**(i) The repertoire is selected by the state's enforcement, not by the movement's preference.** "The parameters
of resistance are also set, in part, by the institutions of repression. To the extent that such institutions do
their work effectively, they may all but preclude any forms of resistance other than the individual, the
informal, and the clandestine" (WotW ch. 7, p. 302). And therefore: "what we may actually be measuring in this
enterprise is the level of repression that structures the available options" (p. 302).

**(ii) Armed action is a symptom of everyday resistance *failing*.** "When such stratagems are abandoned in favor
of more quixotic action, it is usually a sign of great desperation" (WotW p. xvi). And the ordinary phase is not
inconsequential — it *has* political results: "It is in this fashion, and not through revolts, let alone legal
political pressure, that the peasantry has classically made its political presence felt" (p. 36; the "coral
reef" passage).

## 5.4 The permission frontier moves one notch at a time

DAR supplies the mechanism by which everyday resistance accumulates into something the state must answer:

> "the hidden transcript is continually pressing against the limit of what is permitted on stage, much as a body
> of water might press against a dam … a particularly intrepid, angry, risk-taking, unguarded subordinate
> gesturing or saying something that slightly breaches that limit. If this act of insubordination … is not
> rebuked or punished, others will exploit that breach and a new, de facto limit governing what may be said will
> have been established incorporating the new territory." (DAR p. 196)

Definitions: the **public transcript** is "the open interaction between subordinates and those who dominate"
(p. 2), and "the more menacing the power, the thicker the mask" (p. 3). **Infrapolitics** is "a wide variety of
low-profile forms of resistance that dare not speak in their own name" (p. 19).

The enforcement asymmetry is stated as a law of leakage: "any weakness in surveillance and enforcement is likely
to be quickly exploited; any ground left undefended is likely to be ground lost" (p. 195) — with the tithe as the
worked case, where centuries of evasion ended in abolition when the revolutionary government could not enforce
the redemption payments (p. 195).

## 5.5 Design reading — OURS

**(a) `legibility(c)` is measured by the local-guide test, and it is the state's *input*, not its score.** Concretely:
does the state's agent in county `c` require a resident to find a named address, person or meeting? If yes,
`legibility` is low regardless of how many police are stationed there. That keeps legibility a property of the
*relation* rather than of the county, and it matches the existing territory system's framing of "state legibility
accumulates as heat" (`rust/crates/babylon-tick/content/rules/territory.bsl`) while giving it a checkable
predicate instead of an accumulation.

**(b) The state's four-element conjunction should be a gate with a named shortfall.** Administrative capacity ∧
high-modernist programme ∧ coercive willingness ∧ weak civil society ⇒ the state can complete a legibility
project (a cadastre, a registry, a clearance). Fail any term and the result is **miniaturization**: a model
village, a model prison, a pilot county — which is *not* a scaled-down success but a different object with a
different failure rate. This is the cleanest way to model a state that keeps almost succeeding.

**(c) `permission_frontier(c)` is an integer that ratchets.** One notch per unpunished breach; one notch back per
punished breach, at a legitimacy cost. The player's early game is then literally a sequence of small breaches,
and the state's early game is deciding which ones to answer — which is a far better early game than a build queue.
It also gives the Everyday phase a *win condition that is not a rifle*: the frontier reaching a value at which
an extraction (a rent, a tithe, a registration) stops being collectable at all.

**(d) The armed phase must be reachable by *losing* the everyday phase, and only that way.** This is the direct
contradiction of the genre's tech tree and it is what the corpus actually says (WotW p. xvi). Mechanically: the
armed node unlocks when the frontier *collapses* (a punishment wave that closes ordinary repertoire) **and** a
defiant pool exists (Wood §4.2) **and** an armed-self-defence document exists (`research/16` §4.6). Three
conditions, one of which is a defeat. A player who plays the everyday game well never sees the rifle, and that
must be a legitimate, winnable outcome.

**(e) Legibility is dual-use, and the game must show it.** Scott's Amsterdam map (SLS pp. 78–79) is the anti-
triumphal case: the same apparatus that could feed a population can deport it. If the player builds legibility
for a clinic, the state inherits it. That is a real cost of the `research/16` §3.1 "institution that produces a
good," and it should be visible: the registry the clinic keeps is the registry the state reads.

# Part IX — Where these books are weak, and what the engine must not pretend

Marked because a briefing that hides this is a liability.

1. **J/L is a media-derived terrorism database, and they say so.** Groups in "high-income and media-saturated
   countries may be more likely to show up in the terrorism database (which is built from media reports)" (p. 38).
   Their own verdict on causality is modest: "the causal process is not entirely clear" (p. 43). *Use the base
   rates as base rates; do not use them as a law.*
2. **J/L censors the still-active (244/648).** A cross-section of *ended* groups cannot tell you why the others
   did not end. Part VI's "persistence is the default" is the correct inference, but it is an inference.
3. **J/L's single-label coding** forces a group into one basin. Real endings are combinations — their own summary
   says so ("terrorist groups typically end due to a combination of factors," p. 10). The engine should allow a
   *sequence* of partial closures rather than one terminal label.
4. **Kalyvas's model is explicitly static, two-actor, and exogenizes military allocation** (pp. 207–208). He also
   notes people "tend to underestimate the duration and fluidity of civil wars and hence overestimate their own
   security, especially in the war's initial stages" (p. 207) — i.e. the model's rationality assumption is worst
   exactly where the game starts.
5. **Kalyvas's own empirical chapter has a "Mispredictions" section (§9.11).** The zone model is a baseline whose
   *failures* he treats as informative. A deterministic zone→violence rule would therefore be less faithful than
   his own book.
6. **Weinstein is four groups in three conflicts**, chosen to vary the independent variable. The large-N
   extension uses proxies (contraband presence, pro-rebel intervention) and he concedes a rival explanation
   (p. 308). *The direction is well supported; the coefficient is not a tuning target.*
7. **Wood is one province, interview-based, and interpretive.** She says plainly that "the empirical evidence is
   inevitably open to a variety of interpretations" (p. 229). Her three reasons are her reading of testimony, not
   a measured decomposition. The threshold model's parameters are illustrative.
8. **The three Scott books are essays.** SLS is comparative cases, not data. WotW is one village (Sedaka) in
   Kedah in 1978–80. DAR is a theory of transcripts illustrated across centuries. Their value here is
   *definitional precision* (legibility, hidden transcript, infrapolitics) and *mechanism*, not magnitude.
9. **WotW pagination is unreliable in this edition.** The epub's `[Page n]` markers are sparse and sometimes
   mid-sentence; the "ordinary weapons" list appears in both the Preface and ch. 2 with different wording. Cited
   here from the Preface (p. xvi) where the marker was unambiguous; the ch. 7 material is cited as p. 302 with
   that caveat.
10. **Nothing here measures Babylon's Michigan.** All thresholds in the Calibrated rows are hypotheses about a
    fictional 1980s–2000s Michigan county map. They must be tuned against the campaign's own data or they are
    decoration.

# Part X — Deliberately parked, and the handoffs

**Parked.** Tactical resolution (hex/battalion/supply/attrition) — still the separate study `research/16` §VII
names. Nothing here needs a unit.

**Handoffs, in the order they would be built:**

1. **County control layer** (Part II): two presence values per county, zone derived, information stock. This is
   the load-bearing piece and everything else reads from it.
2. **Raid resolution** (Part II §2.4, Part III §3.4c): type derived from information, payoff from the rival's
   protection capability. This is where the state stops being an author of events and becomes a computer.
3. **Closure checkpoints** (Part I): the five quantities and the eligibility test. Cheap, discrete, and it
   replaces the genre's war-score bar.
4. **Composition and reputation latches** (Part III): `consent_free_revenue`, `first_unpunished_atrocity`. Both
   are single writes, and both are irreversible enough to be felt.
5. **Permission frontier and legibility** (Part V): the pre-armed game. Build last, because it only makes sense
   once the county layer exists — and because it is the one that changes what the game *is*.

**The one line to carry into the design doc:** the player does not win by surviving the raid and does not lose by
being raided. The player wins by making the state's own administration fail in a county, and loses by becoming
administrable — by a bargain, a payroll, or a registry. Every book in this set agrees on that, from five
different directions.
