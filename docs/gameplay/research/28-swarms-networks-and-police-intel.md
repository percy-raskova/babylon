## 0. Thesis

1. **Swarming is a doctrine with four simultaneous preconditions, not a shape on a graph** — and
   the corpus is explicit that it fails if any one of them is missing, which makes it the first
   Babylon capability that is multiplicative rather than additive.
2. **A swarm has a duty cycle.** It exists only during the pulse: coalesce, strike, dissever. The
   off-pulse is not a weaker swarm, it is *no* swarm — inert, invisible, and losing morale — so
   the player's real decision is when to spend the whole capability at once.
3. **What replaces the order is shared doctrine**, and the price is that a swarm cannot be
   compelled: in Seattle the line held under tear gas precisely because nobody could have ordered
   it held. The same property that makes the swarm unstoppable makes it uncommandable, including
   uncommandable *to stop*.
4. **The state's intelligence process is six stages, and every stage is a bottleneck with a
   different resource** — nominations, collection channels, an alias merge, a human analyst's
   attention, a jurisdictional cut, and a public claim it must be able to evidence. The player
   does not beat the loop by hiding; the player beats it by choosing which stage to starve.
5. **Resilience and command are the same purchase, bought and sold.** Every step toward
   acephalousness buys survivability with the ability to order, and the state's whole apparatus
   is the machine that prices that trade — which is why the late game is not "can we survive the
   raid" but "what can we still make people do."

## 1. Source-by-source mechanics

### 1.1 *Swarming and the Future of Conflict* (2000)

- **Four historical forms, ranked by information requirement:** melee, massing, maneuver, swarming
  — "melees requiring the least, maneuvers needing more than massing, and swarming depending
  completely on robust, rapid communications" (Summary, p. vii). Swarming is therefore the *only*
  form whose precondition is a comms system, which makes comms the single point of failure by
  construction.
- **The tenet, verbatim:** "centralized strategy, decentralized tactics, distributed formations and
  logistics" (p. 45).
- **Pulsing:** "coalesce rapidly and stealthily on a target, then dissever and redisperse,
  immediately ready to recombine for a new pulse" (*Zapatista*, p. 15; same language in *Swarming*,
  p. 46).
- **Pods and clusters:** platoon-sized pod, "roughly 40–45 soldiers, with ten light strike
  vehicles"; "three pods would then form a cluster"; a cluster should beat a battalion of 750–900
  at "as little as one-sixth the manpower"; ten clusters ought to beat a division (pp. 62, and
  n. 40–41). **The arithmetic is the authors' guess and they say so** — "estimation has to be very
  rough at this point."
- **Connectivity has an optimum, not a maximum.** Too little restricts adaptability; "too much
  creates perturbations"; all-channel pods lacking topsight "risk devoting their efforts to targets
  of lesser importance"; full connectivity "might actually foster indecision" (p. 64). The exemplar
  is d'Erlon at Waterloo, who "spent the entire afternoon and evening marching and countermarching
  between the two fields of battle without firing a shot at either" (p. 65, quoting Chandler).
- **Comms cut ⇒ defeat in detail.** Disruption "will not only render pods and clusters less
  effective, but can also make them vulnerable to being 'picked off' in detail—one by one" (p. 67);
  the doctrinal hedge is to train to fight blind and "fall back on the Channel" — i.e. a
  prearranged fallback position (p. 74).
- **The historical proof is a comms story:** the U-boats were beaten by "improved means of detection
  of their movements—including aerial surveillance, high-frequency direction-finding equipment
  and, finally, wireless communications intercepts" (p. 33). The swarm lost to the mapping of its
  own coordination.
- **Designation beats mass:** in the 1994 BCTP wargames, when a special-forces commander asked that
  his small dispersed units be given *strike designation* tasks as well as reconnaissance, "the
  special forces became the enablers of highly effective swarming of fire… XVIII Corps casualties
  plummeted, on average, by over 80 percent" (p. 82). Small units did not get stronger; they got
  *pointed*.
- **Who actually swarms:** "Civil-society NGO activism resembles theory well"; "business, military
  practices differ most from theory — limited autonomy of agents/units, variations in connectivity,
  rules not so simple" (p. 50). Hizbollah's counter-raid doctrine: units "converge—like antibodies—
  on any intruders… No central leadership is required" (p. 52).
- **Limits the authors concede:** swarming struggles against guerrillas with popular support (n. 23,
  p. 46); Al Qaeda's "strategic swarming" is limited "to the extent to which the Base's operations
  depend upon bin Laden's direct leadership" (p. 53).

### 1.2 *Networks and Netwars* (2001)

- **Five levels a netwar actor must get right:** technological, social, narrative, organizational,
  doctrinal — "A netwar actor must get all five right to be fully effective," and the *narrative*
  level matters most for civil-society actors because they lack clan ties (Summary, pp. ix–xi).
- **Gerlach's SPIN**, the best available description of what a movement *becomes*: "segmentary"
  (groups "grow and die, divide and fuse, proliferate and contract"), "polycentric" (multiple,
  temporary, sometimes competing centres), "networked" via "travelers, overlapping membership, joint
  activities, common reading matter, and shared ideals and opponents" (pp. 289–290). Overlapping
  membership is structural, not incidental: "A person may be a leader in one segment and a follower
  in another" (p. 290).
- **Leaderless resistance is a partial doctrine, not a strategy.** Beam's "phantom cells" tell
  members they "must know exactly what they are doing, and exactly how to do it," and that "No one
  need issue an order to anyone" (p. 334) — which is a *doctrine* requirement disguised as an
  organizational one. The book's judgment: "By itself, a tenet like leaderless resistance is only a
  partial step toward having a doctrine for netwar" (p. 335).
- **The best-documented swarm doctrine in the book is DAN's** (Seattle, N30): affinity groups of
  "5–20 people," self-selected; each group "decides for itself what actions its members will
  undertake"; where groups are proximate they form "clusters," plus roaming "flying groups";
  coordination at spokescouncils by consensus (pp. 335–336).
- **Why it worked, in the participants' own words:** "If a call went out for more people at a
  certain location, an affinity group could assess the numbers holding the line where they were and
  choose whether or not to move… No centralized leader could have coordinated the scene in the
  midst of the chaos, and none was needed… **No authoritarian figure could have compelled people to
  hold a blockade line while being tear gassed—but empowered people free to make their own decisions
  did choose to do that**" (p. 336, Starhawk).
- **Critical Mass** supplies the deniability form: "organized coincidence," a "xerocracy," and the
  motto "No one need take responsibility but everyone can take credit" (p. 337).
- **The network form's own weakness, named:** networks "have more difficulty instilling, and
  enforcing, a sense of personal identity with and loyalty to the network. This is one of the key
  weaknesses of the network form" (pp. 342–343).

### 1.3 *The Zapatista "Social Netwar"* (1998)

- **The mutation, dated:** it "began as a rather traditional, Maoist insurgency" and "within a
  matter of a few days" became a social netwar, because the war of the flea "ran into trouble" and
  the NGOs "mobilized and descended on Chiapas and Mexico City in 'swarm networks'" (p. 45).
- **The specific military error:** the EZLN "organized itself into just a few units of almost
  battalion size (500–700 fighters), which was the optimal battle formation according to Mao"
  (p. 47) — and decentralized "within weeks" (p. 49). **Unit size was the tell.**
- **Packetization:** "drug smugglers can break large loads into many small packets for simultaneous
  surreptitious transport… or NGO activists… have enough diversity in their ranks to go after any
  discrete issue area that arises" (*Zapatista*, p. 15).
- **Authority is borrowed, not held.** "Marcos would learn this and later point out that he could
  not give an order—his order would simply not exist—if it had not been authorized by an assembly
  or a committee representing the indigenas" (p. 33). The assembly's decision is binding in a
  specific way: after "hours and hours, entire nights, for months and months," those against "had
  no option; either they followed along with the rest, or they left the community" (p. 33). The
  CCRIs were an improvised ratification body created to consult assemblies before the 1994
  offensive; "its authority was soon subordinated to the approval of the CCRIs" (pp. 34–35).
- **Comms are improvised and cheap:** "The fax numbers of Mexican and U.S. officials were often
  posted in Internet newsgroups and mailing lists; if a number became inoperable, a new one was
  sometimes discovered and posted" (p. 51). "Action forms" — pre-written letters with the target's
  fax and phone numbers attached — let a reader act in one step during "urgent appeals" (pp. 70–71).
- **The off-pulse problem is stated plainly:** "During periods of inactivity… it is not easy for a
  network to keep up its morale and energy levels" (p. 71).
- **The channel is not trusted:** activists report "junk, false, or kooky information, based on
  rumor," worry about "crying wolf," and fear "that government actors may post misinformation and
  disinformation on the Internet in order to **provoke an overreaction that embarrasses the
  activists**" (p. 71).
- **What actually stopped the army:** not the graph. "Mexican officials admit that they were
  overwhelmed by the 'information war' in the early days of the conflict" (p. 63); two presidents
  halted operations (Jan 1994, Feb 1995) and the government conceded a de facto EZLN-run "free
  zone" (pp. 63–64).
- **The state's counter-form is the swarm:** the army abandoned massed counter-guerrilla practice,
  "creating much smaller operational units, of roughly platoon size (36–45 troops…), and deploying
  them in a dispersed fashion across Chiapas, blanketing the state" (p. 74) — viable only because it
  "devolved greater authority to lower-ranking officers and enjoyed much-enhanced communications"
  (p. 74, n.). Earlier attempts at the same thing had failed for want of officers and radios.
- **Two fronts, one budget:** the "battlespace consisted of both the operational field in Chiapas
  and the arena of the 'infosphere'… hard to operate actively on both at once," and the government
  chose to constrain field operations (p. 76), which strained civil-military relations and led the
  army to resent being "reined in."
- **The interagency loop, dated Jan 1994:** a CISEN-chaired group including defense, social
  development and the Chiapas governorate, which assessed the EZLN as militarily weak and ran a
  three-prong strategy — "a military prong to keep the EZLN confined… while avoiding combat and
  improving the army's human-rights behavior; a political prong to keep the dialogue… from becoming
  national in scope, and to regain control of information; and an economic prong" (pp. 80–81). It
  resumed the offensive in early 1995 **when EZLN information operations moved the currency and the
  stock market** (p. 81).
- **Legibility failure inside the state:** federal oversight "did not fare well" — interservice
  coordination hit "differing bureaucratic cultures," and federal-local coordination hit "a network
  of landlords, ranchers, caciques, and private paramilitary forces whose modus operandi is
  essentially feudal" (p. 81).
- **The precondition list for social netwar** (p. 120–123): an open or opening society; a society in
  flux under strain; local NGOs for transnational ones to link to; growing activist infrastructure;
  "diverse communication systems… for rapid all-channel consultation"; transnational reach into
  capitals; **a target government that "should care about its international image"** — "A pariah
  state, like Iraq, that does not care much about its image… will be less vulnerable to social
  netwar, and less hesitant to crush activists who try to create one" (p. 121); audiences outside
  the zone; a "CNN effect"; and issues amenable to activism (information regimes more than
  state-centric ones).

### 1.4 Sparrow, "Application of network analysis to criminal intelligence" (1991)

- **The five-stage intelligence process, from Scotland Yard's own manual:** "collection, evaluation,
  collation, analysis, dissemination," and "Without the ability to perform effective and useful
  analysis, the intelligence process is reduced to a simple storage and retrieval system for
  effectively unrelated data" (p. 251, frontispiece).
- **The standing complaint:** agencies "typically have plenty of data, much of it computerized, but
  comparatively little capability for extracting useful intelligence from it" (p. 252).
- **The Anacapa chart's three hidden objectives:** spatial proximity ≈ closeness (i.e. a 2-D MDS,
  which for complex networks "there is no reason to believe… will exist"); no crossing lines (i.e.
  the network must be **planar**, which it need not be); and "central on the picture" = max degree,
  which means "the determination of centrality will depend upon **who you know most about**, rather
  than who is central or pivotal in any structural sense" (pp. 255–256).
- **The computer does not analyse.** "The intelligent functions still take place in the minds of the
  analysts… So the predominant locus for the law-enforcement community's intelligent interpretation
  of networks remains somewhere between the 'picture on the wall'… and the analyst's brain"
  (pp. 254–255). Analysts "keep redrawing the graph as new information… becomes available" (p. 255).
- **Four properties that break the toolkit:** size; **incompleteness that "will be anything but
  random — it will be systematic… in accordance with the biases introduced by investigative
  methods"**; fuzzy boundaries ("no obvious criterion by which players can be excluded or included");
  and dynamism — a tie "has a distribution over time, waxing and waning from one period to another"
  (pp. 261–263). Consequence: "precise global network measures (such as radius, diameter, even
  density)" are "almost meaningless" (p. 263).
- **Which measures survive:** betweenness, point strength, business. Degree does not; closeness and
  Euclidean centrality "become quite arbitrary" under fuzzy boundaries (pp. 265–266).
- **Set strength and the jurisdictional cut:** extend point strength to sets (Menger); "the
  practical task facing many law enforcement agencies… is to identify not just a manageable cutset,
  but **manageable cutsets within that agency's jurisdiction**" (p. 266).
- **Two asymmetric overlays:** "drugs essentially flow one way and money flows the other, but the
  two commodities do not necessarily pass through symmetric channels. **Strangling either one of
  those two flows is enough** to put a supply operation out of business" (p. 266).
- **Three equivalences, three uses:** substitutability (identical neighbours), stochastic
  equivalence (same *probabilities* of ties — the street-dealer example), role equivalence
  (automorphism; "individuals in entirely separate organizations… can still be role equivalent")
  (pp. 266–269).
- **Alias detection, as an algorithm:** two aliases "would have no link joining them directly, but
  would have a significant number of paths of length two connecting them… Existence of many paths
  of length two without a direct connection is, otherwise, a most unlikely phenomenon" (p. 270).
- **Multi-role people break template matching:** blockmodelling requires matching *disconnections* as
  well as connections; "as soon as the possibility of multiple roles is acknowledged, then the
  requirement to match the zeroes as well has to be dropped," and the right question becomes "which
  set of roles best explains this individual's aggregate network connections?" (p. 271).
- **Role uniqueness is a profession-level vulnerability:** "targeting courier-recruiters could help
  reduce cross-border currency or drug traffic, just as targeting drivers could stall armed robbery
  gangs… Any kind of role uniqueness represents a strategic vulnerability within a criminal
  profession" (p. 270).
- **Weak ties, with the IRA as the case:** cells are dense cliques, "particularly difficult to
  infiltrate"; command traffic runs on channels that "look exactly like Granovetter's weak ties";
  "The most valuable communications channels to monitor, therefore, are those which are seldom used
  and which lie outside the relatively dense clique structures" (p. 271). Note the terminological
  trap: analysts already used "strong/weak" to mean *reliability of the source*, not structure
  (p. 272).
- **Strategic vs lead-following:** Lupsha's finding that individual-oriented prosecution "merely
  help[s] to open the promotion ladder within organized crime groups" (p. 260).

### 1.5 Everett & Borgatti, "Calculating role similarities" (1988)

- **Role equivalence = orbit:** two vertices are automorphically equivalent if some permutation maps
  one to the other; "the sets {1,2,3,4} and {5,6} are known as the orbits of the graph" (pp. 78–79).
- **Why it is hard:** there is "no known complete set of graphical invariants," so no property list
  computes orbits; "the only algorithm which can find orbits must be based on a direct search
  technique and since this search must be based on permutations it will soon become computationally
  infeasible even for small graphs" (p. 79).
- **The algorithm is a refinement by neighbourhood degree vectors**, `d(N^i(x))`, and — when degree
  alone fails, as on a 26-node regular graph of diameter 2 where every node has an identical degree
  vector at every level — by adding a **betweenness** vector to break the tie (pp. 81–85).
- **The similarity measure is distance-weighted:** `A(x,y) = Σ SIM(i,x,y)/i ÷ Σ 1/i`, so "if a pair
  of actors differs in their immediate neighborhoods (i = 1), they will be assigned an equivalence
  score close to zero," while differing only at the outermost neighbourhood scores near one
  (pp. 85–86).
- **Digraphs are checked six ways** — in/out degree vectors *and* in/out centrality vectors at every
  level (p. 89) — and multi-relation networks require the conditions to hold *per relation*.
- **The honest limit:** "the orbits can only be finer partitions than those found by the algorithm,
  so that once two vertices have been separated we can guarantee that they are in separate orbits"
  (p. 89) — i.e. the algorithm never over-splits, it under-splits.

### 1.6 Sparrow, *Beyond 911* (1990)

- **The reactive strategy's hidden virtue is a legal constraint:** "If the police wait until a crime
  has been committed and reported, there is always a predicate for their intervention… reactive
  tactics prevent the police from shuffling through the drawers of social life" (pp. 97–98). Every
  move toward prevention spends that predicate, which is why prevention "smacks of the police
  state" in the public mind.
- **The consequence is a sensorium made of calls:** "Unless police are on the scene, or witnesses
  call them to it, **a crime is a tree falling in the forest**. Invisible crimes like embezzlement
  and the illegal disposal of toxic wastes almost never come readily to police attention; consensual
  crimes like prostitution and drug dealing rarely do; likewise, crimes whose victims are afraid to
  call" (p. 98).
- **Problem-solving means probing proximate conditions, not root causes:** "crime analysis need not
  be limited to the when and the where… it can also probe what sorts of proximate social activities
  and conditions are leading to the offenses" — and it works "not by getting at the root causes…
  but simply by removing some relatively superficial conditions" (pp. 99–100). The COPE bus-stop
  case is the illustration.
- **Department-wide or nothing:** Stephens insisted the approach be tested "in a way that involved
  the entire police department, not just a special unit set off to the side, not just confining the
  thinking process to a planning or crime analysis unit" (pp. 70–71).
- **The state's own error signal is thrown away:** the complaints department's members "learn these
  things, though, by accident. They are not required to analyze cases in order to generate a
  coherent and reliable picture of the causes of dissatisfaction… If such analysis is not expected,
  it will not get done" (p. 165). What exists instead — "statistical summaries" — "tell more about
  the routing of complaint files than about the failures of the police" (p. 165).

### 1.7 Sparrow, *The Character of Harms* (2008)

- **The whole doctrine in five words:** "Pick Important Problems, and Fix Them" (p. 5).
- **The three unanswerable questions:** can you prove causality, can you assign credit across
  functions, can you measure prevention — "the answers to these three questions… are three no's"
  if the agency keeps doing functional and process business as usual (pp. 126–127).
- **The countable/non-countable boundary, and why it distorts:** you can count the man lowered from
  the roof by his harness; you cannot count the man who did not slip because of the non-slip shoes —
  interventions "separated only by a very few seconds," one countable, one not. "Pressure for
  countable results might bias the choice of interventions towards the set that lies to the right of
  this strange dividing boundary… More resource-efficient and effective intervention opportunities
  might exist, earlier on, but they would not produce countable results" (pp. 140–141).
- **The attribution trap, in one line:** if the commissioner claims a campaign "saved the lives of
  110 kids," "some cynical journalist is surely going to ask the commissioner to name one of them.
  The commissioner cannot name any" (p. 141).

### 1.8 Sparrow, "Problem-oriented policing" (2018)

- **The loop, in two stages, on the knot image:** stage one is "figuring out the structure: the way
  it is. Or the way it works. Its components, structure and dynamics"; stage two is "figuring out
  the weaknesses of the thing itself… Which strand would give way first, or most easily?… if you had
  really understood the structure and figured out the vulnerabilities… then your analysis of the
  structure of the thing leads you to invent a tailor-made solution" (pp. 2–3).
- **Program-centric vs problem-centric:** a general class of harm produces a "general theory of
  operations" and "big machines" that are functional or process based; the alternative is to
  disaggregate the class — "this isn't one problem, but at least 57 varieties" — and unpick them one
  at a time (pp. 3–4).
- **"Vigilance mechanisms"** are the discovery arm: "the methods you use to discover problems or
  potential problems that you might not have known about if you hadn't deliberately looked for
  them" (p. 4).
- **The categories that defeat the machines** (p. 6): catastrophic, emerging, **invisible**,
  **"risks involving conscious adversaries or adaptive opponents, who deliberately circumnavigate
  controls and respond intelligently"**, boundary-spanning, and persistent.
- **The macro circularity trap of underinvestment:** "Nobody really knows how extensive the problem
  is, so the controlling agency cannot build the case for much budget. Because they can't spend
  much, they can't spend much on discovery, so they don't discover much. This reinforces the notion
  that the problem might not be so bad" (pp. 6–7). And the metric is a product of two unknowns:
  detected cases = prevalence × discovery rate, "and both are unknown," so "whenever that product
  moves up or down one never knows which component changed" (p. 7).
- **Deliberate testing of one's own discovery rate** is the escape: "one can measure the discovery
  rate instead, sometimes by testing discovery mechanisms deliberately" (p. 7).
- **Adversary modelling is its own craft:** "if we can't figure out what they are actually thinking,
  then we should work out what they should be thinking. Pay honest people good money for dishonest
  thinking. What would we be thinking if we were in their shoes" (p. 7).
- **The resource constraint, quantified as a rule of thumb:** "for any problem-oriented project an
  agency launches, probably 20% of the effort required will be analytical… Sadly, that usually
  exceeds the analytic capacity agencies have available" (p. 7).
- **The managerial infrastructure** — seven components, of which nomination, selection and coaching
  are the ones scholars can help with: nomination, selection, assignment (people, time, money,
  analysis), recording, formal closure, monitoring/alarms, coaching. Nomination is deliberately
  separated from project leadership because otherwise "every time they do that it makes more work
  for them, [and] they will stop doing it" (p. 8).

## 2. (a) Swarming: the doctrine, its preconditions, its failures

### 2.1 Four levels, multiplicative

The book states it as a conjunction: "The strength of a network, perhaps especially of the
all-channel design, depends on functioning well across all four levels" — organizational, doctrinal,
technological, social (*Swarming*, pp. 59–60) — and *Netwars* adds a fifth, **narrative**, ranking it
first for civil-society actors because they are "less likely to be held together by the kinds of
ethnic or clan ties" (Summary, p. xi).

This is the most important structural fact in the whole corpus for Babylon: **the other capabilities
in the game add, and this one multiplies.** A player with superb organization and no doctrine gets a
crowd; with doctrine and no comms gets a massacre; with comms and no shared purpose gets a rumour
network. The 1998 book's own case shows the levels arriving in sequence and each one changing the
last: the EZLN began as a foco with a hierarchical design, was remoulded by indigenous communal
practice, then remoulded again by the NGOs — "the form and organization of the indigenous communities
permeated and dominated our movement and we had to democratize the Indian way… the EZLN and the
broader Zapatista movement also ended up having to democratize the NGO way" (p. 35).

### 2.2 The duty cycle

Pulsing is not a metaphor for "attacking"; it is a *temporal structure* with three phases and a
mandatory fourth:

| Phase | What is true | What the state can see | What the player can do |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Dormant** | dispersed, no mass, no traffic | almost nothing (this is the whole point) | organize, recruit, argue |
| **Coalescing** | movement, comms volume, a date | **the leak** — this is when a swarm is findable | commit, or abort |
| **Pulse** | omnidirectional, simultaneous | the action itself, and everything it reveals | act |
| **Dispersal** | back to dormant, minus casualties | the aftermath, the wounded, the trail | absorb, deny, narrate |

The corpus supplies the failure at each phase and they are different failures. Dormant is where
morale decays — "During periods of inactivity… it is not easy for a network to keep up its morale and
energy levels" (*Zapatista*, p. 71). Coalescing is where the U-boats died — HF/DF and intercepts
found them *before* the attack (*Swarming*, p. 33). Pulse is where an overreaction becomes an
atrocity nobody ordered (§2.6, item 7). Dispersal is where the wounded and the trail are.

**A swarm has no standing capability.** This is the design consequence, and it is the opposite of
every strategy game's army: the player does not own a force, the player owns a *window*.

### 2.3 Comms: an optimum, and the optimum is low

The book's most counter-intuitive finding, and the one worth building on: **more connectivity is not
better.** "Clearly, the greater the connectivity, the higher the 'average engagement level'… But it
must also be recognized that, under conditions approaching all-channel connectivity, groups of pods
and clusters that lack topsight face some risk of devoting their efforts to targets of lesser
importance. Further, full connectivity, improperly utilized, might actually foster indecision, with
pods and clusters growing uncertain about how to focus their efforts from one moment to the next"
(p. 64).

d'Erlon is the case: a corps with *too much* information and contradictory orders marched all
afternoon between two battles and "without firing a shot at either" (p. 65). The failure is not
ignorance; it is **oscillation**, and it is the exact shape a player's own over-investment in the
comms branch should take on screen: a unit that appears in two zones in one period and acts in
neither.

The floor is equally sharp. Cut the flow and the swarm is not degraded, it is "picked off in detail
— one by one" (p. 67). The doctrinal hedge the book offers is a **prearranged fallback**: units that
lose comms "fall back on the Channel" or move to prearranged positions (p. 74). That is a rule the
player *writes in advance* and cannot revise once the pulse begins — and therefore a rule the state
can learn, predict, and ambush.

### 2.4 What replaces the order: doctrine, and the assembly clock

Two mechanisms, from two books, that must be kept distinct.

**Doctrine substitutes for command.** Shared principles let members be "all of one mind even though
they are dispersed and devoted to different tasks… so that the members do not have to resort to a
hierarchy because 'they know what they have to do'" (*Netwars*, pp. 9–10; *Zapatista*, pp. 13–14).
This is why Beam's leaderless resistance is a *doctrinal* document rather than an organizational
one: "participants… must know exactly what they are doing, and exactly how to do it" (*Netwars*,
p. 334).

**Ratification substitutes for legitimacy.** The Zapatista case supplies the second clock: an order
"would simply not exist" without assembly authorisation (*Zapatista*, p. 33), and authorisation takes
"months and months" of argument, after which dissent has only two options — comply or leave the
community (p. 33). That is a slow, high-latency, *binding* mandate, and it sits underneath a fast,
low-latency, *non-binding* pulse.

Two clocks, one decision: **the pulse is fast and revocable only by failure; the mandate is slow and
revocable only by exit.** Everything interesting in the late game happens in the gap between them.

### 2.5 Packetization

The anti-massing principle: rather than concentrating, split into many simultaneous small units, so
that blocking any one of them accomplishes nothing. The book gives it for drug loads and for NGO
issue-diversity in the same paragraph (*Zapatista*, p. 15) — which is the hint that it is a general
property, not a smuggling trick: **a packetized actor cannot be interdicted, only sampled.**

The corollary is the state's counter, and it is in the same book: **blanketing** — dispersed
omnipresence rather than mass — "to set up an 'omnipresence' that cowed insurgents into military
inactivity" (*Swarming*, p. 26, on Haiti and Chiapas; *Zapatista*, p. 74). A blanket does not
interdict packets; it raises the price of every packet by putting a soldier near each one.

### 2.6 The failure catalogue, and the deflation

Every one of these is documented, and each maps to a mechanic:

1. **Comms cut → defeat in detail** (U-boats; *Swarming*, pp. 33, 67).
2. **Over-connection → indecision** (d'Erlon; p. 65).
3. **No shared purpose → no costly action.** Autonomy means the centre cannot compel. Seattle's line
   held because people chose; the implication is that it would not have held if they hadn't
   (*Netwars*, p. 336). A swarm can only do what its units already want.
4. **Wrong unit size at the wrong moment.** Battalion-sized formations against a state that can
   concentrate fire (*Zapatista*, p. 47: 500–700 fighters, "the optimal battle formation according
   to Mao," and it was the error).
5. **A target that does not care about its image.** "A pariah state… will be less vulnerable to
   social netwar, and less hesitant to crush activists" (*Zapatista*, p. 121). The swarm's leverage
   is a *legitimacy* dependency in the target; remove it and the whole apparatus is inert.
6. **The state adopts the form.** Blanketing, devolved authority, better radios (*Zapatista*, p. 74)
   — the swarm's terrain advantage is not permanent, it is a lag.
7. **Provoked overreaction.** Disinformation planted "in order to provoke an overreaction that
   embarrasses the activists" (*Zapatista*, p. 71). Autonomy cuts both ways: no one can be ordered
   to hold, and no one can be ordered to *refrain*.

**The deflation, from the corpus itself.** Swarming "will work best—perhaps it will only work—if it
is designed mainly around… myriad, small, dispersed, networked maneuver units" (*Swarming*, p. vii) —
which is a *precondition*, not a promise. The historical failures are not outliers: the Zulus were
beaten by fire discipline (p. 30), the Plains nations by numbers (p. 30), the Italians by lack of an
external sponsor (p. 30), the Malays and Huks by the absence of a conventional force to finish the
war (Gann and Taber, cited *Zapatista*, p. 48). And the books' own ten-case survey 1994–2000 gives
five substantial successes, three limited, one failing, one failure (*Netwars*, p. 16 and Table 1.1,
p. 17) — with the failures "networks confronting hierarchical authoritarian governments that have
been willing to use substantial force" (p. 18). Swarming is a
*conditional amplifier*: it multiplies what a movement already has and cannot manufacture the thing
it multiplies.

## 3. (b) The police intelligence process: six stages, six seams

The loop as the corpus actually describes it, not as the org chart does. Sparrow's police manual
names five stages — collection, evaluation, collation, analysis, dissemination (1991, p. 251) — and
the later work adds a stage in front (nomination, 2018) and one behind (assessment, 2008). Six rows
below, six seams. Each row is a bottleneck
with a different scarce resource; the player's move is to starve the cheapest one.

| # | Stage | What the state does | Its scarce resource | The seam | What the player does | What the player risks |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | **Nomination** | canvasses problems, funnels them to a central point and compares them (2018, p. 8) | **attention**, and the 20% analytic rule (p. 7) | the funnel is a queue and the queue is public to anyone who can file | flood it: send more candidate problems than it can assess | each nomination is a document with your fingerprints |
| 1 | **Collection** | contact reports, tolls, financial transactions (1991, p. 252) | **channels** — who is watched, who is talked to | coverage is chosen by prior belief, so coverage *is* the belief | be interesting where you are not | every source you feed becomes a node in its graph |
| 2 | **Evaluation / collation** | merges aliases, resolves entities, discards the unreliable | **the merge decision** | the alias test is structural: many length-2 paths, no direct link (1991, p. 270) | keep two legends' neighbourhoods disjoint, or split one person into two files | a wrong merge is an *asset*, and the state will act on it |
| 3 | **Analysis** | draws the chart, counts degree, hunts cutsets and role uniqueness | **a human analyst's brain** (1991, pp. 254–255) | the chart is redrawn every period and re-centred on max degree (p. 255) | feed the transcript so the centre moves to where you want the raid | the analyst learns your tells faster than you learn theirs |
| 4 | **Dissemination / action** | prices candidates, funds down the list, sweeps, cuts, strikes | **jurisdiction** — the cut must be legal where it is made (1991, p. 266) | the graph is only cuttable where the map and the legal boundary coincide | put the minimal cut across a county line, or across two agencies | two agencies on one target is also two dossiers on one target |
| 5 | **Assessment** | reports outputs, claims credit, defends a budget (2008, pp. 126–127, 140–142) | **a named instance** ("name one of them," p. 141) | it can only count late, near-disaster prevention | demand the name publicly; stage the decoy it will name | the demand also teaches it to build early, uncountable capability |

### 3.1 Stage 0: the funnel, and the circularity trap

The 2018 paper is unusually candid that the state's problem is *selection*, not detection. Nomination
is deliberately separated from project leadership, because otherwise the best people stop nominating
(p. 8). Seven components of managerial infrastructure exist to keep the funnel moving — and the
police profession, Sparrow says, has "talked a lot about the protocols… but we've talked much less
about the background managerial infrastructure" (p. 7).

Underneath the funnel sits the trap, and it is the single best hiding place in the corpus:

> "Nobody really knows how extensive the problem is, so the controlling agency cannot build the case
> for much budget. Because they can't spend much, they can't spend much on discovery, so they don't
> discover much. This reinforces the notion that the problem might not be so bad." (pp. 6–7)

And the metric that would break the loop is itself unreadable: detected cases = prevalence ×
discovery rate, "and both are unknown" (p. 7). **A harm the state cannot estimate is a harm the state
cannot fund.** That is a structural subsidy for invisibility, and it is exactly the reverse of the
usual game logic (where hiding reduces a "suspicion" meter). Here hiding reduces a *budget line*, and
the budget line is what buys the analyst who would have found you.

### 3.2 Stage 1: the degree trap, restated precisely

08 already has "degree, not betweenness." The 1991 paper adds the mechanism that makes it
exploitable rather than merely wrong. Anacapa's third objective is to put the max-degree node "in the
centre of the chart," and then work outward (p. 255). Therefore:

- the chart's **centre is a function of what has been recorded**, not of function;
- every contact report raises a node's degree and can *move the centre*;
- and the analyst "keep[s] redrawing the graph as new information… becomes available" (p. 255).

So the state's targeting is a **moving average of the player's visibility**, and the player can
steer it. This is 08's move 1 ("build the bridge in the open") given its precise mechanism: you are
not hiding a link, you are *choosing whose degree rises*.

### 3.3 Stage 2: the alias test is a real constraint, not a flavour text

The detection rule is exact: "many paths of length two… without a direct connection" (p. 270). Read
as a design constraint rather than a threat:

- A legend can only survive if the two identities **do not share neighbours**. One shared
  acquaintance, one shared landlord, one shared arrest, and the test fires — or worse, does not fire
  and the state merges two of *your* people who are not the same person.
- The state's merge is a **created fact**. Two nodes fused into one dossier means one arrest
  "solves" two functions, and the agency's clearance metric is satisfied by a fiction.
- This is also how the player can *give* the state a twin: two real cadres whose neighbourhoods
  rhyme will be read as one person, and the state will spend a warrant on a hypothesis.

The 1991 paper's own example is cross-agency: "It is conceivable that the same individual could be
known to different agencies by different names… the merged data would show two or more nodes for the
same person" (p. 270). In Babylon the interesting version is the inverse — **two people the state
believes are one.**

### 3.4 Stage 3: the analyst is the algorithm

The most under-modelled fact in the corpus: in 1991 the computer does not analyse. "The analyst then
shunts entities around on the screen in an attempt to produce some meaningful, planar, stress-free,
centrality-preserving two-dimensional representation… the computer acts as a highly versatile image
storage and retrieval device, but performs relatively mundane analytic tasks" (p. 257). Thirty years
later the 2018 paper still budgets **20% of every project as analysis** and notes that this "usually
exceeds the analytic capacity agencies have available" (p. 7).

Therefore the state's analytic throughput is a **headcount and a queue**, not a compute. Which means:

- the loop has a *rate limit* that the player can measure by watching how long the state takes to
  respond to a new name;
- a single competent analyst is therefore a strategic asset, and an agency's ability to *lose* one
  is a real vulnerability — which is the 1991 paper's own complaint about a field that had "almost
  no overlap" with the discipline that could staff it (p. 253);
- and the state's own account of why it fails is *organizational*, not evidentiary: complaints data
  is collected and never analysed because "if such analysis is not expected, it will not get done"
  (*Beyond 911*, p. 165).

Also from 1991, the two overlays: goods one way, money the other, and "strangling either one of those
two flows is enough" (p. 266). That is a *two-channel* resilience rule, not a one-channel one — the
player must keep the material flow and the financial flow on separate graphs, because a single
cutset that severs both ends the operation.

### 3.5 Stage 4: the jurisdictional cut

Sparrow's most concrete operational sentence: the task is "to identify not just a manageable cutset,
but **manageable cutsets within that agency's jurisdiction**" (p. 266). A minimal cut that requires
two agencies to act is not a cut; it is a *referral*, and referrals are where 17's budget substitution
and 08's boundary-spanning tax live.

For Babylon this converts a graph property into a **map property**, and it is the cleanest possible
justification for the Physical map screen existing alongside the Topology screen: a link is only
severable where the state's legal boundary and its map boundary overlap, and the player's job is to
keep the overlap narrow.

### 3.6 Stage 5: the assessment seam

Two independent findings combine into one player move.

*The state must name an instance.* Prevention is countable only in "a rather arbitrary and quirky
sliver of interventions that come late in the unfolding chronology, and in many cases worryingly
close to the disaster itself" (*Character of Harms*, p. 141). The commissioner who claims 110 lives
saved "cannot name any" (p. 141).

*And the demand for countable results pushes it there.* "Pressure for countable results might bias
the choice of interventions towards the set that lies to the right of this strange dividing
boundary… More resource-efficient and effective intervention opportunities might exist, earlier on,
but they would not produce countable results" (pp. 140–141).

So: **force the state to evidence its competence, and it will move its violence later in the
chronology — nearer to the event, nearer to the decoy you have staged.** This is the political twin
of 08's Kalyvas move (make the state's violence land where denunciation is safe). One makes the
state's violence *misdirected*; this one makes its *reporting* misdirected, and the reporting is
what buys next period's budget.

### 3.7 The state's counter-loop, and its two known failures

*Zapatista* ch. 5 gives the only fully documented counternetwar in the corpus, and its shape is a
warning against modelling the state as stupid:

- an **interagency group** (CISEN, Jan 1994) that assessed the EZLN as militarily weak and the
  country-wide armed threat as overstated (pp. 80–81);
- a **three-prong strategy** — confine militarily while improving human-rights behaviour, localise
  politically while regaining control of information, co-opt economically;
- a **trigger keyed to markets, not battles**: it resumed the offensive when EZLN information
  operations moved the currency and the stock market in December 1994 (p. 81);
- and two **documented failures**: interservice coordination blocked by "differing bureaucratic
  cultures," and federal-local coordination blocked by the feudal power structure of landlords,
  caciques and paramilitaries (p. 81).

The market trigger is the single most usable line in the book. **The state's threat sensor is not the
battlefield; it is the exchange rate.** A movement that can move the currency has, in effect, a
detection organ pointed *at itself* — which is why the Zapatistas' most effective operation was
informational and its cost was borne by investors who had never heard of Chiapas.

## 4. (c) Network vulnerability, from both sides

### 4.1 What makes a clandestine network mappable

Every item is a *requirement*, and every requirement is a place to be seen. This is the useful form
of the question: not "what does the state see" but "what must a network do that leaves a trace."

1. **It must coordinate.** The strongest single finding in the corpus. The U-boat swarm was beaten by
   direction-finding and intercepts, not by convoy escorts (*Swarming*, p. 33). Swarming has the
   *highest* information requirement of the four forms (p. vii) — so the most capable form is the
   most detectable form, and the correlation is not a coincidence, it is the same variable.
2. **It must concentrate scarce functions.** Role uniqueness is "a strategic vulnerability within a
   criminal profession" (1991, p. 270): courier-recruiters, drivers, printers, safehouse-holders.
   The state does not need to find the network; it needs to find the one person whose function has no
   substitute.
3. **It must merge identities.** The alias test (p. 270) fires on shared neighbours; a network with
   any density at all cannot keep two legends disjoint forever.
4. **It must pulse.** An action has a date, and preparation has a signature (movement, traffic,
   absence). The swarm's own duty cycle makes the coalescing phase the leak (§2.2).
5. **It must cross boundaries.** Jurisdiction, language, legality, currency — each crossing is a
   stitched seam, and seams are where the state's map is joined (*Zapatista*, p. 81: the state's
   federal-local failure).
6. **It must grow.** Density is interceptable; 08's Weinstein reading and the 1991 paper's
   "incompleteness is systematic" agree: success produces the records that undo it.
7. **It must have a public.** A social netwar's audience is its weapon and its exposure — the
   "CNN effect" cuts both ways (*Zapatista*, pp. 121–122).

### 4.2 What makes it resilient

1. **Duplicate the function, not the person.** Role equivalence, not substitutability (1988,
   pp. 78–79; 1991, pp. 266–269). Two nodes with *the same pattern of ties to different people* are
   equivalent even with no acquaintance in common — which is why "we have three of these" is a real
   defensive statement and "we have two who know each other" is not.
2. **Make every minimal cut cross a border.** The jurisdictional cut (1991, p. 266).
3. **Put the centre where the function is not.** Degree is the observer's metric; a decoy whose
   degree rises is a decoy that gets raided (1991, p. 255).
4. **Split the flows.** Goods and money on separate graphs, "strangling either one… is enough"
   (p. 266) — so *not* splitting them is the vulnerability.
5. **Have no centre of gravity.** "The deniability built into a network affords the possibility that
   it may simply absorb a number of attacks on distributed nodes, leading an attacker to believe the
   network has been harmed… when, in fact, it remains viable" (*Zapatista*, pp. 15–16).
6. **Hold the narrative.** The fifth level is the cheapest redundancy in the system: a shared story
   makes units act without orders (*Netwars*, pp. ix–xi).
7. **Be embedded in a public the state cannot attack cheaply.** The Zapatista case's actual victory
   condition (§4.3).

### 4.3 The exchange rate

This is the synthesis the two earlier briefings leave implicit, and it is the one I would build on.

**Every purchase of resilience is paid for in command.** Concretely, from the corpus:

| Move toward resilience | Buys | Costs |
|---|---|---|
| all-channel instead of hub | decapitation-proof (*Zapatista*, p. 13) | dense comms, therefore interceptable; "difficult to organize and sustain" (*Swarming*, p. 59) |
| leaderless instead of cadre | no head to arrest (*Netwars*, p. 334) | cannot compel the costly act (p. 336); "more difficulty instilling, and enforcing… loyalty" (pp. 342–343) |
| assembly mandate instead of order (*Zapatista*, p. 33) | legitimacy; a following that does not leave | latency measured in months; exit is the only dissent |
| packetization instead of massing (*Zapatista*, p. 15) | uninterdictable | cannot concentrate; each packet is weak alone |
| dispersal instead of garrison (*Swarming*, p. 46) | stealthy ubiquity | logistics, medical, and the coalescing signature |
| a public instead of secrecy (*Zapatista*, pp. 121–122) | a shield the state cannot strike | the public's opinion becomes a constraint on *you* — "no authoritarian figure could have compelled" cuts both ways |

And the state's mirror-image trade is the same table read backwards: **the state buys reach with
legibility.** It cannot act on what it has not resolved (17 §4.6), and everything it resolves can be
read by whoever captures the file. The Zapatista interagency group is the pure case: it coordinated
better and thereby created a document trail of its own assessment that the EZLN's information
operations could then attack (*Zapatista*, pp. 80–81).

**The late game is therefore not a battle for territory but a negotiation over which side pays more
for its own coordination.**

### 4.4 The state's metamorphosis is in the corpus, not a concession

The Zapatista study documents the state adopting the network form in three places, and each is
described as *expensive and partial*, never as a solved problem:

- **Blanketing**: platoon-sized dispersed units with devolved authority and better comms
  (*Zapatista*, p. 74), viable only after earlier failures "traced to organizational requirements for
  field-grade officers… and to inadequacies in electronic communications" (p. 74, n.).
- **Interagency hybrid**: "counternetwar may require very effective interagency approaches, which by
  their nature involve networked structures… the challenge will be to blend these two forms
  skillfully while retaining enough core authority to enforce adherence to networked processes"
  (*Zapatista*, pp. 17–18).
- **The two-front budget bind**: it "was hard to operate actively on both at once," and the
  government "opted to constrain field operations… in order to deal with the other front, despite the
  strain this put on civil-military relations" (p. 76).

The last is the most gameable: **the state cannot afford both fronts at once, and choosing one
generates a grievance inside the agency that lost.** That is 17's Bonapartist arithmetic arriving
from a second direction — not a fiscal contraction but a *front* contraction.

## 5. Design readings (OURS)

Nine rulings — eight mechanics and one guardrail. Each is a mechanic, not a theme; each names the
screen or verb it attaches to; each carries its own failure mode so it cannot become a power.

**R1 — The Pulse is a window, not an army.** *(Topology + Physical map.)* The armed capability is
declared as a pulse: a start, a set of zones, a dispersal. During the pulse, armed nodes act; outside
it they are inert and cannot be tasked. The player commits the plan *before* the first move and
cannot add zones mid-pulse. The state's counter is not defence, it is *detection of coalescence*.
*Failure mode:* a pulse declared to solve an internal political problem arrives with no mandate
(see R2) and with the state already watching the staging areas.

**R2 — The Mandate Clock runs slower than the Pulse Clock.** *(Topology, the assembly/committee
surface.)* A pulse needs a mandate; mandates are produced by deliberation that takes periods and
cannot be shortened by spending. A mandated pulse cannot be recalled; an unmandated one can, at the
cost of the authority of whoever called it. Render the gap as **the plan sitting on the table while
the vote is still open** — the player can see the window closing and the mandate not yet granted, and
must choose which clock to break. *Failure mode:* mandates granted after the pulse are retroactive
legitimation, and the movement learns to launder orders through assemblies — a body that ratifies
becomes a body that is managed, and the mandate decays into procedure.

**R3 — Designation, not numbers.** *(Topology.)* The swarm's reach is set by a small number of
*designator* nodes — scouts, liaisons, the person who knows which yard is empty — not by the count of
fighters. Losing one designator contracts reach by a whole zone without losing a single fighter; the state's HVT logic
therefore finds the right target for the wrong reason (its role-uniqueness test and the player's
reach dependency coincide). This is the 7th Special Forces result (*Swarming*, p. 82) rendered as a
vulnerability rather than a buff. *Failure mode:* the designators are exactly the people the player
cannot afford to keep out of meetings, so reach and exposure rise together.

**R4 — Clogged circuits.** *(Topology, comms as a state not a stat.)* Comms investment has an
optimum. Over-invest and units oscillate: a node appears in two zones in one period and acts in
neither (d'Erlon, *Swarming*, p. 65). Under-invest and a cut turns the pulse into a list of isolated
nodes "picked off in detail." Render as behaviour, never as a slider: two zones, one confused crew,
no action. *Failure mode:* the player reads oscillation as enemy interference and invests *more*,
which is the trap.

**R5 — A legend must have no shared neighbours.** *(Intel ledger.)* A dual identity is only viable
while its two acquaintance sets are disjoint; the moment they rhyme, the state's alias test fires
(1991, p. 270). Show it as two ledger entries whose acquaintance lists visibly converge over periods.
*Failure mode:* the state's *false* merge is more useful to the player than a true one — two cadres
read as one person, one warrant, one fiction. The player who optimises for "undetected" misses that
the merge is a gift.

**R6 — The blank space is the budget.** *(State AI; player-visible only as absence.)* The state's
analytic capacity is a headcount, and a harm it cannot estimate is a harm it cannot fund
(2018, pp. 6–7). So low-visibility organizing does not merely avoid arrest; it *defunds the analyst*
who would have found it. Render as the state's *absence* — a county with no file, no referrals, no
patrols — and as its violence concentrating next door, where the file exists (08's Kalyvas
mechanism). *Failure mode:* the same trap protects the state from seeing *the player's* growth, and
the player will misread its own quiet counties as safe rather than as unmeasured.

**R7 — Name one.** *(Wire / Ticker; a player move in the assessment stage.)* The player can demand
that the state evidence its prevention with a named instance. Because prevention is countable only
late in the chronology (*Character of Harms*, p. 141), the demand pushes the state toward
near-disaster interventions — where the player stages the decoy that will be found. *Failure mode:*
the demand also teaches the state to build early, uncountable capability, which is the one thing that
would actually work; a player who uses R7 every period is training the enemy.

**R8 — Two fronts, one appropriation.** *(State AI.)* Field and infosphere compete for one budget;
choosing one generates a grievance in the agency that lost (*Zapatista*, p. 76). Raise the price of
the information front until the state defunds the field, then operate in the field it abandoned —
and expect the defunded agency to become the Bonapartist cell 17 describes. *Failure mode:* the same
lever works on the player, whose own information work competes with organizing for the same cadre
hours.

**R9 — (guardrail) The pariah switch.** *(State AI, late game.)* Social netwar's leverage is the
target's desire for legitimacy: "A pariah state… will be less vulnerable" (*Zapatista*, p. 121). So
the state has a move that turns the player's best weapon off — stop caring about image — and it is
cheap, brutal, and *available*. The player's job is to make that switch cost the state something
domestic, because a state that can afford to be hated cannot be swarmed. *Failure mode:* if the
switch is free, the late game has one dominant strategy and it belongs to the enemy.

## 7. Uncertainties, dead ends, and what would falsify this

1. **The 1992 *IJIC* paper is unread.** The corpus copy is image-only and no OCR is installed. The
   1991 *Social Networks* paper is the same argument and is cited in full; any claim about the 1992
   paper's distinctive content would be invention. If it matters, it needs OCR, not inference.
2. **The pod/cluster arithmetic is the authors' back-of-envelope.** "One light strike vehicle… equal
   to one tank" (p. 62, n. 41) is an assumption, not a finding. Do not tune Babylon's combat numbers
   to it. The *structural* claims (designation, dispersal, comms dependence) are separately
   evidenced; the ratios are not.
3. **"Swarming works" is not the corpus's finding.** Five substantial successes, three limited, one
   failing, one failure across ten cases 1994–2000 (*Netwars*, Table 1.1, p. 17), and the failures
   are networks against states "willing to use substantial force" — i.e. the failures are exactly the cases Babylon's
   late game would model. The books are advocacy documents written to get the US military to adopt a
   doctrine; their case selection is not neutral and I have not audited it.
4. **The Zapatista causal claim is the authors' own.** "Our analysis, however, is that… the
   transnational activist netwar—particularly the information operations—was a key contributing
   factor" (p. 63). RAND was arguing for the significance of a concept it had invented. The *events*
   (two halts, a free zone) are hard; the *weighting* is soft. I have used the events and flagged
   the weighting.
5. **The assembly-mandate evidence is thin.** The "comply or leave" account is one informant's
   (Tello, p. 184) and the vote dates are disputed in the sources themselves (1992 vs 1993, *Zapatista*
   p. 34, n. 15). The *mechanism* (authority borrowed from a slow body) is well attested; the
   *procedure* is not.
6. **Seattle is not a general case.** N30 is the corpus's own example of "information-age networks…
   prevail[ing] against hierarchies… at least for a while" (*Swarming*, p. 51) — a hedge the authors
   put there themselves. Nothing in the corpus shows affinity-group swarming working
   against a state that has adopted blanketing. The 1998 book says so directly about Chiapas: the
   army "proved that the EZLN was a 'paper tiger' from a military standpoint" (p. 76).
7. **What would falsify R1 (the pulse as window).** If playtests show the player can hold a standing
   armed capability and still be survivable, the pulse structure is unnecessary decoration and
   should be cut rather than tuned.
8. **What would falsify R2 (the mandate clock).** If the delay never changes a decision — if the
   player always waits and always wins by waiting — then the clock is a cutscene and the two-clock
   design should collapse into one.
9. **What would falsify R6/R7 together.** If the state's blindness never produces a *visible*
   asymmetry the player can act on, the intel loop is set dressing. The test: a player should be able
   to say, without any readout, "they are not here because they have no file here" — and be right
   often enough to plan around.
10. **A caution on the whole shelf.** These books are counterinsurgency and enforcement literature;
    the state's loop is described by people who wanted it to work, and the swarm is described by
    people who wanted the US military to adopt it. Both are useful *as specifications of the enemy's
    own model*, which is how 08 uses them. Read straight, they would make the player a special-forces
    commander with a network chart — which is the failure mode this briefing exists to prevent.
