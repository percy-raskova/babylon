# 29 — Army intelligence and the COIN manual, inverted

Read-only briefing, 2026-10-25. Question: **if the player is the insurgent, what does the U.S. Army intelligence cycle look like as seven player verbs — and what does FM 3-24 look like as a catalogue of the ways the state will try to make the player legible, dependent, or divided?**

**Documents read.**

- **ADP 2-0, Intelligence, October 2026** (supersedes the 31 July 2019 edition). 56 pp. Read in full via the Army Publishing Directorate PDF (ARN47886). Current edition, three weeks old at the time of writing.
- **FM 3-24 / MCWP 3-33.5, Insurgencies and Countering Insurgencies, May 2014, incorporating Change 2, May 2025.** 202 pp. Read in full. Change 2 only replaces “gender” with “sex” on pp. 2-3 and 3-3; the body is the 2014 text with Change 1.
- **FM 3-24 / MCWP 3-33.5, Counterinsurgency, December 2006.** Read in full. Used only where the 2014 edition drops a concept the player needs (provocation, informants, the “irreconcilable” vignette).
- **FM 3-24.2, Tactics in Counterinsurgency, April 2009.** Read in full. Used for populace and resource control and for the double-agent passage.
- **ATP 2-01.3, Intelligence Preparation of the Battlefield, March 2019 (Change 1).** Read in full. The current edition is Change 3 (May 2025); the four-step process and the NAI/TAI/event-template definitions are stable across the editions I could compare.
- **CALL Handbook 09-27, Commander’s Guide to Money as a Weapons System, April 2009.** Read in full. This is the source of the phrase “money as a weapons system”; FM 3-24 does not use it.
- **FM 2-0, Intelligence, October 2023.** Only the “Intelligence Requirements” excerpt was available in the mirror used; the PIR function is paraphrased from that excerpt and flagged where it matters.

**Limits.**

- FM 3-24 is an **external-intervener** manual: U.S. forces supporting a host nation (¶1-1–1-3, ¶1-92). The game’s state is domestic. The translation “host nation → county government; U.S. forces → federal/auxiliary task force” is a deliberate fiction, not a doctrine claim. See §14.
- The 2014 manual does **not** use the pair “reconcilables / irreconcilables.” It uses “moderate insurgents” (¶10-47), “rifts” (¶10-42), and “generous surrender terms” (¶10-53). The pair appears once in the 2006 edition, in a commander’s vignette, not as doctrine (2006, p. 4-7). The mechanic below is an inference from that practice; it is marked OURS and uncertain.
- “Money as a weapons system” is **not** FM 3-24 language. FM 3-24’s term is “integrated monetary shaping operations” (¶10-56–10-71). MAAWS is CALL 09-27 (2009). Both are used, separately.
- The seven-step intelligence cycle in the prompt is a **teaching model**, not the current Army list. ADP 2-0 (2026) has six steps and merges process/exploit and analyze/produce (¶4-4, fig. 4-2). The seven verbs below deliberately split the merged steps for game legibility; the doctrine citation for each verb is given.
- Paragraph numbers are from the editions named. ADP 2-0 (2026) is new enough that a future edition may renumber; quotations are verbatim from the PDF read on 2026-10-25.
- This is fiction design. It is not real-world operational guidance.

---

## 0. Thesis in five sentences

1. The Army’s intelligence cycle is a **capital** cycle — platforms, staffs, and databases converted into decisions — while the clandestine player’s cycle is a **social** cycle that converts relationships into decisions, so every step costs a relationship rather than a satellite pass.
2. The player’s PIR is not a document but **three Standing Asks**: questions that change a decision this week, each with a named person who could answer it and a price that person pays for answering.
3. The player’s IPB is not a staff product but **the County Book**: a distributed, decaying, and seizable map of ground, state, society, and economy, whose accuracy is an asset and whose existence is a liability.
4. FM 3-24 is best read as a **menu of state moves**, each of which is either an exploit (a lever the player can pull) or a trap (an offer that makes the player legible, dependent, or divided).
5. The manual’s deepest threat is not firepower but **classification** — the state’s power to decide who is reconcilable and who is irreconcilable, and to make that decision true by offering just enough to split the player’s organization.

# Part I — The player’s intelligence cycle

## 1. The doctrinal baseline, and why the game splits it

ADP 2-0 (2026) defines the intelligence process as “the process by which information is converted into intelligence and made available to users” (¶4-3, quoting JP 2-0). Its steps are **plan and direct; collect; process and exploit; analyze and produce; disseminate and integrate; evaluate and provide feedback** (¶4-4, fig. 4-2). The steps overlap and recur; they are not a clean sequence (¶4-4).

The older seven-step list in the prompt — plan, prepare, collect, process, exploit, analyze, disseminate — is a teaching model that separates two pairs the current Army manual merges: **process** and **exploit** are one step in ADP 2-0 (¶4-12–4-14), and **analyze** and **produce** are one step (¶4-15–4-18). The current manual adds a sixth step, **evaluate and provide feedback**, which the older seven-step teaching list did not separate as its own step (¶4-22–4-25).

For the game, the split is useful because each half is a different player activity with a different failure mode. The seven verbs below are therefore a **design decomposition of ADP 2-0’s six steps**, not a claim about current doctrine. The mapping is explicit:

| ADP 2-0 (2026) step | Player verb | Player artifact | Currency spent |
|---|---|---|---|
| Plan and direct (¶4-5–4-7) | **ASK** | the Standing Asks | attention; a relationship risked by asking |
| Prepare / IPOE (¶1-9, §5-8; ATP 2-01.3 ¶1-13) | **MAP** | the County Book | memory; the danger of writing things down |
| Collect (¶4-8–4-11) | **LISTEN** | the ears | a favor; a lookout’s safety |
| Process and exploit (¶4-12–4-14) | **SIFT** | the provenance tag | time; the risk of corroboration |
| Analyze and produce (¶4-15–4-18) | **READ** | the Pattern / the Tell | analysis; the temptation to see what you expect |
| Disseminate and integrate (¶4-19–4-21) | **PASS** | the courier / the drop | latency; the messenger’s body |
| Evaluate and provide feedback (¶4-22–4-25) | **BURN** | the Burn List | a capability destroyed on purpose |

**OURS.** The seven verbs are the player’s interface to the intel cycle. They should be usable in any order, but each one should produce a visible object on the player’s board: an Ask, a page of the Book, an Ear, a provenance tag, a Tell, a courier route, a Burn. The state’s cycle produces *products*; the player’s produces *obligations and liabilities*. That difference is the game.

## 2. The seven verbs

### 2.1 ASK — plan and direct

**Doctrine.** Planning and directing includes “determining and prioritizing intelligence requirements,” “preparing collection and production plans,” “developing an appropriate intelligence architecture,” and “issuing orders and requests” (ADP 2-0 ¶4-5). The staff produces a “synchronized and integrated information collection plan focused on answering intelligence requirements” (¶4-7). PIRs are part of the commander’s critical information requirements (ADP 2-0 ¶4-24) and, in FM 2-0’s phrasing, are “the intelligence component of commander’s critical information requirements” used to focus limited collection (FM 2-0 2023, “Intelligence Requirements” — paragraph not captured, flagged).

**Player version.** The cell lead holds **three Standing Asks**. Not a list of interests; three questions whose answers would change an action this week. Each Ask names (a) the decision it serves, (b) the person or place that could answer it, (c) the price that source pays for answering, and (d) the answer-by time. The Ask is never written down. It is carried in the head of the person who owns the decision, and it is given face to face to the person who will answer it.

**What it looks like.** A meeting in a parked car. A question phrased as gossip: “Is the sheriff still short on jail beds?” A burner used only to receive the answer, never to ask the question. The ask is deliberately *small*: not “what is the state doing?” but “will the raid come before the funeral?” The state’s PIR may be “who is the leader of the network?”; the player’s Ask is “which of my people is the state already talking to?” — a question the state’s own behavior can answer.

**Failure.** The cell asks too many questions, or asks questions whose answers cannot change anything. The state’s collection is limited by assets (ATP 2-01.3 ¶1-28, ¶1-44); the player’s is limited by *people willing to answer*. Over-asking burns ears, and a burned ear is worse than a blind spot because it tells the state you were asking.

**OURS.** Three slots, not a slider. The player can add a fourth Ask only by converting an existing relationship into a “standing” obligation, which raises that contact’s exposure. The interface question is not “what do you want to know?” but “what will you do differently if you know?” If the answer is nothing, the Ask is invalid.

### 2.2 MAP — prepare / intelligence preparation

**Doctrine.** IPOE is “the systematic process of analyzing the mission variables of enemy, terrain, weather, and civil considerations in an area of interest to determine their effect on operations” (ADP 2-0 §5-8, IPOE block; the same definition appears as IPB in ATP 2-01.3 ¶1-1). Its four steps are define the operational environment, describe environmental effects, evaluate the threat, and determine threat courses of action (ATP 2-01.3 ¶1-13). The process identifies gaps that become information requirements and PIRs (¶1-44), and it produces NAIs and TAIs for collection and targeting (¶1-46).

**Player version.** The **County Book**: four ledgers, kept in the heads of four different people, never in one place.

1. **Ground.** Roads, bridges, fords, cut-offs, bus routes, curfew geography, dead zones, cell coverage, cameras, patrol routes, the places a car can be parked without being seen.
2. **State.** Which agency owns which question: sheriff, state police, prosecutor, federal task force, National Guard, county board, code enforcement, school district. Their rivalries, budgets, election calendars, and informant handlers. The manual assumes unity of effort, but admits that unity of command over civilian organizations is impossible and that partners have “many interests and agendas” (FM 3-24 ¶1-53). The state is not one mind; the Book must show the seams.
3. **Society.** Kinship, land tenure, debts, feuds, churches and mosques, unions, burial societies, sports clubs, veterans’ posts, school boards. Who resolves disputes when the state is not trusted? Who can call a strike or shut a road?
4. **Economy.** Wages, remittances, harvest, smuggling, contracts, rents, fuel, food flows. Who loses if the project happens? Who gains? FM 3-24’s own economic analysis says the informal economy may be of “equal or greater importance” than the formal one (¶2-25, ¶2-27); the player’s Book lives there.

**The four attributes.** Coverage (how much of the county you can name), Confidence (how many independent sources corroborate it), Freshness (how fast it decays), and **Exposure** (what happens if the state gets it). The first three are the usual intelligence metrics. The fourth is the inversion: the state’s IPB is an asset that grows; the player’s Book is a **witness list**. FM 3-24 tells the state to build “a common searchable combatant command database of insurgent actions and personnel” (¶8-8) and to exploit “computers, cell phones, and other means of communication” found on targets (¶8-28). The Book is exactly the thing that database wants.

**County-level NAIs and TAIs.** The Book’s watch list (NAIs) should be places where the state’s *logistics* are visible: the garrison gate, the courthouse, the land office, the bus depot, the contractor’s yard, the funeral home, the jail visiting room, the hospital, the high school, the county fairground. The action list (TAIs) should be places where the player can act: the checkpoint, the informant’s house, the contractor’s equipment yard, the recruiter’s office, the armory, the judge’s neighborhood. ATP 2-01.3 defines an NAI as the area or node “against which information that will satisfy a specific information requirement can be collected” and a TAI as the area “where high-value targets can be acquired and engaged” (¶1-46). The player’s version adds a third list: **places you must never be seen**, which the state’s Book will have too.

**Failure.** The Book becomes centralized (one notebook, one phone, one person), or it becomes “accurate but unusable” (so compartmented that no one can act on it), or it becomes “usable but wrong” (rumor promoted to fact). The state’s IPOE has a staff to refine it (ADP 2-0 ¶5-36); the player has a handful of people and a decay clock.

**OURS.** The Book is a **risk-allocation mechanic**, not a tech tree. Splitting it lowers Exposure but raises the cost of Coverage and Confidence; centralizing it does the reverse. If the state captures a page, it gains a targeting list, not a debuff. The player’s counter is not “encrypt the Book” but “decide which pages can be lost.”

### 2.3 LISTEN — collect

**Doctrine.** Collection is “the acquisition of information and the provision of this information to processing elements” (ADP 2-0 ¶4-8). Collection assets collect about “the threat, terrain and weather, civil considerations, and other aspects of the OE” (¶4-9). In COIN, “people may approach Soldiers and Marines … and offer information” (FM 3-24 ¶8-15), but people also “provide inaccurate and conflicting information … to settle tribal, ethnic, or business disputes” (¶8-17), and sources need protection (¶8-16).

**Player version.** **Ears**: lookouts, shopkeepers, barbers, bus drivers, custodians, nurses, clerks, funeral-goers, jail visitors, delivery drivers, sympathetic members of the volunteer fire department. An Ear is not a sensor; it is a person with a normal pattern and a threshold. The player does not “task” an Ear; the player **asks a favor**, and the favor is the exposure.

**What it looks like.** A lookout at the bus depot who notes which deputies get off the 6:40. A cousin who works the courthouse copier. A sympathizer who drives the ambulance and knows which nights the jail calls in extra beds. A bartender who hears the contractor’s foreman complain about the new hires. The player’s collection is episodic: market day, payday, the fair, the funeral, the school board meeting. The state’s collection is persistent; the player’s is *occasioned*.

**Failure.** The player mistakes gossip for collection. FM 3-24 warns that information is often self-interested (¶8-17); the state’s own doctrine says a “high number of un-coerced informant reports” could be “an important indicator of a breakdown in insurgent security” (¶12-24). The player’s Ear can be working for the other side, or can be *seen* working for the player. The worst outcome is not a lie; it is a true report that identifies the source.

**OURS.** Every Ear has **reliability**, **risk**, and **reach**. “Pressing” an Ear raises yield and risk; “resting” it lowers both. The state’s tip line and rewards program are the mirror (FM 3-24 ¶10-30; CALL 09-27 ch. 6): the player’s Ear and the state’s informant are the same social role, and the player should never be sure which side of the channel they are on.

### 2.4 SIFT — process and exploit

**Doctrine.** Processing and exploitation is “the conversion of collected information into forms suitable to the production of intelligence” (ADP 2-0 ¶4-12). Data must be sorted, correlated, and converted; information “must be evaluated and corroborated to assess reliability, validity, and relevance” (¶4-14). FM 3-24 tells the state to verify information before using it to support operations (¶8-17).

**Player version.** **SIFT** is a person, not a pipeline. The sifter asks three questions of every report: Who said it? What do they gain? Who else could know? The output is a claim with a **provenance tag**: source, date, motive, confidence, and the cost of being wrong. The player’s version of “fusion” is a meeting of two or three people who do not like each other enough to lie in the same way.

**What it looks like.** A kitchen table with three versions of the same patrol schedule. A coded notebook that records “R (barber, owes us, scared)” rather than “source says.” A decision to throw away a report because the source’s brother-in-law is the contractor. A deliberate check: send a second Ear to the same place with a different question.

**Failure.** The player accepts the first corroboration as truth, or discounts a true report because the source is unlikeable. The state’s fusion cell has more sources and can afford to be wrong (ADP 2-0 ¶5-12; FM 3-24 ¶8-12); the player cannot. The failure mode is not “bad data”; it is **a confident story that everyone in the cell repeats** because it flatters the group’s theory.

**OURS.** A three-source rule is too rigid for a small cell; use a **two-source + motive** rule. The player must name the source’s interest before the claim enters the Book. If the sifter cannot name an interest, the claim is marked “unowned” and cannot be used for an action that risks lives.

### 2.5 READ — analyze and produce

**Doctrine.** Analysis and production converts processed information into intelligence “through the integration, evaluation, analysis, and interpretation of all-source data” (ADP 2-0 ¶4-15). Analysts conduct predictive analysis to identify threat activities and trends, using indicators for each threat course of action (¶4-16). The event template is a guide to collection that “depicts the named areas of interest where activity, or its lack of activity, will indicate which course of action the adversary has adopted” (ATP 2-01.3 ¶6-65).

**Player version.** **READ** is the cell’s theory of the state: its patrol rhythm, its raid thresholds, its budget cycle, its election calendar, and **which agency will move first**. The output is a **Pattern** (a calendar plus a map) and a **Tell** (the indicator that distinguishes one state course of action from another). The player is not trying to know everything; the player is trying to know *what the state will do next* before the state knows what the player will do next.

**The state’s course-of-action menu.** The player’s event template should distinguish at least these:
- **Raid:** fuel trucks staging, leave cancelled, jail beds cleared, EMS staged, briefing at the armory, helicopter maintenance.
- **Census/mapping:** door-to-door teams, handheld devices, school enrollment drives, utility hookup lists, property-tax mailers, “community survey” contractors.
- **Offer/talks:** a visiting official, an elders’ meeting, a reduced sentence, a press conference, a new “hotline.”
- **Development:** a grant announcement, an out-of-county contractor, a jobs fair, a new well/clinic/school, a photo op.
- **Repression:** new checkpoints, curfew, license-plate readers, drones, a “task force,” a federal grant for “domestic violent extremism.”
- **Withdrawal/transition:** handover ceremony, new local uniforms, a reduced federal footprint, a shift in who answers calls.

**Failure.** The player reads intentions instead of logistics. The state’s doctrine says “without accurate and predictive intelligence, it is often better to not act rather than to act” (FM 3-24 ¶1-80); the player’s version is “if you cannot name the Tell, do not move.” The other failure is **mirror-imaging**: assuming the state will act like a rational unitary actor, when the manual’s own coordination problem (¶1-53) means the sheriff, the task force, and the prosecutor may each be playing a different game.

**OURS.** The Tell should be a *public* object on the player’s board: a short list of indicators, each tied to a state agency and a location. When two indicators fire, the player gets a prediction; when three fire, the player gets a warning. The state’s event template is a collection plan (ATP 2-01.3 ¶6-65); the player’s is a *decision* plan. This is the mechanic that lets a small cell beat a larger OODA loop: not faster processing, but a pre-committed branch.

### 2.6 PASS — disseminate and integrate

**Doctrine.** Dissemination is “the delivery of intelligence to users in a suitable form”; integration is “the application of intelligence to appropriate missions, tasks, and functions” (ADP 2-0 ¶4-19). Effective dissemination depends on an architecture and must account for “latency and transport method bandwidth limitations” (¶4-20); the dissemination plan includes the time required for intelligence to reach the user (¶4-21).

**Player version.** **PASS** is a choice between three channels, each with a different latency/risk trade:
- **Face to face.** Fastest, highest trust, highest exposure: two people in one place.
- **Courier.** Moderate latency, moderate trust, the courier becomes a node the state can follow.
- **Dead drop / signal.** Lowest exposure, highest latency, and the message cannot answer a follow-up question.

The player’s “architecture” is a chain, not a network. A chain is efficient and fragile. FM 3-24’s 2006 edition lists “insurgents’ need for secrecy” and “informants within the insurgency” as vulnerabilities (2006 ¶1-95); a chain concentrates both.

**What it looks like.** A pre-arranged phrase on a burner that means “the raid is on.” A written message in a cigarette pack behind a loose board. A meeting in a church parking lot after the 10:30 service, with a lookout at the road. A courier who does not know what they are carrying.

**Failure.** The player passes *too much*: a message that explains the reasoning gives the state the network. The player passes *too little*: the action cell gets a warning without a branch and improvises. The state’s dissemination can be broad because it has an architecture (¶4-20); the player’s must be narrow because every additional recipient is another person who can be arrested.

**OURS.** Treat PASS as **bandwidth allocation**: the player has a fixed number of “message slots” per period, and each slot has a latency and an exposure. The state’s collection is persistent; the player’s dissemination is the bottleneck. The design question is “who does not get told?” not “how do we send it?”

### 2.7 BURN — evaluate and provide feedback

**Doctrine.** The final step is continuous assessment: “deliberate evaluation and feedback are essential,” and assessments identify “threat deception and denial efforts” (ADP 2-0 ¶4-22–4-24). The state’s feedback loop is designed to improve collection and to learn (FM 3-24 ¶1-90).

**Player version.** **BURN** is the inversion: the player’s feedback loop is designed to **forget**. After an action, the player decides what to destroy: the phone, the SIM, the safehouse, the car, the contact, the plan, the photo, the address book. The player also decides what to *keep*: the lesson, the relationship, the route, the cover identity. The state’s site exploitation (FM 3-24 ¶8-28) makes this concrete: a phone is a map; a notebook is a witness list; a safehouse is a node.

**What it looks like.** A phone smashed and burned, not stored. A safehouse emptied before the next patrol. A contact “rested” for a month. A plan changed after one indicator fires. A courier route abandoned after a near-miss. A meeting place not reused.

**Failure.** The player keeps a capability because it is useful. That is the trap: the state does not need to catch the player in the act; it needs to catch the *record* of the act. The 2006 manual’s informant passage says nothing is more demoralizing than discovering that people inside the movement are providing information (2006 ¶1-104); the modern equivalent is the seized phone that contains the whole network.

**OURS.** The Burn List is a **voluntary capability loss**. Each burn removes a bonus and reduces a risk. The player should feel the cost: the reliable phone, the safehouse near the bus depot, the cousin who works at the courthouse. The mechanic makes “security” a series of small deaths rather than a stat.

## 3. The Standing Asks (the player’s PIR)

**Definition.** A Standing Ask is a question that (a) changes a decision this period, (b) has a named source or place that could answer it, (c) has a price the source pays for answering, and (d) expires. The cell holds three. The state’s PIRs are limited by collection assets (ATP 2-01.3 ¶1-28, ¶1-44); the player’s are limited by **relationships willing to bear the risk of answering**.

**The four tests.**
1. **Decision test.** If either answer leaves the player’s action unchanged, it is not an Ask.
2. **Source test.** Name the person who could plausibly know, and what they lose by telling you. If you cannot name them, the Ask is a wish.
3. **Betrayal test.** If the state obtained this Ask, what would it learn about the player’s intentions, contacts, and fears? An Ask that reveals more than it yields is a state trap.
4. **Expiry test.** An Ask that does not expire becomes a standing order, and standing orders are what informants report. The state’s collection is continuous; the player’s questions should be *episodic*.

**County examples.**
- “Will the sheriff’s department move on the north-side cell before the harvest?” (decision: relocate or stay; source: a jail trustee or a dispatcher; price: their job or their safety).
- “Who is telling the task force where the meetings are?” (decision: cut a contact or feed them a false meeting; source: the person who is always asked to confirm; price: exposure).
- “Will the county accept the development grant, and who controls the contractor list?” (decision: contest, capture, or shadow the project; source: a county clerk or a board member’s aide; price: access).
- “Which faction will the state invite to talks?” (decision: preempt, split, or join; source: a church elder or a defense lawyer; price: being seen talking to the state).
- “Is the new checkpoint a raid staging point or a permanent control?” (decision: avoid, probe, or attack; source: a highway department worker; price: a job).

**The state’s Ask list is legible.** Because PIRs drive collection and collection is observable (ADP 2-0 ¶4-5, ¶4-8; ATP 2-01.3 ¶1-44), the player can infer the state’s priorities from where it looks. A raid on a church safehouse says the state’s PIR is “where do they meet?” A census in a neighborhood says the PIR is “who lives there?” A sudden interest in a contractor says the PIR is “who is on the payroll?” The player’s READ step should include a **state PIR estimate**: what are the three questions the state is trying to answer this month? The player can then *sell* the state an answer (through a double agent or a planted document) or *starve* the question.

**OURS.** The Ask is the game’s core “intent” object. It should be visible to the player, invisible to the state, and expensive to change. The state’s counter is not to steal the Ask; it is to make the source afraid to answer, or to make the answer irrelevant by acting faster than the player can ask.

## 4. The County Book (the player’s IPB)

**Four ledgers, not one map.** Ground, State, Society, Economy (§2.2). The Book is a graph: nodes are places, people, and institutions; edges are trust, obligation, fear, kinship, employment, debt. FM 3-24’s “attack the network” doctrine tells the state to map friendly, neutral, and threat networks (¶7-51, ¶7-55); the player’s Book is the same object seen from the other side. The difference is that the state’s map is a targeting product, and the player’s is a **survival product**.

**The state ledger is the one most players will under-build.** The manual’s own coordination problem is the reason. Unity of command over civilian organizations is impossible; partners have “many interests and agendas” (¶1-53). A county has a sheriff who wants clearance rates, a prosecutor who wants convictions, a federal task force that wants a network diagram, a county board that wants grants, a school district that wants calm, and a National Guard that wants to go home. Each has a different PIR. The player who knows which agency owns which question can:
- give the sheriff a drug bust that has nothing to do with the movement;
- give the task force a network diagram that is true but useless;
- give the county board a grant success that makes the project look good while the player captures its jobs;
- make the prosecutor’s case collapse by producing a witness who is unreliable.

**OURS.** The State ledger should be a **jurisdiction map**, not a chain of command. Each agency gets a PIR, a budget cycle, a career incentive, and a threshold for cooperation. The player’s best intelligence is not “what does the state know?” but “which agency will act on it, and which will sit on it?”

**The seizure problem.** The Book’s Exposure attribute is the design’s sharpest inversion. The state’s IPB is protected by classification and force; the player’s Book is protected by memory and compartmentation. If the state captures a page:
- it gains the nodes on that page (names, places, routes);
- it gains the *shape* of the player’s ignorance (what the player was trying to learn);
- it gains a propaganda win (“we found their list”);
- it may gain a **false** picture if the player has planted a page.

The player can deliberately let a page be captured. That is a **deception move**, and it should be expensive: the people on the page are real, even if the page is false. The state’s validation process (FM 3-24 ¶10-44: defector information “must be evaluated and validated to prevent misinformation and deception”) is the counter.

**The Tell, not the prediction.** The Book should not produce a prophecy. It should produce a **short list of indicators** that the player can watch. The state’s event template is a collection guide (ATP 2-01.3 ¶6-65); the player’s Tell list is a **branching plan**: if the fuel trucks stage, the raid is within 24 hours; if the census teams appear, the mapping is underway; if the visiting official arrives, an offer is coming. The player’s advantage is not a better forecast; it is a pre-committed response that runs faster than the state’s fusion.

## 5. The asymmetry

| Dimension | State | Player |
|---|---|---|
| **Currency** | capital: platforms, staff, databases, money | relationships: favors, kinship, fear, obligation |
| **Persistence** | continuous; sensors stay on | episodic; market day, funeral, payday, meeting |
| **Architecture** | network; redundant; centralized fusion | chain; efficient; fragile |
| **Failure** | embarrassment, budget cut, career | arrest, death, network roll-up |
| **Improvement** | learning organization (FM 3-24 ¶1-90) | trust and speed; trust decays when unused, burns when used |
| **Blind spot** | the specific, the local, the unrecorded | the pattern, the aggregate, the state’s own seams |
| **Best target** | the seam between agencies | the informant, the courier, the book |

**The central asymmetry.** The state can afford to be wrong. Its collection is free at the margin: a patrol, a census, a tip line, a satellite pass. The player cannot afford to be wrong, because the cost of a wrong Ask is a person. This is why the player’s cycle must be *slower* in planning and *faster* in execution: the state’s advantage is persistence; the player’s is the pre-committed branch.

**OURS.** Do not balance the two cycles by giving the player “better intel.” Balance them by making the state’s intelligence *abundant but ambiguous* and the player’s *scarce but actionable*. The player should frequently know one thing the state does not, and never know whether it is still true.

## 6. The failure modes of the player’s loop

FM 3-24 (2006) lists the vulnerabilities a skilled counterinsurgent exploits: “insurgents’ need for secrecy,” “inconsistencies in the mobilization message,” “need to establish a base of operations,” “reliance on external support,” “need to obtain financial resources,” “internal divisions,” “need to maintain momentum,” and “informants within the insurgency” (¶1-95). The player’s loop should be scored against the same list, inverted:

| Insurgent vulnerability (2006 ¶1-95) | Player-side symptom | State’s collection move |
|---|---|---|
| Secrecy | too many people know the Ask; the Book is centralized | signals intelligence, site exploitation, arrests |
| Message inconsistency | the cell says “defense” but acts “offense” | information operations, credibility attack |
| Base of operations | one safehouse, one clinic, one meeting place | surveillance, raid, cordon |
| External support | money or weapons from outside the county | financial investigation, interdiction |
| Financial resources | visible spending, unpaid cadre, extortion | counter-threat finance (FM 3-24 ¶9-49) |
| Internal divisions | factions, gossip, old feuds | amnesty, rewards, “rifts” (¶10-42) |
| Momentum | long quiet periods, no action | patrols, checkpoints, “presence” |
| Informants | a courier who talks, a cousin who reports | tip lines, rewards, double agents |

**OURS.** These eight are the player’s “health bars,” but they should not be visible as bars. They should be visible as *events*: a cousin stops coming to meetings, a courier buys a new car, a faction leader asks for a private meeting with the state. The state’s intelligence cycle is the machine that turns these symptoms into a raid.

# Part II — FM 3-24 as a vulnerability catalogue

## 7. How to read the manual

FM 3-24 is not a script. It is a **menu of state moves** under constraints. The 2014 edition lists ten strategic principles (¶1-77–1-92), five phases (shape, clear, hold, build, transition; ¶9-7–9-37), five imperatives for the phases (¶9-11), an indirect method (identify, separate, isolate, influence, reintegrate; ¶10-25–10-53), and a money doctrine (integrated monetary shaping operations; ¶10-56–10-71). Each of these is a *move* the state can make. The player’s job is to read the move and choose: **exploit** (convert it into a cost for the state) or **avoid the trap** (refuse the offer that makes the player legible, dependent, or divided).

The distinction matters because the same doctrinal move can be both. A development project is an exploit if the player captures its jobs and a trap if the player contests a clinic the population needs. An amnesty offer is an exploit if the player sends a double agent and a trap if it lets the best commander leave with impunity. The catalogue below gives both readings for each imperative.

**The state may not follow its own doctrine.** A competent state will; a corrupt, rivalrous, or politically pressured state will not. The player’s best exploits are often in the gap between the doctrine and the local commander’s incentives.

When this briefing says “the state,” it means the coalition of agencies that share the counterinsurgency mission — sheriff, prosecutor, task force, county board, Guard — not a single mind. The manual’s own coordination problem (¶1-53) is the reason the seams are the player’s best target.

## 8. The ten strategic principles as state moves

### 8.1 Legitimacy is the main objective — ¶1-78

**State move.** Govern through a mix of consent and coercion; make the population accept the state’s authority as legitimate (¶1-27–1-33). Legitimacy “determines the transaction costs of political and governmental power” (¶1-29). A legitimate government needs less coercion; an illegitimate one relies on it (¶1-29).

**Exploit.** Force the state to defend an act the population sees as illegitimate. The state’s own doctrine says coercive acts “can damage legitimacy if the methods cannot be justified under the values and norms of the population” (¶1-30). A corrupt contractor, a brutal deputy, a closed clinic, a land seizure: each is a legitimacy cost the player can amplify. The player does not need to win the population’s love; the player needs the state to spend its legitimacy faster than it can replenish it.

**Trap.** The state benefits when the player attacks a school, a clinic, or a funeral. That converts the player’s violence into the state’s legitimacy (¶1-89; ¶9-31). The trap is not the target; it is the *audience*. If the population sees the player as the reason the clinic closed, the state wins even if the clinic was a mapping project.

**OURS.** Legitimacy should be a **two-sided ledger**, not a player stat. The state loses legitimacy when it coerces the innocent; the player loses it when it coerces the neutral or destroys a service. The design question is “who does the population blame?” — and the answer should depend on what the player did *last*, not on a slogan.

### 8.2 Understand the society and culture — ¶1-79

**State move.** Invest in language, culture, and local knowledge; overcome the insurgent’s “distinct advantage in their level of local knowledge” (¶1-79). The manual’s tools are the green cell, cultural advisors, and the Human Terrain System, which “conducts field research and analysis of the local population” (¶3-23).

**Exploit.** The state’s cultural knowledge is a **map of the player’s social base**. Its researchers, interpreters, and liaison officers are collection assets, whether or not they think of themselves that way. The player can feed them a version of the culture that is true but useless: emphasize the feuds that don’t matter, the festivals that don’t organize, the elders who don’t deliver. The player can also *become* the cultural broker: the person the state must ask to understand the county.

**Trap.** The state’s cultural outreach is also a **legitimacy channel**. A well-run clinic, a school, a funeral visit: these are not just intelligence; they are the state being seen to care. If the player attacks the researcher, the state gets a martyr and the population gets a reason to talk.

**OURS.** The state’s cultural knowledge should be modeled as a **coverage map**, not a truth. It can be broad and shallow (the county’s “tribes”), or narrow and deep (one family). The player can poison the broad map with true-but-irrelevant detail and protect the narrow map by keeping the state out of the places that matter.

### 8.3 Intelligence drives operations — ¶1-80–1-82

**State move.** Build a cycle in which intelligence drives operations, operations produce intelligence, and reporting flows bottom-up (¶1-81). Gather “civil information” about the population, not just insurgent combatants (¶1-82). Separate intelligence from civil information so NGOs will cooperate (¶1-82).

**Exploit.** The state’s sensor is the population, and the population has its own disputes. FM 3-24 admits that people provide inaccurate information “to settle tribal, ethnic, or business disputes” (¶8-17). The player can use the state’s own tip lines and reward programs as a **market for false information**: pay a contractor’s rival to report the contractor; let the state chase a feud that is not the movement. The state’s “un-coerced informant reports” metric (¶12-24) can be *performed*: a quiet month looks like a healthy informant network, even if the network is dead.

**Trap.** The state’s civil-information channel is a **laundering mechanism** for mapping. An NGO or a development agency may refuse to share “intelligence,” but it will share “civil information” (¶1-82). The clinic’s patient list, the school’s enrollment, the utility’s hookup list: these are the state’s map with a humanitarian face. The player who treats the NGO as neutral is the player who gets mapped.

**OURS.** The state’s intelligence cycle should be modeled as **several competing cycles**, not one. The sheriff’s PIR is not the task force’s PIR; the county board’s “civil information” is not the prosecutor’s evidence. The player’s best move is to sell each agency the answer it wants and let them argue about the truth.

### 8.4 Security under the rule of law — ¶1-83–1-84

**State move.** Transition security to host-nation forces; use legal population-control measures: curfews, movement restrictions, travel permits, registration cards, biometric census (¶1-84). The 2009 tactics manual adds the full control grid: curfews, travel permits, restricted areas, census/registration, block committees, ID cards, job licensing, immigration restrictions, food/medical/fuel controls, firearms and vehicle registration (FM 3-24.2 ¶3-169–3-170), enforced by checkpoints and roadblocks (¶3-171).

**Exploit.** The control grid is a **legibility machine**, and legibility is expensive. Every permit, ID, checkpoint, and registration is a transaction the player can capture, forge, or turn into a grievance. The player can:
- become the fixer who gets permits for the neighborhood (a legitimacy win);
- forge the ID of a dead man (a short-term win, a long-term risk);
- force the state to enforce the grid against the wrong person (a legitimacy loss);
- make the checkpoint the site of a provocation (see §12.3).

**Trap.** The grid is also a **filter**. The state does not need to catch the player at a checkpoint; it needs the checkpoint to generate a list of who passes, when, and with whom. The player who uses the same route, the same ID, or the same cousin at the checkpoint is building the state’s graph. The trap is convenience.

**OURS.** The control grid should be a **playable surface**, not a background cost. The player’s movement, money, and identity all pass through it. The state’s grid is the mirror of the player’s Book: it is a map of the population, and the player’s job is to be a blank spot on it.

### 8.5 Population security (cross-cutting) — ¶9-18, ¶9-24–9-27, ¶9-31

**State move.** The state lives among the population, provides continuous security, protects key infrastructure, and ties people to institutions (¶9-18, ¶9-24–9-27, ¶9-31). The population must believe the state will remain and the insurgents will not return (¶9-18); people who do not feel secure “do not risk overtly supporting host-nation efforts” (¶9-31). This is not a separate strategic principle; it is the manual’s test of whether the hold and build phases are real.

**Exploit.** Make the state’s protection **unequal or unreliable**. The state cannot protect everyone; resources are limited and it must prioritize “parts of the infrastructure vital for stability” (¶9-27). The player can attack a site the state promised to protect and let the state explain; force a choice between a school and a checkpoint; make protection look like a curfew; or offer a parallel security guarantee the state cannot match.

**Trap.** The state’s protection is **real where it is dense**. If the player attacks a protected neighborhood, the population may turn, and the state’s garrison becomes a shield rather than a hostage. The trap is the security dilemma: the player’s attack makes the state’s protection more valuable.

**OURS.** Model protection as **coverage versus credibility**: the state can cover a few places well or many places poorly. The player attacks the credibility of the coverage, not the coverage itself. The design question is “where does the state’s promise outrun its presence?”

### 8.6 Prepare for a long-term commitment — ¶1-85

**State move.** The state’s doctrine says COIN is protracted; the population must have confidence in the government’s “staying power” (¶1-85). The state will build institutions, train local forces, and wait.

**Exploit.** The state’s staying power is a **budget and political calendar**, not a doctrine. A county government has an election, a grant cycle, a fiscal year, and a state legislature. The player can make the state’s commitment expensive at the moments when its political cover is weakest: before an election, after a scandal, during a budget crisis. The player does not need to defeat the state; the player needs to make the state’s coalition unwilling to pay.

**Trap.** The state’s long war is designed to **outlast the player’s organization**. The manual says insurgents “can become extremely difficult to identify, track, and interdict if they are effective in acting clandestinely” (¶1-85), but also that insurgencies may persist for years. If the player bets on the state leaving, the player may be the one who exhausts first. The trap is the waiting game: the state can build while the player hides.

**OURS.** Model the state’s commitment as a **coalition of payers** (county board, state, federal grant, local donors), each with a horizon. The player’s attacks should target the *payer’s* willingness, not the state’s capacity. But the player’s own cadres also have a horizon; a long quiet period is a morale and recruitment problem.

### 8.7 Manage information and expectations — ¶1-86–1-87

**State move.** Tell the truth, refuse projections, do not promise more than can be delivered (¶1-86). Make deeds match words; every action has an information reaction (¶1-87). The state’s information operations are synchronized with operations (¶9-22, ¶9-30, ¶9-33).

**Exploit.** The state’s credibility is a **gap** between promise and delivery. The player can widen it: force the state to promise security it cannot deliver, then make a delivery fail; expose the contractor who was paid but did not build; make the state’s “success” story collide with a funeral. FM 3-24 says “actions of Soldiers and Marines … are the most powerful influence on credibility” (¶7-89); the player’s action is to make the state’s actions contradict its words.

**Trap.** The state’s listening session, town hall, or “community liaison” meeting is a **focus group** that maps the player’s support. The state’s information operation is also a collection operation: FM 3-24 says information operations should “facilitate effective information collection on the combatants” (¶9-22). The player who attends to argue is the player who is counted.

**OURS.** The state’s IO should have a **collection yield** as well as a message effect. Every town hall, survey, or liaison meeting adds to the state’s Book. The player can attend and lie, send a surrogate, or disrupt — each has a different cost. The trap is the microphone.

### 8.8 Use the appropriate level of force — ¶1-88–1-89

**State move.** Calculate force carefully; use escalation-of-force procedures; restrain soldiers because “the more force that is used, the less effective it can be” (¶1-37); “do not create more enemies than you eliminate” (¶1-89).

**Exploit.** **Provocation.** FM 3-24 says insurgents often carry out an act “with the primary purpose of enticing counterinsurgents to overreact” (¶7-7), and the 2006 edition names it: “provocation of a government response” (2006 ¶3-92). The player can force the state to choose between restraint and legitimacy. A funeral, a market, a school, a checkpoint: the player creates a situation where the state’s restraint looks weak and its force looks brutal.

**Trap.** If the state *does not* overreact, the player has spent legitimacy for nothing. FM 3-24 warns that the urban/terrorist approach may produce only “shallow support” because the population sees the state’s brutality, not the insurgency’s program (¶4-42). The trap is the disciplined state: it absorbs the provocation, isolates the player, and makes the player look reckless. The state’s own doctrine is a **restraint manual**; the player is fighting it.

**OURS.** Provocation is a **discipline contest**, not a free win. The state has a restraint posture (by agency and commander); the player has a patience/legitimacy budget. The player wins only if the state spends its restraint first. The state can also *stage* a restrained response — a surgical raid, a community meeting, a condolence payment — to deny the player the propaganda win. See §12.3.

### 8.9 Learn and adapt — ¶1-90

**State move.** The state is a learning organization: observe, draw lessons, circulate best practices, adapt as fast as the insurgents (¶1-90). The manual’s assessments chapter measures what works (¶12-1–12-24).

**Exploit.** Force the state to learn the **wrong lesson**. The player repeats a pattern long enough to be noticed, then breaks it at the moment the state has reorganized around it. The classic: let the state think the player avoids checkpoints, then hit one; let the state think the player meets on Sundays, then meet on Wednesday. The player’s advantage is that the state’s learning is *institutional* and slow; the player’s is *personal* and fast.

**Trap.** The state’s learning curve is real. Every repeated tactic becomes a pattern; every pattern becomes a target. FM 3-24’s “attack the network” doctrine is designed to find the critical node (¶4-77, ¶7-55). The player who repeats a success is building the state’s model of the player.

**OURS.** The state should have a **model of the player** that updates after every action. The player can see the model only indirectly (through the state’s raids and questions). The design question is “what does the state now believe about us?” — and the player should be able to *feed* that belief.

### 8.10 Empower the lowest levels — ¶1-91

**State move.** Push resources and decision authority down to local commanders; they have the best grasp of the situation (¶1-91). The manual’s ideal is decentralized, adaptive COIN.

**Exploit.** Local commanders have **local ties, grudges, and careers**. The sheriff’s deputy who grew up with the player’s cousin is not the same actor as the federal task force. The player can target the local commander’s reputation, leverage a family connection, or make the commander’s career depend on a false success. The state’s decentralization is a seam.

**Trap.** The local commander may be the player’s **best interlocutor**. Decentralization means the state can negotiate, trade, and make deals without the capital knowing. If the player kills the local commander, the replacement may be younger, harder, and less embedded. The trap is decapitation: it feels like a win and produces a more competent enemy.

**OURS.** The state’s local commanders should be **characters with incentives**, not generic units. Each has a PIR, a career horizon, a relationship to the county, and a threshold for violence. The player’s intelligence target is not the headquarters; it is the commander’s *next promotion*.

### 8.11 Support the host nation — ¶1-92

**State move.** The long-term goal is a host nation that can stand alone; external forces enable, but the host nation must “win on its own” (¶1-92). The state builds local institutions, local forces, and local legitimacy.

**Exploit.** The host nation is the **weak link**. Its local forces are less capable, more corrupt, and more locally rooted. The player can capture them, infiltrate them, or make them irrelevant. The manual’s own transition logic says the host nation must be seen as legitimate (¶1-83); the player can make the local partner’s corruption the story.

**Trap.** The local partner is also the **population’s cousin**. If the player attacks the local police, the player attacks the neighbor, not the occupier. The population’s loyalty is the prize, and the local force is the institution through which the state hopes to win it. The trap is the easy target: the local force is weaker, but hitting it costs more legitimacy than hitting the federal task force.

**OURS.** The “host nation” layer should be the **county government and its officers**, not a foreign nation. The player’s choice is to capture, infiltrate, or destroy the county’s institutions. Each choice changes who the population blames when services fail.

## 9. The phases and their imperatives

FM 3-24’s direct approach is **shape–clear–hold–build–transition** (¶9-7). The phases are not linear; areas can reverse (¶9-10), and the state should not clear an area it cannot hold (¶9-23). The five imperatives (¶9-11) are a compact statement of the state’s theory:

1. Conditions are assessed across several variables and cannot be taken in isolation. → **Exploit:** the state’s assessment is a map; feed it a false picture in one variable and let the others look consistent.
2. The phase dictates the decisive operation, priorities, and resourcing. → **Exploit:** the phase is predictable; attack the *transition* between phases, when the state is changing its mix.
3. In clear, hold, and build, the state must simultaneously conduct offense, defense, and stability. → **Exploit:** the state is stretched; force it to choose between securing a site and securing a route.
4. Each area requires its own assessment, and assessments change. → **Exploit:** the state’s local knowledge is uneven; find the area it misreads.
5. The framework is not a straightforward path; the state can lose progress. → **Exploit:** the state’s gains are reversible; make it overextend, then re-enter.

| Phase | Doctrine | State move | Exploit | Trap |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Shape** | ¶9-14–9-16 | Census, biometrics, key-leader engagement, information operations, deception; “initial census … is the most effective tool to establish initial accountability for everyone” (¶9-15) | Treat the census/project as a map; contest, capture, or shadow it; feed the census a false household | The project’s benefits are real; attacking a clinic or well turns the population |
| **Clear** | ¶9-17–9-22 | Remove open insurgent presence; raids, cordons, searches; secure access and key points | The clear is your recruitment; make it overreach; force it to clear more than it can hold (¶9-23) | The state benefits if you defend a position you cannot hold; the clear can be bait |
| **Hold** | ¶9-23–9-30 | Bases among the population; continuous contact; protect infrastructure; build local forces | Attack the hold’s supply and legitimacy, not its armor; make the base a hostage | The base may be in a school or clinic; attacking it costs you the population |
| **Build** | ¶9-31–9-33 | Tie inhabitants to institutions; services, governance, rule of law; the capacity-building line of effort is decisive | Offer a better/parallel service; capture the contractor; make the state’s delivery fail | The build’s jobs and contracts are a loyalty test; taking them makes you dependent |
| **Transition** | ¶9-34–9-37 | Handover to local forces; multiple transitions; reduce the external footprint | The handover is a seam; infiltrate/capture the local force; exploit the competence gap | The local force is the population’s cousin; hitting it costs more than hitting the occupier |

**OURS.** The phase table should be the state’s **AI script**, not a player menu. The player reads the phase from the state’s behavior and chooses the exploit. The most interesting phase is **transition**, because it is where the state’s legitimacy claim is most fragile and the player’s local roots are most valuable.

## 10. The indirect method: identify, separate, isolate, influence, reintegrate

FM 3-24’s indirect method is the state’s attempt to “divide and conquer the insurgency” (¶10-28). It is the most directly relevant doctrine to the player’s splintering threat.

| Step | Doctrine | State move | Exploit | Trap |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Identify** | ¶10-28–10-30 | Biometrics, census, tip lines, civil records, rewards, forensics, document exploitation; “mapping the human environment and patterns of life” (¶10-29) | Feed the identity system a false identity; capture the enrollment clerk; make the state’s list wrong | The list is also a **protection** system for the innocent; attacking it can unite the population |
| **Separate** | ¶10-31–10-34 | Bases among the population, cordons, checkpoints, “security bubbles,” combat outposts | Make the separation expensive; attack the cordon’s logistics; use the “bubble” as a recruiting ground | The state’s base can be a **hostage**; it can fortify a school and dare you |
| **Isolate** | ¶10-35–10-40 | Cut the insurgency’s links to the population, funding, and communications; counter-threat finance; information operations | Disperse; use dual-use channels; make the state’s economic isolation hurt the neutral population (¶10-39) | Over-broad isolation strengthens the insurgency; the state may do it for you and blame you |
| **Influence** | ¶10-41–10-45 | Amnesty, reintegration, defectors, information operations; “rifts … can be exploited”; “offering amnesty or a seemingly generous compromise can also cause divisions” (¶10-42) | Send a double; let a tired cadre accept and keep the network; use the state’s offer to identify who wants out | The offer can **split** you; the individual offer is the test |
| **Reintegrate** | ¶10-46–10-53 | Jobs, education, protection from reprisal, “generous surrender terms” (¶10-53); “protect and empower moderate insurgents from extremist influence” (¶10-47) | The reintegration program is a **recruitment pool**; the state’s vetting is shallow; “opportunists can infiltrate local civilian security forces” (¶5-36) | The program gives the state a **legal** channel into your base; the people who accept are your neighbors |

**OURS.** The indirect method is the state’s **counter-recruitment** program. It should be modeled as a set of offers, not a single “amnesty” button. Each offer has a target, a price, and a protection guarantee. The player’s response is a **cohesion** decision: forbid, allow, or send a double.

## 11. Money, targeting, and the network

### 11.1 Money

FM 3-24 calls it “integrated monetary shaping operations”: the coordinated use of money, goods, or services to support the commander’s objectives (¶10-56). The manual is explicit that money creates **winners and losers**: decisions about “which contractors, communities, and individuals are chosen to receive funding could create winners and losers” and “exacerbate tensions” (¶10-57). Haphazard money can be “highly destructive” and create an unsustainable economy (¶10-58). Commanders “do not invest money in an intelligence vacuum” (¶10-68); property records, census records, and deeds help the state “determine who should or should not be living in a specific area” (¶10-59).

The phrase “money as a weapons system” comes from CALL Handbook 09-27 (2009), not FM 3-24. The handbook treats money as a weapon that can “defeat COIN targets without creating collateral damage” (foreword), funds CERP projects (ch. 4), pays rewards for information (ch. 6), and provides intelligence contingency funds (ch. 7). Its rewards chapter includes a revealing firewall: company commanders “must ensure they do not develop an ongoing working relationship with local nationals through any level of the DOD Rewards Program” (ch. 6). The program is built to keep the source at arm’s length; the player wants the handler.

**Exploit.** Price the project. Every grant, contract, condolence payment, and payroll creates a winner and a loser. The player can organize the losers, expose the winner, tax the project, or force it to hire the player’s people. The player can also **counterfeit the receipt**: make the state’s money look like the player’s patronage.

**Trap.** The state’s money is a **ledger**. It identifies who took it, who benefited, and who is owed. If the player takes the money, the player is on the list. The state’s audit (¶10-71) is not just accounting; it is intelligence.

**OURS.** Money should be a **two-sided flow**: the state’s project creates a visible beneficiary list; the player’s extortion/taxation creates a visible dependency. The player’s choice is not “take the money or not” but “who is seen taking it.”

### 11.2 Targeted threat infrastructure and counter-finance

FM 3-24’s “targeted threat infrastructure” attacks “the physical and societal mechanisms that support and fuel the insurgency” (¶9-44). It is only effective if another force is securing the population and building legitimacy (¶9-44). Counter-threat finance fusion centers find, interdict, and seize illicit funds (¶9-49).

**Exploit.** Disperse and disguise. The state targets the physical and societal mechanisms; the player’s logistics should be indistinguishable from the civilian economy. Many small caches, not one big one; informal receipts, not ledgers; dual-use vehicles, not a fleet. The state’s own doctrine warns that crop eradication can hurt legitimacy (¶9-46); the player can make the state’s targeting look like collective punishment.

**Trap.** The state’s targeting is an **information operation**. A seized cache is displayed to prove the player’s weakness; a “cleared canal” may be a raid. The trap is the project that is actually a map: the state clears the canal, counts the houses, and photographs the bridges.

**OURS.** The player’s logistics should be a **hidden network** with visible civilian cover. Every seizure should cost the state legitimacy if the cover was civilian. The player’s counter to counter-finance is not better hiding; it is **making the state’s seizure look like theft**.

### 11.3 Attack the network

FM 3-24’s “attack the network” approach has three activities: “Support friendly networks. Influence neutral networks. Neutralize threat networks” (¶7-55). It maps friendly, neutral, and threat networks (¶7-51), and targets critical nodes (¶4-77). The state’s social-network analysis identifies leadership, autonomy, and hierarchy (¶7-53).

**Exploit.** Design for decapitation. Cells, redundancy, no single charismatic node (¶4-49: organizations dependent on a charismatic leader are vulnerable if the leader is co-opted or killed). Feed the state a **false node**: a rival, a front, a dead end. The player’s network should be a set of small, replaceable pieces, not a pyramid.

**Trap.** The state’s “support friendly networks” and “influence neutral networks” are the real threat. The state is not just killing the player’s nodes; it is building a **rival civic network** around the player. If the player only defends its own nodes, it loses the neutral middle: the church, the union, the sports club, the school board.

**OURS.** The player’s network should be scored on **redundancy** and **neutral ties**, not just secrecy. The state’s attack-the-network campaign should target the player’s *relationships to neutral institutions*, not only its cadre. The player’s counter is to make the neutral institutions useful to the player and dangerous for the state to attack.

## 12. Four deep dives

### 12.1 The project is the map

**Doctrine.** The state’s shaping phase includes “an initial census in towns or cities using biometrics data collection devices” as “the most effective tool to establish initial accountability for everyone” (¶9-15). Identity activities include “population census using biometric data collection devices and mapping the human environment and patterns of life” (¶10-29). Property records, census records, and deeds help the state “determine who should or should not be living in a specific area” (¶10-59). The Human Terrain System “conducts field research and analysis of the local population” (¶3-23). Civil information is gathered outside the intelligence lane so NGOs will cooperate (¶1-82). Biometrics “deny threat forces any freedom of movement within the population” and “positively identify” people (¶8-23).

**The inference.** The manual does not say “development projects are intelligence collection.” It says the state gathers civil information, maps the population, and uses property records and census data to secure the population; it also says commanders “do not invest money in an intelligence vacuum” (¶10-68). The design inference — **the project is the map** — is a synthesis of those passages, not a quotation. Mark it OURS.

**Exploit.** The player has three responses to a project (a clinic, a well, a road, a school, a jobs program):
- **Contest.** Attack or block it. Cost: legitimacy, state information operations, the population’s anger if the service is real. Benefit: denies the state’s legibility gain.
- **Capture.** Let it happen, but control the contractor, the labor, the beneficiary list, or the site. Cost: dependency, corruption, the state’s audit. Benefit: resources, patronage, intelligence on the state’s priorities.
- **Shadow.** Build a parallel service nearby. Cost: cadre, money, time. Benefit: legitimacy, an independent base, no legibility to the state.

The best move is usually **capture or shadow**, not contest. Contesting a real clinic is a gift to the state. Capturing it makes the state’s project deliver the player’s legitimacy. Shadowing it makes the state’s project redundant.

**Trap.** The project’s benefits are **real**. The state knows the player’s dilemma: if the player attacks, the state wins the population; if the player allows, the state maps the population. The trap is the *binary*: the player thinks the choice is “attack or allow,” but the actual choice is “who controls the delivery.”

**OURS.** Every development project should have two yields: a **service yield** (health, water, jobs) and a **legibility yield** (names, addresses, biometrics, property records, contractor lists). The player can increase the first and decrease the second by capturing the project, or decrease both by contesting it. The design question is “who is the project for?” — not “is it a trick?”

### 12.2 The offer is the split

**Doctrine.** Amnesty and reintegration give insurgents “the means to quit” (¶10-42). “Rifts between insurgent leaders, if identified, can be exploited.” “Offering amnesty or a seemingly generous compromise can also cause divisions within an insurgency and present opportunities to split or weaken it. Counterinsurgents can also act to magnify existing rifts” (¶10-42). Amnesty programs must convince insurgents they will be “treated well and protected from their former comrades’ potential reprisal” (¶10-43), and “modest monetary rewards” are important. Former insurgents can provide information and become allies, but their information “must be evaluated and validated to prevent misinformation and deception” (¶10-44). Reintegration should “protect and empower moderate insurgents from extremist influence” (¶10-47). Counterinsurgents should offer “generous terms” and “protect surrendered insurgents from reprisals” (¶10-53).

**The honest note.** This is the closest the 2014 manual comes to “reconcilables vs irreconcilables,” but it does not use the pair. It uses “moderate insurgents” and “rifts.” The 2006 edition uses “irreconcilable” once, in a commander’s vignette: Maj. Gen. Mattis classified provincial constituents into tribes, former regime elements, and foreign fighters; “fence-sitters” were a winnable constituency; the “irreconcilable former regime elements and foreign fighters” were neutralized; the “hard-core extremists, who would never give up,” were destroyed (2006, p. 4-7). That is a **practice**, not a doctrinal category.

**The mechanic.** The state maintains a **classification ledger** for each faction: *moderate* (can be offered a place), *fence-sitter* (can be won), *irreconcilable* (must be destroyed). The ledger is not a description; it is a **weapon**. The state can move a faction from “moderate” to “irreconcilable” by accusing it of an atrocity, or from “irreconcilable” to “moderate” by offering it a deal. The player can influence the ledger:
- by what the faction does (violence against civilians moves it toward “irreconcilable”);
- by who it talks to (contact with the state moves it toward “moderate”);
- by what it refuses (refusing an offer can be used to prove it is “irreconcilable”);
- by rumor (the state’s intelligence is not omniscient; the player can feed the ledger).

**The splintering sequence.** The state makes an individual offer to a respected commander. The commander faces a choice: accept (and be protected from reprisal), refuse (and be hunted), or ask the organization. The organization faces a choice: forbid (and risk losing the commander), allow (and lose a cadre but gain a potential sleeper), or send a double (and risk detection). Whichever it chooses, the organization is now divided between those who would accept and those who would not. The state’s offer has made the internal contradiction **visible**.

**OURS.** This is the game’s **split engine**. The state’s offer should be targeted at the player’s *most respected* cadre, not the weakest. The player should be able to:
- **preempt** (purge or promote the cadre before the offer),
- **negotiate** (ask for a better offer, which proves the player can be talked to),
- **double** (send someone who accepts but reports back),
- **refuse** (and pay the cohesion cost).

The design question is not “will you accept amnesty?” but “who do you let leave, and what does that say about who you are?”

### 12.3 The provocation is a discipline contest

**Doctrine.** Insurgents often act “with the primary purpose of enticing counterinsurgents to overreact” (¶7-7). The 2006 edition defines “encouraging overreaction” as “enticing counterinsurgents to use repressive tactics that alienate the populace and bring scrutiny upon the government. It is also referred to as provocation of a government response” (2006 ¶3-92). The urban/terrorist approach attacks symbolic targets “to cause government forces to overreact against the population” (¶4-42). The state’s counter-doctrine is restraint: “do not create more enemies than you eliminate” (¶1-89); “the more force that is used, the less effective it can be” (¶1-37).

**The mechanic.** Provocation is a **two-sided gamble**:
- The player chooses a site and an act: a funeral, a market, a school, a checkpoint, a symbolic building.
- The state’s local commander/agency has a **restraint** posture. If the state holds, the player loses legitimacy/support (the population sees the player as reckless). If the state breaks, the state gains heat/repression and the player gains recruits/legitimacy.
- The player can repeat, but each repetition raises the state’s learning and the population’s suspicion.

**The trap.** The state can **stage restraint**. A surgical raid, a community meeting, a condolence payment, a quick trial: these deny the player the propaganda win. The state’s information operations (¶1-86–1-87; ¶9-22) are designed to make the state’s restraint visible. The player who expects every provocation to produce a massacre is playing the state’s game at its best.

**OURS.** Provocation should be a **contest of discipline**, not a damage button. The state has a restraint stat by agency and commander; the player has a patience/legitimacy budget. The player wins only if the state spends its restraint first. The state’s counter is to make the player’s provocation look like the real violence.

### 12.4 The informant is a channel

**Doctrine.** The 2006 manual lists “informants within the insurgency” as a vulnerability (¶1-95) and says nothing is more demoralizing than discovering that people inside the movement are providing information (¶1-104). Counterinsurgents attract informants with fear of prosecution or rewards, but must protect them and their families (¶1-104). The 2014 manual treats “a high number of un-coerced informant reports” as a possible indicator of a breakdown in insurgent security (¶12-24). Sources must be protected (¶8-16), and people lie for their own disputes (¶8-17). In FM 3-24.2, the state’s countermeasures note that because insurgents are indigenous, their networks “can be infiltrated to gather intelligence and turn insurgent operatives into double agents” (Table 2-1, p. 2-25).

**The mechanic.** The player’s network is a graph. The state’s informant is an **edge**, not a node. The player has four responses:
- **Burn.** Remove the person. Cost: lost capability, fear, the risk of punishing an innocent.
- **Feed.** Use the channel to send disinformation. Cost: confirms the channel’s value to the state; risks the network.
- **Turn.** Try to double the informant. Cost: time, risk; the informant may be a state double.
- **Ignore.** Keep using the channel. Cost: the state learns.

**The trap.** The state may **let the channel work**. It does not need to arrest the courier; it needs the courier to lead to the next node. FM 3-24’s database of insurgent actions and personnel (¶8-8) and site exploitation of phones and computers (¶8-28) are the state’s map. The player who assumes “the channel is compromised, so burn it” may be wrong; the channel may be *monitored*, and burning it tells the state the player knows. The deeper trap is the **Malinovsky/Azef paradox**: the state’s best informant is the one the player trusts most, and the player’s suspicion is itself a weapon the state can use to split the organization.

**OURS.** The informant should be a **channel with a direction**, not a binary “loyal/traitor” tag. The player can use the channel, but every use teaches the state something. The state can use the channel, but every use risks exposing the informant. The design question is “what do you want the state to believe?” — not “who is the spy?”

## 13. The reconciliation problem

The player’s splintering threat is structural, not a single betrayal. The sequence below is a **design reading** of the doctrine, not a doctrinal procedure; the manual supplies the moves, not the order.

1. **The state classifies.** It decides who is moderate, who is a fence-sitter, who is irreconcilable (¶10-47; 2006 p. 4-7). The classification is a **target list** and a **negotiation list** at the same time.
2. **The state offers.** Amnesty, reintegration, jobs, protection (¶10-42–10-53). The offer is individual, not collective; it gives a person a way out and a reason to distrust the organization’s refusal.
3. **The state magnifies rifts.** “Counterinsurgents can also act to magnify existing rifts” (¶10-42). The state’s intelligence cycle is the machine that finds the fault line and presses it.
4. **The state validates defectors.** Former insurgents can provide information, but the state must “evaluate and validate” it to prevent deception (¶10-44). The player can exploit the validation gap: a defector who is *too* useful is suspect, but a defector who is plausible can feed the state a false picture.
5. **The state protects the defector.** Amnesty works only if the defector believes they will be “treated well and protected from their former comrades’ potential reprisal” (¶10-43). The player’s threat of reprisal is the state’s best recruiting tool; the player’s promise of safety is the counter.

**The design reading.** The player should not have a single “loyalty” stat. The player should have a **settlement ledger**: for each faction and each leader, the state’s current classification, the state’s offer, the person’s fear, and the person’s ambition. The player’s actions move the ledger; the state’s offers move it too. The splinter is not a random event; it is the visible result of the state’s offer plus the player’s internal contradiction.

**The hard question.** If the player forbids all contact with the state, the player loses the ability to negotiate and looks like the irreconcilable the state says they are. If the player allows contact, the state gets a channel and the organization gets a faction that can leave. The design should make **both** choices cost something, and the cost should be legible before the player chooses.

## 14. What the manual does not cover

FM 3-24 is written for an external intervener supporting a host nation (¶1-1–1-3, ¶1-92). The game’s domestic state has tools the manual barely mentions:

- **Legal process.** Subpoenas, grand juries, warrants, prosecutions, forfeiture. The manual’s “rule of law” is about host-nation courts (¶1-83–1-84); a domestic state can use its own courts as a collection and coercion system. The player’s “legal” defense is not a right; it is a resource.
- **Federalism.** The county, state, and federal agencies have different jurisdictions, budgets, and politics. The manual’s coordination doctrine (¶1-52–1-56) is a foreign-intervener problem; the domestic version is a **turf war**.
- **Elections.** The sheriff, prosecutor, and county board are elected. The manual’s “legitimacy” is a population perception; the domestic version is also a **turnout** and a **donor** problem.
- **Media and the national audience.** The manual’s information operations assume a foreign audience and an international coalition (¶1-86–1-87); a domestic incident can become national in hours. The player’s provocation can be a national story, which is both a risk and a lever.
- **The population as the polity.** In a domestic setting, the population is not a foreign “host nation”; it is the electorate, the jury pool, and the taxpayer. Coercion costs more legitimacy because the coerced are the state’s own citizens.

**OURS.** The game should not import the manual’s “host nation” assumptions wholesale. The domestic state’s intelligence cycle is *more* legalistic, *more* fragmented, and *more* media-sensitive than FM 3-24’s. That is the design space.

## 15. Design readings (OURS)

These are proposals, not implemented mechanics. Each is labeled with the decision it creates.

1. **The Standing Asks (3 slots).** *Decision:* what will you do differently if you know? *State learns:* what you are asking, if the source talks. *Failure:* over-asking burns the network. Mirrors PIRs (ADP 2-0 ¶4-24; ATP 2-01.3 ¶1-28, ¶1-44).
2. **The County Book (four ledgers; coverage/confidence/freshness/exposure).** *Decision:* what can you afford to write down, and who can afford to lose it? *State learns:* the nodes on any captured page. *Failure:* centralization or rumor. Mirrors IPOE/IPB (ADP 2-0 §5-8; ATP 2-01.3 ¶1-13, ¶1-46).
3. **The Ear (reliability/risk/reach; press or rest).** *Decision:* do you spend the relationship now or save it? *State learns:* who is asking. *Failure:* the Ear is a state informant or is seen. Mirrors HUMINT/source protection (FM 3-24 ¶8-15–8-17).
4. **The Tell (state COA indicators).** *Decision:* which branch do you pre-commit? *State learns:* whether you are reacting to its logistics. *Failure:* mirror-imaging. Mirrors the event template (ATP 2-01.3 ¶6-65).
5. **The Burn List (voluntary capability loss).** *Decision:* what do you destroy before the state finds it? *State learns:* nothing, if the burn is complete. *Failure:* keeping the useful phone. Mirrors site exploitation (FM 3-24 ¶8-28) and the feedback step (ADP 2-0 ¶4-22–4-25).
6. **The Project (service yield vs legibility yield; contest/capture/shadow).** *Decision:* who controls the delivery? *State learns:* the beneficiary list, the census, the property records. *Failure:* contesting a real service. Mirrors shaping/identity activities (FM 3-24 ¶9-15, ¶10-29, ¶10-59).
7. **The Offer (state classification ledger; preempt/negotiate/double/refuse).** *Decision:* who do you let leave, and what does it say about who you are? *State learns:* your rifts. *Failure:* the split becomes public before you choose. Mirrors amnesty/reintegration (FM 3-24 ¶10-42–10-53).
8. **The Provocation (discipline contest).** *Decision:* do you spend legitimacy to make the state spend restraint? *State learns:* your willingness to hurt the neutral. *Failure:* the state stages restraint. Mirrors overreaction/provocation (FM 3-24 ¶4-42, ¶7-7; 2006 ¶3-92).
9. **The Informant (channel with direction).** *Decision:* do you burn, feed, turn, or ignore? *State learns:* the network, if you keep using it. *Failure:* burning a monitored channel. Mirrors informants/double agents (2006 ¶1-95, ¶1-104; FM 3-24 ¶12-24; FM 3-24.2 Table 2-1).
10. **The Control Grid (permits, IDs, curfews, checkpoints).** *Decision:* which transaction do you capture, forge, or refuse? *State learns:* who passes, when, with whom. *Failure:* convenience. Mirrors populace/resource control (FM 3-24.2 ¶3-169–3-171; FM 3-24 ¶1-84).

**The three that carry the most design weight:** the **Standing Asks**, the **County Book as a seizable asset**, and the **Offer/Split ledger**. The first turns PIRs into a relationship budget; the second turns IPB into a risk-allocation problem; the third turns amnesty into the game’s splintering engine.

## 17. Uncertainties and open questions

1. **The 2026 ADP 2-0 is three weeks old.** It supersedes the 2019 edition and changes the intelligence process from four steps to six. Future editions may renumber; the quotations are from the PDF read on 2026-10-25.
2. **The seven-step cycle is not current Army doctrine.** It is a teaching model that splits ADP 2-0’s merged steps. If the game’s tooltips say “the Army intelligence cycle has seven steps,” they are wrong about the current manual. Say “the classic seven-step teaching model” or “our seven verbs.”
3. **“Reconcilables vs irreconcilables” is not a 2014 doctrinal pair.** The 2014 manual uses “moderate insurgents” and “rifts”; the 2006 manual uses “irreconcilable” once in a commander’s vignette. The game’s mechanic is an inference. Do not put the pair in the state’s mouth as a quotation.
4. **“Money as a weapons system” is CALL 09-27, not FM 3-24.** FM 3-24’s term is “integrated monetary shaping operations.” If the state’s dialogue uses “money as a weapons system,” it is quoting the handbook, not the field manual.
5. **The domestic translation is a fiction.** FM 3-24 is an external-intervener manual. The game’s county/state/federal setting changes the legal, electoral, and media constraints. The translation is a design choice, not a doctrinal claim.
6. **The PIR definition is flagged.** The FM 2-0 (2023) excerpt gives the definition but not the paragraph number. The ADP 2-0 (2026) citation (¶4-24) is secure for PIRs as the intelligence component of CCIRs; the formal definition should be re-checked against FM 2-0 when a full copy is available.
7. **The current ATP 2-01.3 is Change 3 (May 2025); I read Change 1 (March 2019).** The four-step process, NAI/TAI, and event-template definitions are stable across the editions I could compare, but a future citation should use C3 if the exact wording matters.
8. **The “project is the map” inference is not a quotation.** FM 3-24 says the state gathers civil information, maps the population, uses census/property records, and does not invest money in an intelligence vacuum. It does not say “development projects are intelligence collection.” The design reading is OURS.
9. **The “state’s classification ledger” is not a doctrine.** The manual describes amnesty, rifts, and “moderate insurgents”; it does not describe a formal ledger. The mechanic is OURS.
10. **The manual’s “population security” phrase.** The 2014 edition uses “securing the population” and “security under the rule of law,” not the exact phrase “population security.” The concept is present; the phrase is not.
