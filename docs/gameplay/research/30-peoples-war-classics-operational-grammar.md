# 30 — People's War Classics: the operational grammar

Read-only briefing, 2026-10-25. Question: **what is the actual operational grammar of people's war —
unit scales, stage transitions, preconditions, equipment flow, leadership presence, and the planning of
the insurrection itself — and where do the classics contradict each other badly enough to become
selectable doctrines rather than one correct line?**

**Scope exception, recorded deliberately.** This briefing reads Trotsky's *How the Revolution Armed*
(military volumes only: Vol. I 1918 and Vol. V 1921–23, plus one 1919 chapter). That is a scoped exception
to the standing no-Trotsky rule in `research/16`; nothing here imports Trotsky's political line, and the
material is used only for two mechanics the corpus otherwise lacks: **co-optation of the old officer corps**
and **doctrine-as-art**. The exception should not be generalised to other research.

---

## 0. Thesis

1. **The classics agree on a ladder and disagree on its risers.** Every text builds the same ascending scale
   — firing group → squad/section → company → battalion → regiment → division — but each one names a
   *different* gate for the next rung: territory (Mao), mass (Neuberg), enemy cadres (Trotsky), tempo
   (Marighella), or will (RAF).
2. **Logistics is the real stage clock.** Giáp's army grows *because* it arms itself from the enemy
   ("the sole source of supply could only be the battlefront"); Mao says the same in one sentence — "The
   enemy is the principal source of their supply." A force that cannot convert enemy materiel into its own
   order of battle cannot climb, no matter how good its politics.
3. **The insurrection is a planned object, not a mood.** Neuberg's fourteen-element town plan and eight
   data requirements are the most gameable artifact in the whole corpus, and they are explicitly
   *front-loaded*: detailed for the first phase only.
4. **Leadership presence is a modelled quantity, not flavour.** Trotsky's commissar is a co-signature on
   command; his train is a mobile command post; Neuberg synchronises watches five minutes from the objective;
   Giáp's "each Party cell a staff" pushes staff function down to the village. Presence has a radius and a latency.
5. **The contradictions are the design.** "Losses must be avoided even at the cost of losing ground" (Giáp)
   and "The defensive is the death of every armed rising" (Neuberg) cannot both be true in one combat model —
   so they should not be. They are two curves, not two opinions.

# Part I — Per-text mechanics

## 1.1 Giáp: the stage ladder is a logistics ladder

**The three stages are named, but the mechanism is accumulation, not escalation.**

> "in principle, starting from a stage of contention, it goes through a period of equilibrium before
> arriving at a general counter-offensive."
> — *People's War, People's Army*, "The Vietnamese people's war of liberation…", p. 11

> "it was necessary to accumulate thousands of small victories to turn them into a great success, thus
> gradually altering the balance of forces, in transforming our weakness into power."
> — ibid., p. 11

Note what "small victory" means operationally: not a battle won, but a **ratio change**. The stage variable
is the exchange ratio plus the force ceiling, and the counter-offensive is only permitted when the ratio has
crossed. Giáp is explicit that the crossing cannot be jumped:

> "in their plan of general counter-offensive put forth in 1950 when this was not yet permitted by objective
> and subjective conditions."
> — "The great experiences gained by our Party…", §3, p. 50 (listed under erroneous tendencies)

So: **the counter-offensive is a gated action, and attempting it early is a modelled error, not a risky
gamble.** The 1950 attempt is named as subjectivism, alongside defeatism — the two failure poles.

**The scale ladder, in Giáp's own ascending sentence:**

> "our people's army constantly grew and passed from the stage of combats involving a section or company, to
> fairly large-scale campaigns bringing into action several divisions. Gradually, its equipment improved,
> mainly by the seizure of arms from the enemy."
> — "The Vietnamese people's war of liberation…", p. 12

and the finer version:

> "Starting from small operations with the strength of a platoon or a company to annihilate a few men or a
> group of enemy soldiers, our army went over, later, to more important combats with a battalion or regiment
> to cut one or several enemy companies to pieces, finally coming to greater campaigns bringing into play many
> regiments, then many divisions."
> — "People's War, People's Army", p. 23

**Three-tier force structure is a precondition, not a product:**

> "From the first days, there appeared three types of armed forces: para-military organisations or guerrilla
> units, regional troops and regular units."
> — ibid., p. 24

> "The regular forces were, at a given time, compelled to split up into companies operating separately to
> promote the extension of guerrilla activities while mobile battalions were maintained for more important
> actions."
> — ibid., p. 24

That last sentence is the whole mechanic of **dispersal-for-recruitment / concentration-for-decision**:
the same force can be either a seeding mechanism or a striking mechanism, never both at once, and the split
is a player decision.

**Equipment flow is one-directional by necessity:**

> "Importation was impossible, the neighbouring countries being hostile… The sole source of supply could only
> be the battlefront: to take the material from the enemy to turn it against him."
> — ibid., p. 24

> "A great part of our military materials came from war-booty." — ibid., p. 24

Consequence for design: **capture is not a bonus, it is the production function.** Industry ("arms factories
set up later on with makeshift means were far from being able to meet all our needs", ibid.) is a supplement
with a hard ceiling.

**Population as infrastructure:**

> "Each inhabitant was a soldier, each village a fortress, each Party cell, each village administrative
> committee a staff." — ibid., p. 23

> "each person was a soldier, each village a fortress, each Party branch and Resistance committee a staff. It
> was so in the free zones and all the more so in the enemy-occupied zones." — ibid., p. 48

Two things to notice. First, the staff function is **distributed to the village**, which is why Giáp's
command latency is low despite poor communications. Second, the slogan is asserted for *occupied* zones more
strongly than free ones — the fortress is a cellular property of settlement, not a walls-and-bunkers object.

**Loss policy is explicit and unusual:**

> "it proves absolutely necessary not to lose sight of the main objective of the fighting that is the
> destruction of the enemy manpower. Therefore losses must be avoided even at the cost of losing ground. And
> that for the purpose of recovering, later on, the occupied territories."
> — ibid., p. 23

Ground is a **loan to the enemy**, repayable later. This is the exact inverse of a territorial victory-point
model, and it is the sentence that makes Giáp incompatible with Neuberg's offensive imperative.

**Self-reliance as a rate, not a slogan:** "to build up our strength during the actual course of fighting"
(p. 12); "we have mainly to rely on our own forces" (First Session CC, 1951, quoted p. 50).

**What Giáp does not give you.** No street-fighting technique, no urban plan, no unit TO&E, no insurrection
timing rule. His city phase is a single clause — "after fierce street-combats in the big cities, [the army]
beat strategic retreats to the countryside on its own initiative" (p. 22) — which is a **stage-zero exit**,
not a mechanic.

## 1.2 Neuberg: the insurrection as an engineered object

*Armed Insurrection* is the only text in the set that is a **manual**, and it is a manual for one operation:
the seizure of a city. Its value is that it states thresholds numerically and then reports what happened when
they were missed.

**The art claim, with its source:**

> "'Insurrection is an art just as war is, and like the other arts it is subject to certain rules; if these
> are forgotten, the consequence will be defeat for the party which has failed to observe them.'"
> — Wollenberg, "How we wrote *Armed Insurrection*", p. 23 (quoting the classic formula; preface, not Neuberg's text)

> "It is impossible to act with a vanguard alone, and … any intervention by such a vanguard which does not
> enjoy the active support of a majority of the working class is doomed to failure."
> — ibid., p. 51 (on Reval)

That second sentence is the **mass gate**, and it is the one RAF later denies.

**Unit scales are built from the bottom in secrecy, then fused at the last minute.** Reval:

> "Combat squads — at first in the form of groups of three … subsequently in the form of groups of ten —
> began to be created in the spring. A week before the Reval uprising, the groups of ten were fused into
> companies, and then the latter into battalions of 120–150 men apiece."
> — ch. 3, *The Reval Uprising*, p. 64

Hamburg's structure is the most explicit TO&E in the corpus:

> "at the base, groups of eight men with a commander; four such groups formed a squad, and four squads formed
> a section … Each section had attached to it a number of cyclists and motorcyclists, several nurses (from the
> Workers' Aid Association) and some scouts, mainly women."
> — ch. 4, *The Hamburg Uprising*, p. 89

Canton: "isolated and carefully camouflaged ten-man groups, without any larger units" until two weeks before
the rising (ch. 5, p. 115). Shanghai III: "a commander for every twenty or thirty men" (ch. 6, p. 144).

The pattern: **the cell is 3–10; the fused company is 80–150; nothing above battalion exists before D-day.**
Fusion is itself a risk event — Reval fused a week out, and the commanders had never worked together.

**Subversion of the enemy's army is a precondition with a number.** Reval's insurgents assumed a garrison of
766–830 hostile effectives (junker academy, NCO college, mounted police) would not fight, because cells
existed in every unit; they were wrong, and the text names why:

> "the Party's influence in the army had weakened … The old soldiers, their term of service over, had been
> demobilised and with them the most revolutionary elements had departed."
> — ch. 3, p. 65

> "It was naive to think that the men of the 10th Regiment, without communist soldiers, would actively join
> the insurgents at the behest of nine unknown workers."
> — ch. 3, p. 74

**Partial demands are the subversion interface.** Neuberg reproduces the Comintern's list — pay, food, abolition
of saluting and corporal punishment, right to marry, right to wear mufti, right to vote, service in home
district, native language in drill (ch. 7, pp. 157–159). This is the actual mechanic: subversion runs on
*grievance*, not ideology, and the grievances are concrete and enumerable.

**Surprise has three preconditions, stated as a checklist:**

> "surprise operations … can only succeed given certain preconditions: (a) careful reconnaissance of the
> targets to be seized; (b) an extremely detailed plan of action, and perfect coordination (with respect to
> the timing and allocation of missions) between the various units or individuals taking part in the attack;
> (c) support for the detachment or detachments at the appropriate moment from the mass of workers."
> — ch. 10, *The Beginning of the Insurrection*, p. 207

**Timing is physical.** Hamburg checked and synchronised watches immediately before the assault; assembly
points were chosen to be exactly five minutes from the police stations; H-hour 05:00 (p. 210). Reval failed
one objective because the upper-floor group arrived "a minute or two later" than the ground-floor group (p. 210).

**The plan, as fourteen numbered requirements** (ch. 10, pp. 221–222) — this is the design gold:

> (a) evaluation of the balance of forces in the town; (b) date; (c) main objectives that *must* be taken;
> (d) secondary objectives for phase two; (e) deployment with maximum manpower on main targets; (f) likely
> follow-on tasks after first missions; (g) course of action if a unit fails; (h) measures against relief
> troops from outside; (i) measures to bring the mass of workers in and distribute arms; (j) elimination of
> counter-revolutionary leaders; (k) provision for forming regular Red Army units *during* the fighting;
> (l) liaison organisation; (m) location of the commander and Revolutionary Committee at start; (n) political
> measures the Revolutionary Committee enacts.

And the plan rests on eight datasets (p. 222): social map of districts (friendly / mobilisable / hostile to be
destroyed); police deployment and degree of disaffection; addresses of officials and hostile leaders; arms
depots and their guards; tactical evaluation of buildings and blocks; vehicles to be requisitioned; technical
data for transport/telephone use; the insurgents' own personnel and commanders.

Note (m): **the plan must contain the location of its own command.** Neuberg is treating command presence as
a map object.

And the plan is explicitly **front-loaded**:

> "The tactical plan … must be as detailed as possible, and concern itself solely with the first phase of the
> insurrection … As far as the subsequent stages are concerned, the leadership can and must give instructions
> of a general kind in good time."
> — ch. 10, p. 220–221

**Street fighting: the four techniques.**

- *Reconnaissance*: "the smaller the scale of an operation, the more detailed the reconnaissance must be";
  leaders "from the highest to the lowest should seize every possibility of carrying out personal
  reconnaissance" (p. 208). Hamburg's disarmament of 17 police stations was impossible without prior study of
  "the approaches to them, the layout of their rooms, the location of the arms-magazines, the positions of the
  sentries" (p. 209).
- *Liaison*: the plan must specify it (element l); the failure mode is Reval's groups dispersing instead of
  joining a neighbouring group (p. 77); the Shanghai III fix was a dedicated liaison detachment plus a scout
  detachment at high command (p. 144).
- *Barricades*: trench 1–1.5 m wide, 1.5 m deep across the road; a barrier 50–70 m in front (wire, carts,
  furniture, sand barrels); sited at crossroads; **few marksmen in the trench** (artillery target), most
  shooters on roofs, balconies and windows of corner houses for converging fire; scouts forward (pp. 246–248).
  Anti-tank: concealed ditches 3 m across, 3–4 m deep, covered to bear 2–3 tons; or a concentrated charge of
  5–7 grenades under the hull; 76 mm field guns if available (p. 248).
- *Attack*: successive capture of blocks and barricades; either down the street (fast, needs artillery to
  silence machine-guns) or by circling through houses, courtyards and gardens (slower, fewer losses); each
  company/battalion should have one or two guns or mortars and plenty of grenades, plus axes, crowbars, picks,
  rope-ladders (pp. 252–253).

**The city has a political geography with a default plan:**

> "the insurrection will begin in the suburbs, with simultaneous diversionary actions in other districts, and
> will culminate in a concerted general assault on the city centre."
> — ch. 11, *Characteristics of the City*, p. 226

**Durations, as calibration data:** Moscow ~a week; Hamburg and Canton >2 days; Shanghai 28 hours; Reval 3–4
hours (ch. 11, p. 225).

**The case studies, as failure/success patterns.**

| Case | Force | Enemy | Outcome | Named cause |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Reval 1924 | 400 in 3 battalions; 100 pistols, ~60 rifles, 3 SMGs | 766–830 plus ~500 fascists | crushed in 3–4 h | dispersal of force; masses inert; garrison not actually subverted; no barricades; unfamiliar weapons (ch. 3, pp. 64–77) |
| Hamburg 1923 | OD 1,300; 15 Hundreds of 40–60; Barmbek 19 rifles + 27 revolvers | 20 police stations; 600 police, 6 armoured cars | not crushed — halted by party order after 2 days | political preparation absent; commanders appointed hours before; but barricades + roofs + mass support produced a 60:5 kill ratio (ch. 4, pp. 89–103) |
| Canton 1927 | 2,000 red guard; 29 Mausers, 200 grenades, **no rifles**; 1,000-strong training regiment with 200 communists | ~8,750+ incl. loyal regiments and Mauser companies | city taken, then annihilated | strategic balance: 50,000 relief troops 2–3 days away, unsubverted; no peasant support within reach (ch. 5, pp. 115–128) |
| Shanghai III 1927 | 5,000 combat squads, commander per 20–30; 150 Mausers | police + garrison | success in 28 h, then politically disarmed | model of preparation; but the political leadership handed power back (ch. 6, pp. 143–149) |

The Hamburg number is the important one for a game: **250–300 fighters held off a superior police force for
two days and inflicted ~60 dead for 4–6 of their own**, because barricades, roofs and population support
multiplied a tiny force. The same text says the rising was politically a failure. Success and failure are
different axes.

## 1.3 Trotsky: co-optation under co-signature

Read strictly for two mechanics.

**(a) The military specialists: a defection/co-optation system with a control cost.**

> "We need a real armed force, constructed on the basis of military science. Active and systematic
> participation in all our work by military specialists is therefore a matter of vital necessity."
> — "A Necessary Explanation", 23 April 1918

> "It is the fundamental task of the institution of commissars for military affairs to ensure this conformity
> [to the nature of workers' and peasants' power]."
> — ibid.

The recruitment logic is stated as a supply constraint, not a preference:

> "whoever is engaged in current work on building the army, and has to deal with actual regiments, battalions,
> companies and platoons, which need today, at once, real live regimental, battalion and company commanders,
> will not philosophize but will choose commanders from the material that is at hand."
> — "The Military Specialists and the Red Army", 1918

> "only those divisions are good ones in which both of these categories work side by side" (regular officers
> and promoted NCOs/privates) — ibid.

And the risk is priced honestly — betrayal versus incompetence, with no clean answer:

> "if it be asked which has done us most harm up to now: betrayal by former regular officers or incompetence
> on the part of many new commanders, I personally should find it hard to give an answer." — ibid.

The officer corps is stratified rather than binary (ch. 18, "The Officer Question"): careerists who repaint
themselves; a small genuinely converted group; pen-pushers; open enemies; and "the most numerous group" —
cowardly waverers waiting to see. That is a **five-category personnel pool**, and it maps directly onto a
recruitment table with different loyalty and competence distributions.

**(b) The commissar is a co-signature with a sphere, not a spy.**

> "The sphere in which the commissar has fewest 'rights' is that of operations, of command … there cannot be
> two commanders at once … But nobody has ever forbidden the commissar to express his opinion regarding
> operational problems, to give advice, to supervise the execution of an operational order."
> — "On the Military Commissars", Autumn 1918

> "In the sphere of political education it is the commissar who wields the conductor's baton, just as in the
> sphere of operational command this will always be wielded by the commander." — ibid.

> "the closer they will come to that system of one-man authority in which the person placed at the head of a
> unit combines in himself both commander and commissar." — ibid.

Mechanically: attach a political officer to a specialist commander; the officer cannot change the order, but
can *refuse to countersign*, report, and remove. The cost is a second leader slot per formation — which is
also why the army can absorb unreliable specialists at all.

**(c) Doctrine is an art, and the quote is narrower than the folklore.**

> "does military doctrine mean the sum-total of military methods, and is this a theory, or is military doctrine
> an art, the sum total of certain applied methods which, taken together, teach one how to fight? It is
> imperative to distinguish between science, as objective cognition of what exists, and art, which teaches how
> to act."
> — "Opening and Closing Speeches in the Discussion on Military Doctrine", 1 November 1921

> "war cannot have any eternal laws." — "Military Doctrine or Pseudo-Military Doctrinairism", §I

> "an art of war has developed historically. Its methods and usages undergo change, together with the social
> conditions which govern it." — ibid.

> "Civil war signifies … the war is waged, especially in the initial stage, by an enterprising minority on each
> side, and, consequently, by more or less scanty and therefore mobile masses; and, for this reason, much more
> depends on improvisation and accident." — ibid.

> "Every individual regiment, and the army as a whole, were living improvisations." — ibid.

> "Marxism does not supply ready recipes." — ibid.

The often-quoted formula "military doctrine is an art, not an empirical science" is a **paraphrase** of the
1921 distinction; the literal sentence is the science/art distinction above. Use the paraphrase only as a
label, never as a quotation.

**(d) Building while fighting, and presence at the decisive point.**

> "The most responsible decisions were taken under fire … Illusions and errors brought with them almost
> immediate retribution." — "Author's Preface: Through Five Years", Vol. I

> "Any opposition there might be to these decisions was tested in action there and then, on the spot." — ibid.

The armoured train is documented in the Trotsky Internet Archive's **editorial** introduction ("often times
written on the armored train Trotsky used to command the Red Army during various campaigns"), not in the
texts themselves; treat the train as archival framing, and the mechanic — a command post that moves to the
sector that is deciding — as supported by the preface's "on the spot" and by the order-and-inspection
structure of the volumes. **Uncertainty flagged.**

## 1.4 Mao: base first, and the enemy as arsenal

**The precondition list is seven items and it is ordered:**

> "1. Arousing and organizing the people. 2. Achieving internal unification politically. 3. Establishing
> bases. 4. Equipping forces. 5. Recovering national strength. 6. Destroying enemy's national strength.
> 7. Regaining lost territories."
> — ch. 1, *What Is Guerrilla Warfare?*

Equipping comes **after** basing. That ordering is the whole doctrine: you cannot arm before you have somewhere
to put the arms and someone to feed the bearer. And the political goal precedes everything:

> "Without a political goal, guerrilla warfare must fail … The moment that this war of resistance dissociates
> itself from the masses of the people is the precise moment that it dissociates itself from hope of ultimate
> victory." — ch. 1

**Guerrilla is a phase, not a identity:**

> "During the progress of hostilities, guerrillas gradually develop into orthodox forces that operate in
> conjunction with other units of the regular army." — ch. 1

> "guerrilla operations … are but one step in the total war … they, lacking the quality of independence, are of
> themselves incapable of providing a solution to the struggle." — ch. 1

**The scale ladder is administrative, not just tactical** (ch. 5, *Organization for Guerrilla Warfare*):

> "Regular combatant guerrillas are organized into one of three general types of units. The first … is the
> small unit, the platoon or company. In each county, three to six units may be organized. The second type is
> the battalion of from two to four companies. One such unit should be organized in each county … The third
> type may be regiments of from two to four companies, or battalions."

With a territorial overlay — military areas subdivided into districts of two to six counties, each with a
military commander and political commissioners (ch. 5) — and a mass layer:

> "All the people of both sexes from the ages of sixteen to forty-five must be organized into anti-Japanese
> self-defence units … As a first step, they must procure arms, then they must be given both military and
> political training." — ch. 5

**Equipment flow, in one line:**

> "Guerrillas must not depend too much on an armory. **The enemy is the principal source of their supply.**"
> — ch. 5

with the sequence "elementary weapons … and as many new-type rifles as are available … After a period of
resistance, it is possible to increase the supply of equipment by capturing it from the enemy" (ch. 5), plus
one district armory for manufacture/repair and a demolition unit per regiment.

**Concentration is a local, temporary, numerical fact:**

> "If she can maintain communications, she will be able at will to concentrate powerful forces speedily at
> strategic points to engage our organized units in decisive battle." — ch. 4 (the enemy's advantage, stated
> as the thing to deny)
> "We must make war everywhere and cause dispersal of his forces and dissipation of his strength." — ch. 4

Mao's own concentration language in this text is about **the enemy's** inability to concentrate; the positive
maxim ("concentrate a superior force to destroy the enemy one by one") belongs to his 1936–47 strategic
writings, not to this 1937 pamphlet. See correction C1.

## 1.5 Marighella: the mission catalog and the firing group

**Unit scale is capped by design.**

> "A team of no more than four or five is called a firing group. A minimum of two firing groups, separated and
> insulated from other firing groups, directed and coordinated by one or two persons, this is what makes a
> firing team."
> — ch. 7, *The Firing Group*

That is the ceiling: 4–5 per group, ~10 per team, plus a strategic command. There is no battalion in the
Minimanual, and there is no territorial formation. The organisation is a **network with a mission queue**,
not a hierarchy:

> "there is no such thing as a firing group without its own initiative … any firing group can decide to raid a
> bank, to kidnap or execute an agent of the dictatorship … without the need to consult with the general
> command." — ch. 7
> "No firing group can remain inactive waiting for orders from above. Its obligation is to act." — ch. 7

**The mission catalog is the early-game op list, already ordered by complexity:**

> "The inexperienced guerrilla must be gradually introduced into actions and operations which run from the
> simple to the complex."
> — ch. 17, *On the Types and Nature of Missions*
> assaults; raids and penetrations; occupations; ambushes; street tactics; strikes and work stoppages;
> desertions, diversions, seizures, expropriation of weapons, ammunition and explosives; liberation of
> prisoners; executions; kidnappings; sabotage; terrorism; armed propaganda; war of nerves.

Each has a stated objective, which makes them convertible into ops with different yields:
- ambush — "The principle object of the ambush is to capture enemy weapons and to punish him with death" (ch. 22);
- expropriation — the military urban guerrilla deserts "with modern weapons and ammunition, to hand them over
  to the guerrillas", ideally "by government transportation" (ch. 25);
- sabotage — five target classes: economy, production, transport/communication, military-police installations,
  repressive apparatus, property of exploiters (ch. 29);
- armed propaganda — every action is propaganda, plus a clandestine press (ch. 31);
- war of nerves — false clues, false plans, rumours, exploitation of government errors, international
  denunciation (ch. 32).

**The anti-base rule is explicit and is the sharpest break with Mao:**

> "our urban technique can never be permanent, can never defend a fixed base nor remain in any one spot waiting
> to repel the circle of repression."
> — ch. 9, *Characteristics of the Urban Guerrilla's Tactics*

and the purpose is to buy time for someone else:

> "to wear out, demoralize and distract the enemy forces, permitting the emergence and survival of rural
> guerrilla warfare, which is destined to play the decisive role."
> — ch. 9

So Marighella is not a rival end-state to Mao; he is a **metropole holding action** for a rural war elsewhere.
That distinction matters for a game whose map may contain both.

**The contradiction inside Marighella himself** is more useful than the one usually attributed to him. He
demands permanent offensive tempo (ch. 7, ch. 9: "defensive action means death for us"), *and* he lists as
deadly sins:

> "The fifth sin … is rash action. The guerrilla who commits this sin loses patience, suffers an attack of
> nerves, does not wait for anything, and impetuously throws himself into action, suffering untold defeats."
> "The seventh sin … is to fail to plan things, and to act spontaneously."
> — ch. 37, *The Seven Sins of the Urban Guerrilla*

The "better to err acting than to err not acting" formula **does not appear in this edition** (searched all 38
chapters read; §6, C2). The real Marighella line is: act continuously, but never without reconnaissance,
mechanisation and a withdrawal plan (ch. 34).

**Popular support is a threshold, not a majority:**

> "It is enough to win the support of a portion of the population … 'Let he who does not wish to do anything
> for the guerrillas do nothing against them.'" — ch. 31

That is a **neutrality**, not loyalty — a much cheaper and more realistic support model than "win the people".

## 1.6 RAF: armed struggle as precondition, vanguard as model

From the 1971 excerpt, three claims, all load-bearing and all contested elsewhere:

> "We are arguing that armed struggle is a necessary precondition for the latter to succeed and progress …
> and that it can and must begin now."
> — *The Urban Guerrilla Concept*, 1 April 1971

> "We doubt it will be possible to create a strategy to unify the working class or to create an organization
> that could simultaneously express and initiate the necessary unifying process."
> — ibid.

> "Unity can only be created through the common struggle of the conscious section of the working class and the
> intellectuals, one which they do not stage-manage, but which they model."
> — ibid.

> "Long-term, meticulous work is crucial for the urban guerrilla, insofar as we want to go beyond discussion
> to action." — ibid.

Mechanically this is the **inverted gate**: Neuberg and Mao put mass/territory *before* armed action; RAF puts
armed action before unity and claims unity is *modelled* by the act. That is a distinct doctrine with a
distinct curve — action unlocks legitimacy rather than legitimacy unlocking action — and it should be
selectable, and expensive.

# Part II — The stage / scale table

One table, synthesised. "Ceiling" is the largest formation the text will actually field at that stage;
"gate" is what must exist before the next rung; "failure mode" is the named historical failure.

| # | Stage | Ceiling | Gate to next rung | Equipment source | Leadership presence | Failure mode | Text |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| S0 | Clandestine cell / self-defence unit | 3–10, unarmed or improvised | A mass base that will feed and hide it; political goal stated | Local manufacture, private arms, sticks/spears | Cell leader; Party branch as staff | Acting before the mass exists (Reval) | Neuberg ch. 3 p. 64; Giáp p. 24; Mao ch. 5 |
| S1 | Firing group / guerrilla squad | 4–5 (Marighella) to 8–10 (Neuberg, Canton) | A second group + a coordinator; and either a village base or an urban support network | Seizure from single officers/police; expropriation; desertion with weapons | Group leader is the best shot; no staff | Inactivity, or action without recon | Marighella ch. 7, 17, 25; Neuberg ch. 5 p. 115 |
| S2 | Platoon / company (guerrilla band) | 80–150 (fused companies), 3–6 per county | Enough cadres to command; a district armory or supply; a base area or an urban *radius* | "The enemy is the principal source of their supply" | District military commander + political commissioner | Dispersal without concentration; unfamiliar weapons | Mao ch. 5; Neuberg ch. 3 p. 64; Giáp p. 24 |
| S3 | Battalion / mobile battalion | 2–4 companies; 1 per county; ~300–600 | Ability to concentrate without losing the base; regional troops to hold ground | War booty at scale; local repair; first factories | Regional committee + mobile command | Holding ground instead of men (Canton, Dien Bien Phu 1953 decision) | Mao ch. 5; Giáp p. 24; Neuberg ch. 5 |
| S4 | Regiment / division | 2–4 battalions per regiment; many regiments per campaign | Logistics that survive movement; a rear; heavy weapons crews | Divisional capture; state arsenals; foreign supply | Front command; commissar co-signature at every level | Premature general counter-offensive (1950) | Giáp pp. 12, 23, 50; Trotsky ch. 24 |
| S5 | Army / front / state | Hundreds of thousands; fronts | A state that can pay, feed, replace; a doctrine that is an art | Industry + capture + alliance | General HQ, inspectorate, mobile command post | Building the army while fighting it (Trotsky); political disarmament after victory (Shanghai III) | Trotsky Vol. I; Giáp p. 24; Neuberg ch. 6 p. 149 |

**What changes between rows** (this is the part a tech-tree usually gets wrong):

1. **Command latency goes from zero to positive.** S0–S1 leaders are in the same room; S3+ needs liaison
   detachments, scouts, and written plans (Neuberg ch. 6 p. 144, ch. 10 p. 221 element l).
2. **The unit of decision changes.** S1–S2 decides *whether to act*; S3–S4 decides *where to concentrate*;
   S5 decides *whether to accept battle at all* (Giáp's 1953 refusal at Na San, pp. 83–84).
3. **Equipment changes from windfall to pipeline.** At S1 each gun is an event; at S4 a lost company is a
   supply problem. Giáp's whole ladder is driven by this (p. 12, p. 24).
4. **Ground changes sign.** At S1–S2 ground is a hiding place (positive); at S3 it becomes a liability to be
   traded (Giáp p. 23: "losses must be avoided even at the cost of losing ground"); at S5 it is the object again.
5. **The political officer changes function.** At S1, morale; at S3, co-signature and purge; at S5, the
   commander *is* the commissar (Trotsky, "On the Military Commissars").

# Part III — Contradictions as selectable doctrine lines

Each row is a real disagreement between texts, not a synthesis. The point is that the engine cannot satisfy
both sides with one coefficient.

| Axis | Line A | Line B | What differs mechanically |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Base** | Mao: "Establishing bases" is precondition #3; bases must "support independent operations" (ch. 1, ch. 5) | Marighella: "can never defend a fixed base nor remain in any one spot" (ch. 9) | A generates a defensible stock and a recruitment rate that compounds; B has no stock, high op tempo, and cannot survive a sustained sweep |
| **Tempo vs preparation** | Neuberg: "The defensive is the death of every armed rising"; "Energy, offensive action and attack must dominate all else at this stage — whatever the cost may be" (ch. 10, p. 250) | Giáp: "refusing to give battle likely to incur losses"; "losses must be avoided even at the cost of losing ground" (p. 12, p. 23) | A trades casualties for initiative and collapses if the first blow misses; B trades ground and time for exchange ratio and may never reach the gate |
| **The gate to action** | Neuberg/Lenin: "impossible to act with a vanguard alone" (ch. 2, p. 51) | RAF: "armed struggle is a necessary precondition" for unity; unity is "modelled" by the act | A requires a mass/army threshold before any op; B unlocks ops immediately but legitimacy grows only *from* ops, and repression scales faster |
| **The enemy's cadres** | Trotsky: recruit the specialist, bind him with a commissar, accept betrayal as a cost (ch. 24) | Neuberg: the junker academies, NCO colleges and "Mauser companies" are the executioners and must be disarmed or destroyed (ch. 3 p. 73; ch. 5 p. 113) | A converts enemy skill into your order of battle at a loyalty discount; B removes enemy skill and gains nothing but safety |
| **Timing** | Neuberg: an uprising *can* be timed to a date and a congress (ch. 2, p. 51) | Giáp: the counter-offensive is gated by objective conditions; forcing it is "subjectivism" (p. 50) | A is a scheduled high-variance roll; B is a threshold that cannot be rushed |
| **Supply** | Giáp/Mao: capture is the production function | Marighella: expropriation (banks) funds the war; Trotsky: state industry and conscription | Capture scales with enemy commitment; expropriation scales with urban density and feeds repression; industry requires a state |
| **Ground** | Mao: bases and liberated territory are the point | Giáp: ground is a loan; Cannae-style encirclement is preferred to holding | A scores territory; B scores enemy manpower destroyed |
| **Urban purpose** | Marighella: urban war is decisive in wearing down the state | Giáp/Mao: cities are a phase to survive, then abandon | A wins by attrition in the city; B must exit the city to win |

## Doctrine cards (usable as starting builds)

**D1 — Protracted Base (Mao/Giáp).** Start S0. Gate: political goal → base → equipment. Growth is
superlinear but delayed; counter-offensive locked until exchange ratio crosses. Strength: cannot be decapitated.
Weakness: slow, and loses if the enemy is willing to trade the countryside for time.

**D2 — Urban Attrition (Marighella).** Start S1 directly. No stock, high tempo, mission queue. Growth is
linear in ops but capped at team scale; each successful op raises repression, which raises recruitment and
casualties at the same time. Strength: forces the state onto the defensive everywhere. Weakness: no path to S3.

**D3 — Insurrectionist Art (Neuberg).** Start S1–S2, build toward a single scheduled S3 event. Requires:
mass support threshold, army subversion threshold, reconnaissance of every objective, synchronised watches.
Strength: one roll can take a city. Weakness: if any precondition is missing the force is destroyed in hours
(Reval), and even success can be politically reversed (Shanghai III).

**D4 — Vanguard Spark (RAF).** Start S1 with no mass gate. Every op generates legitimacy *and* repression;
legitimacy only converts if the state over-reacts. Strength: can act on turn one. Weakness: no unity mechanic
until the state makes a mistake; structurally unable to reach S4.

**D5 — Co-optation (Trotsky).** Start with a state or a large formation. Convert enemy specialists; each
converted commander consumes one commissar slot and carries a betrayal probability that spikes when the front
moves. Strength: instant S4 competence. Weakness: command reliability is a per-unit random variable, and
purges cost competence.

**D6 — Holding Action (Marighella's real thesis).** Urban ops exist to protect a rural build elsewhere. The
city is a timer bought for another theatre; the doctrine wins only if the rural line reaches S4 before the
urban network is rolled up.

# Part IV — Design readings (OURS)

**R1. Model the stage variable as a *ceiling plus a ratio*, not a level.** Giáp's ladder is a force-ceiling
that rises with captured equipment and a ratio that must cross before the counter-offensive unlocks. Two
numbers, not one XP bar. The 1950 "premature general counter-offensive" is then a legal action with a
predicted failure, not a disabled button.

**R2. Make capture the primary equipment production function, with industry as a capped supplement.** Both
Giáp and Mao say it flatly. If the player's materiel income is mostly domestic, the people's-war curve is
wrong. Implement: `equipment_gain ≈ f(enemy_commitment, tactical_success) − f(attrition)`, and let a
district armory raise the *repair/convert* rate rather than create weapons from nothing.

**R3. Use Neuberg's fourteen plan elements as the insurrection planner's actual UI.** Not a "plan
insurrection" button: a checklist whose *unfilled* elements become the failure script. Missing (m) — the
commander's start location — means the player's command node has no position and cannot issue orders for the
first hours. Missing (h) — relief troops — means the enemy arrives on schedule and unopposed. This converts a
wiki page into a mechanic and gives the player something to do in the boring preparatory turns.

**R4. Give leadership presence a radius and a latency, and let it be moved.** Trotsky's train, Neuberg's
synchronised watches, Giáp's village staff. Implement a mobile command element with: `inspection` (reduces
local corruption/loyalty decay), `co-signature` (blocks a betrayal roll), `synchronise` (sets H-hour for
adjacent units), and a travel time that makes it a real choice. The train is the S5 version; the Party cell is
the S0 version.

**R5. Subversion runs on grievances, not ideology.** Neuberg's partial-demands list is the substrate. An army
unit should expose 3–6 concrete grievances (pay, food, corporal punishment, furlough, service location,
language). Agitation addresses a grievance; addressing enough of them converts the unit's *neutrality* first,
and only then its *defection*. Reval failed because influence had decayed with turnover — model **cohort
turnover** as a decay term on subversion.

**R6. Distinguish neutrality from loyalty, for populations and for soldiers.** Marighella's "let he who does
not wish to do anything do nothing against them" and Reval's "the men remained neutral until they could get
more information" both describe the same middle state. Three-state support (hostile / neutral / active) is more
faithful and more interesting than a 0–100 opinion bar, and it makes the first blow's *visibility* decisive.

**R7. Price the two defensive doctrines differently.** Neuberg's active defence (barricades, roofs, cross-fire,
counter-attacking shock squads) converts a tiny force into a disproportionate casualty ratio but cannot hold
ground. Giáp's ground-trading defence converts space into time and preserves the force. Same combat resolver,
different loss functions: `casualty_exchange` vs `force_preservation`.

**R8. Cap Marighella at team scale and give him a different win condition.** A firing group cannot become a
battalion; if the player wants S3 they must change doctrine (D6 or D1). That is the honest reading and it
prevents the urban line from becoming a strictly better early game.

**R9. The counter-offensive should require an *enemy* precondition, not just a friendly one.** Giáp waits for
the enemy to be "sufficiently exposed" and for international conjunctures; Neuberg waits for the army to be
subverted and the masses to be rising. Model the gate as `friendly_ratio ≥ x AND enemy_exposure ≥ y`. Otherwise
the player just farms.

**R10. Doctrine change should cost the stock it was built on.** A base-first player who switches to urban
attrition loses the base's compounding; an urban player who switches to base-first has no territorial
apparatus to receive. The doctrine cards are cheap to *state* and expensive to *switch*, which is what makes
them identities rather than loadouts.

# Part VI — Corrections, misattributions, uncertainty

**C1. "Fight no battle unprepared" is not in *On Guerrilla Warfare* (1937).** The 1937 text's discipline is
"complete preparation" in the narrow sense of reconnaissance and command (ch. 1: "in spite of the most complete
preparation, there can be no victory if mistakes are made in the matter of command"), but the maxim belongs to
Mao's later strategic writings (1936 *Problems of Strategy in China's Revolutionary War*; 1947 ten principles),
which were **not** part of this read. Do not cite this briefing for that sentence. The *operational* content in
1937 is real: base before equipment (ch. 1), enemy as arsenal (ch. 5), dispersal of the enemy (ch. 4).

**C2. "Better to err acting than to err not acting" is not in the Minimanual.** I searched every chapter cited.
The nearest actual sentences are "Its obligation is to act" (ch. 7) and "defensive action means death for us"
(ch. 9) — but ch. 37 lists "rash action" and "fail to plan things" among the seven deadly sins. The quote is
probably from another Marighella text or from a secondary summary; it should be dropped or re-sourced, because
as stated it contradicts the manual's own risk section. **This is a correction to the brief's framing.**

**C3. "Military doctrine is an art, not an empirical science" is a paraphrase.** The literal 1921 sentence
distinguishes "science, as objective cognition of what exists" from "art, which teaches how to act". Use the
distinction, not the quotation marks.

**C4. The armoured train is archival framing.** It appears in the Trotsky Internet Archive introduction, not in
the volumes read. The mechanic (mobile command presence) is independently supported; the specific vehicle is not.

**C5. The local Mao mirror contains only ch01.htm.** Chapters 2–6 were fetched from the same MIA path. No
sibling files existed at `/media/user/data/old-hdd/old-hdd/www.marxists.org/archive/mao/works/1937/guerrilla-warfare/`
— the mirror path is `…/reference/archive/mao/…` and holds one chapter.

**C6. The RAF source is an excerpt.** The Blanqui Archive page gives translated extracts, not the full 1971
pamphlet. Claims about RAF internal organisation, the "military apparatus" debate, and the later
*RAF-Aufruf* are out of scope and not asserted here.

**C7. Giáp's numbers are war-propaganda numbers.** The 16,000 at Dien Bien Phu and the 500,000 French corps
are the text's own figures; they are internally consistent but not independently checked against French
archives. Use them as *design magnitudes*, not history.

**C8. Neuberg is a Comintern training manual with an axe to grind.** Its judgments on Hamburg's leadership
("in practice betrayed the insurrection") and on Canton's "putsch" character are partisan. The *numbers* and
*procedures* are the usable part; the verdicts are not.

**C9. The Reval, Hamburg, Canton and Shanghai cases are not one phenomenon.** Reval was a conspiracy with no
mass; Hamburg was a mass rising with no leadership; Canton was a city rising inside a hostile province;
Shanghai III was a successful rising that was politically surrendered. Any single "insurrection" mechanic that
treats them as one distribution will be wrong. They are four different failure/success modes, which is why
they belong in a scenario selector rather than a difficulty slider.
