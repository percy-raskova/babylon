# Households and their homes: design

**Status:** Second draft, 20 November 2026, for the Director's approval. Nothing is built. The
Director chose the subject, stated its aim, corrected one reading and approved the direction on
that day (section 2). Three architects checked the direction against the code before the first
draft. Four readers then checked that draft: a builder, the engine, the rulings and sources, and the
player's view. They found step A sound and step B not ready to plan. This draft answers them, and
Appendix D lists what they found.

**What approval covers.** Step A is ready to plan and build. Step B's rules are stated here. Its
plan is written after the gate of section 9: step A played, and the census tables captured, with the
opening's arithmetic worked on Wayne's real figures. Approval also covers that capture. The tables
are federal public data, and the units of step A are the wired consumer that the ruling of 17 August
asks for.

**Scope:** The second slice of the class-agents lane (PER-366) and the first relation of the land
foundation. Units and homes are in Wayne County only (county geoid 26163). One new reference table
covers all 3,144 counties.

**How to read the marks.** **[Director]** records a decision the Director has made. **[Call]** is a
choice the author made that the Director can overrule. **[Record needed]** marks a choice that must
be written into a decision record before it is built; section 8 lists them. Each number carries its
evidence class: Observed, Derived, Calibrated or Designed. Money is in game currency units and is
written with a dollar sign.

## 1. What this slice is for

The Director set the aim: "the goal and intention of this should be to give richness to the "NPC"s
and enhance their agency with NPCs being household units and social classes". The subject is home
ownership, because "a large portion of the labor aristocracy derives wealth from home ownership".

Today a Wayne household has nothing of its own. Appendix A gives the engine facts.

- The county's 700,591 households are four pooled budgets, and three small named ones. Every
  household of a budget shares one purse.
- The County Review counts households in positions. A position has a band and a count. It has no
  money.
- Nobody owns a home and nobody rents one. Housing is a product that every household buys from a
  business, at one price.
- Nothing can be owed. A household that cannot pay goes without, and nothing follows.

So no household can be poorer than its neighbor in the same budget, and none can decide anything
about where it lives.

This slice changes that in two steps. Each step can be played.

1. **A household unit holds its own money.** Each unit gets its own account. Its wages, its relief
   and its tax are its own. What it cannot afford, it goes without. The player can see who is going
   without, reach them, and see in the next period what changed.
2. **A household unit has a home.** It rents or it owns. A tenant pays rent. An owner pays property
   tax and, if the home is mortgaged, the mortgage. What is not paid is owed. A tenant who stays
   behind is put out. An owner who stays behind on the tax loses the home to the county, which
   offers it for sale. A tenant with enough money buys one. The player's organizer can bring an
   owner to the county to ask for the exemption the law allows, and that keeps the home.

A class is then visible as what its units did: how many paid, fell behind, lost a home, bought one,
or went to the county.

**Three things in this draft differ from what the Director approved on 20 November.** Approval of
this draft covers them.

1. **The large landlord is a business, not a kind of household.** **[Call]** The author's playback
   listed four kinds of unit: tenant, owner, small landlord and large landlord. The engine cannot
   tell a large landlord from any other business owner, because a business's shares are spread over
   every owner household of the county alike. So a business lets homes, as it does today, and its
   rent reaches the business-owner households as their income from ownership. That is also what
   ADR208 R9 ruled for the rentier.
2. **Existing mortgages are in.** **[Call]** Direction 2 said that mortgages and lending would wait.
   After the approval the author told the Director that existing mortgages would come in with the
   homes and that only new loans would wait. The reason is the ruling itself: "low equity evolves
   into equity over time", and equity can only grow if what is owed on the home is in the books and
   is paid down. The Director has not yet answered that message.
3. **The bottom half's owners open with no equity.** **[Call]** ADR305 says the bottom 50% "don't
   own homes". In Wayne about 64% of households own, so some owners must be in the bottom half.
   Under the ruling of 20 November they are owners like any other. This draft reads the law's words
   as "they hold no home equity at the opening": such a household owes on its home what the home is
   worth (section 7.15).

## 3. What is known

### What the engine holds

- **Households are counted groups.** Wayne has four large budget groups and three small named ones.
  A group has one account. The word "owner" in a group's name means a household with a share in a
  business. That share is Designed: one household in ten.
- **A position** is a group's households at one crew, with a band, the people at work, the people
  looking for work and the relief units held. The recorded opening has 111 positions over 41 crews.
- **The bands** are set once: 7,006 households in the top 1%, 63,054 in the next 9%, 280,236 in the
  next 40% and 350,295 in the bottom 50%.
- **Every money path is keyed by the account that is paid or charged**: wages, wage tax, county
  support, income from ownership and purchases. Nothing in the running game needs to know how many
  accounts a county has.
- **Housing** is a service called Housing provision. Each household buys one unit a period from one
  business fixed at the opening. The opening price is $1,450 and the price moves by a rule.
- **A business's shares** are carried at zero. They are never priced or sold. Half of a business's
  earnings are paid out to the households that hold its shares, and every owner household of the
  county holds the same share.
- **The county** taxes wages and business income at 15%. It has no tax on property. Tax is collected
  at the end of the close, after a household has bought what it needs.
- **Nothing can be owed** except a wage that an employer has not yet paid. There is no bank.
- **When a household is short,** the engine buys goods before services, and inside each it follows
  the order of internal identifiers. So what a poor household gives up first is an accident.

### What the sources say

Appendix B gives each source.

- **Who owns in Wayne.** In the project's held census table, labelled 2023, 447,120 of 693,446
  households own their home: 64%. Of households with a white householder 77% own (279,218 of
  362,903). Of households with a Black householder 47% own (124,369 of 262,653). The slice builds
  its own pinned table from the 2024 files, and these figures are provisional until then.
- **Whose wealth homes are.** In the Federal Reserve's accounts for the second quarter of 2026 the
  next 40% hold 46.6% of all household real estate, and real estate is 37% of what they own. The
  bottom 50% hold 9.7% of household real estate and 22.6% of all mortgage debt.
- **How many owe on the home.** In the 2025 Survey of Consumer Finances 65.6% of families own their
  home and 42.0% owe on it, so about two owners in three have a mortgage.
- **How a home is lost in Wayne.** Michigan's law hands unpaid property tax to the county treasurer
  each March. A fee of 4% and interest of 1% a month are added. After one year the home is forfeited
  and the interest rises to 1.5% a month. After another year the county forecloses, and sells the
  home at auction in the autumn. Wayne County foreclosed on about 25,000 properties in 2015, more
  than 9,000 of them occupied.
- **Who lets homes.** A 2022 count found 42,191 landlords in Detroit. 87% owned one or two
  properties. The 5% that owned five or more held about a third of the rental housing.
- **MIM's line.** "The key to full rights as an Amerikan citizen is home-ownership. You can't be a
  settler without a house." (MIM Notes 60.) "Net worth is means of production, in which home equity
  and pension funds have to be counted." Appendix C gives the texts.

## 4. Goals, non-goals and anti-goals

**Goals**

1. Every Wayne household unit has money of its own, and every dollar a unit receives or pays can be
   read in its own account.
2. In step A the player can see who is going without, by count, reach them, and read the result in
   the next period.
3. A unit's housing situation is its own: it rents, or it owns, with a value, what it owes and its
   equity. Equity is a number that moves.
4. A household's own choices follow from its own figures, and a receipt explains each: what it pays
   first, whether it buys, whether it goes to the county. What happens to it is kept apart from what
   it chose (section 7.14).
5. Arrears are really owed. A tenancy really ends. A home really changes hands, with the money and
   the debts that go with it.
6. The county gains a tax on homes in its own books, and acts on unpaid tax by its own rule.
7. A class can be read as what its units did in the period, by count.
8. The opening obeys the rule of thirds with homes in it, or the build refuses it by name.
9. Every count and every dollar adds back, at every grouping, every period. Every rule is exact and
   repeatable.
10. Each period the player still makes one choice, and from step B it has a third road: the county's
    relief, the food run, or a household's home.

**Non-goals for this slice**

- Households that let homes, and their tenants (the small landlord).
- New loans, buying on credit and land contracts.
- A sale by the owner, a second home, and a business that buys homes.
- Foreclosure by a lender, and foreclosure on a business's homes.
- Homes built or pulled down. The number of homes is fixed at the opening.
- Nation on a unit. Tenure by race of householder is shown as an observed table. No unit carries it
  and no rule reads it.
- Bodies of owners, of tenants or of landlords at the county, and a county board that changes the
  tax.
- Units or homes outside Wayne.
- Farm land, forest land, public land and the land ledger (section 11).

**Anti-goals: the slice has failed if it contains any of these**

- A line drawn between owners by the size of their equity.
- A household's money or debt that is only a display and that no payment reads.
- A rule that decides for a household from the average of its group. Section 5 says what a
  household's own figures are.
- A class label that is assigned by hand and then drives behavior.
- A home value that follows a market the game does not have.
- An individual person or a single house simulated as an object.
- A home that is taken or sold with no account that pays and no account that is paid.
- An opening bent quietly to meet the law.
- A census figure shown as if the game had measured it, or a designed figure shown as observed.
- A screen that lists every unit and every figure at once.

## 5. Rules this design must obey

- **The four counting rules** of ADR302 hold for homes, debts and money as they do for households.
- **A household's own figures.** **[Call]** **[Record needed]** A household's labor figures are its
  position's: at work, looking for work, relief units, hours. Its money and its home are its
  unit's, because the unit is the smallest thing that holds money or a home. A unit's purse is spent
  for whole households: it pays, or buys, for as many of its households as its cash covers, and the
  rest fall behind or go without. That is a declared split of one total, which is ADR302's third
  rule. A figure shown "for each household" is a rate of two of the unit's totals, which is its
  second.
- **Article II of the Constitution.** The count of homes in a county is ground. Who holds a home is
  an overlay that play can change.
- **Article IV.** Every value carries its evidence class. A Designed value says so on screen.
- **The Aleksandrov test.** Every new object names the material thing it stands for: a household's
  purse, a house, a rent, a tax bill, a debt.
- **Amendment AM** (proposed, ADR300). No rule has an imposed curve. A rule decides for one
  household, and the answer for a unit is the count of households the rule admits.
- **ADR184.** What a body spends belongs to that body. The county forecloses and sells as the county
  administration, from its own account.
- **ADR182.** What exists is public. A unit's dollar figures are earned by contact, as in the County
  Review. Counts are public.
- **ADR098.** Only data that may be redistributed enters a committed source. The census tables are
  federal public data. Detroit's parcel file prints "No License Provided" and is not used.
- **The audit's rules for new data** (12 August, section 8): pin one vintage with its hash; carry
  withheld values as withheld; never average a rate; name the consumer before the download.
- **Determinism.** Whole numbers and exact fractions. No random draws. No order taken from an
  identifier.

|---|---|---|

### Smaller choices, with the bound not taken

- **A ledger inside the pooled account** was rejected. Every payment reads the real account, so one
  unit could still spend another's money. It would be a display.
- **A unit for every position** was rejected. Its name would change at every hire and layoff, and
  money would have to follow.
- **A home as a stock of goods** was rejected. Goods are bought, shipped and used up. A home has a
  holder, a value for tax and a household living in it.
- **A bank** was rejected for this slice. Existing mortgages need one record each and a payment.
  They need no credit.
- **A home value that follows prices** was rejected. A home is carried at what was paid for it, as a
  business share is carried today.

## 7. The design

Sections 7.1 to 7.6 are step A. Sections 7.7 to 7.19 are step B.

### 7.1 Step A: the unit

A **household unit** is a set of households that share one purse and that the player can follow
from period to period. It answers three questions, and the answers never change.

| Question | Answers |
|---|---|
| Which budget group? | One of the engine's household groups in Wayne |
| Which crew were its people bound to at the opening? | One workplace and one way of being paid there. Or none |
| Which band was it in at the opening? | The top 1%, the next 9%, the next 40% or the bottom 50% |

- **The numbers at work, looking for work and holding relief units are states inside a unit.** They
  move every period. The unit does not. The engine never moves a person to another workplace, so a
  hire or a layoff happens inside one unit and moves no money between units.
- **A position** is now a row inside a unit: the unit's households with the same number at work,
  looking and holding relief units. The County Review's rules run on positions as they do today.
- **One purse for a unit.** **[Call]** The households of a unit share cash although some have work
  and some do not. The engine pays one crew as one amount, and that is the floor of the grain.
- **Each unit is an account of the kind the engine already has.** **[Record needed]** No new kind of
  account is added. The unit's identity is made from its group, its opening crew and its band.
- **Wayne only.** **[Call]** The other counties keep their budgets as they are. A campaign asks for
  units in its policy file, and the build that makes them has a new compiler name (section 7.18).
  Every earlier compiler gives the same bytes as before.
- **The three small named groups** (the claimants, the aid donors and the aid partners) each stay
  one unit. The hearing, the aid mandates and the organizer name them. If a rule of the opening
  would cut one of them, the build refuses the campaign by name. **[Call]**
- **Collective residence** stays one unit with no households, as today.
- **Two words.** **[Call]** On screen the new object is always a "household unit" and the county's
  support is always a "relief unit". The budget groups read "with a business share" and "no business
  share", so that "owner" is free for homes.

### 7.2 Step A: the opening

The opening divides each Wayne budget group among its units. Nothing new is decided about money.

- **Bands and units come from one rule.** The build runs the County Review's own placement on the
  pooled groups. Each distinct group, crew and band among its rows is a unit.
- **A crew that spans two bands becomes two crews,** one for each unit, so that a unit's wages are
  paid to it as one amount.
- **Cash, goods, needs and support follow the counts.** The engine sets a household's opening cash,
  its pantry and its needs from its persons and households, and its support from its job-seekers.
  So the units of a group add back to today's group exactly, with no rule of their own.
- **Persons are split by a declared rule.** **[Call]** A unit's persons are its labor force, plus a
  share of the group's other adults and children in proportion to its households, by largest
  remainder. The build refuses a unit with fewer persons than households.
- **Shares in businesses are split in two stages.** First a business's shares go to the budget
  groups by households, as today. Then a group's shares go to its units by households, by largest
  remainder. The same is done for the ages the engine keeps. One stage would move each group's total
  by rounding.
- **Wealth is measured, and the law binds in step B.** **[Call]** ADR305 names a measurement as the
  first task of this slice: the share of household wealth the top tenth holds, set beside the law's
  two thirds. Step A makes it. ADR305 also says the law binds the opening split of wealth "when
  positions hold accounts of their own". Step A cannot meet that. A household's only wealth in
  step A is two periods of cash and a pantry, nearly the same for every household, so the four bands
  open with about 1%, 9%, 41% and 49% of it. Giving each of the first three a third would leave the
  bottom half unable to buy food in the first period. Homes and what is owed on them are what can
  carry the law, and they arrive in step B (section 7.15). The Director corrected an earlier
  deferral of this law, so section 13 asks.

### 7.3 Step A: the running game

- **Wages** are paid to the unit whose people earned them. **Wage tax** is charged to that unit.
  **County support** is paid to the unit whose households hold the relief units. **Income from
  ownership** is paid to the unit that holds the shares. None of this needs new code: each path
  already pays the account it is given.
- **A unit that is short goes without, in a declared order.** **[Call]** **[Record needed]** It pays
  for food first, then materials, then utility, then housing, then household services, then public
  provision. The reason: a household eats first and keeps the lights on. Today the order is an
  accident of identifiers. The other bound is to cut every need in proportion. The order is carried
  by each unit's own buying rule, so it applies to Wayne's units and to no other county.
- **Tax is collected last, as it is today.** A unit that spent its cash on its needs pays less of
  its wage tax. The county's list ranks a household by the wage tax collected from its unit, so such
  a unit stands lower on the list. That is the Director's ruling at work: "the households that pay
  least lose first". The session's record says so in words: "ranked lower: tax not collected". The
  other bound is to take the wage tax when the wage is paid, as an employer does.
- **What is not bought is unmet, and is not owed.** That stays as it is. Step B changes it for
  housing only.
- **A household that leaves** is taken from its unit. Today the roster can only take a leaving
  household from rows with no crew. With units, a crew's unit may have none, so the rule must also
  take from crew rows, in a declared order. **[Record needed]**

### 7.4 Step A: what changes in the County Review

- **The county's list ranks a household by the wage tax collected from its own unit.** The formula
  is the same. The rate that was a budget group's is now the unit's own.
- **The hours a household spends in the room are its own unit's hours.**
- **The support line** is set for each unit.
- **The livelihood profile** shows the unit's own wages, support, aid and income from ownership.
  The words "the group's total, not a household's" go. Income from ownership is also shown for each
  household, as a rate: the unit's total divided by its households.
- **The recorded playthroughs change.** A unit with no earner now lives on its own support alone.
  It will go without sooner than the pooled group showed. That is a result to report.

### 7.5 Step A: what the player sees and does

- **Who is going without is public, as a count.** For each band, and for each unit under it: the
  households that went without food, utility or housing in the period just closed. It stands beside
  "who works", which is public today. Dollar figures are still earned by contact.
- **The roster opens folded.** It shows the four bands. A band opens to its units, and a unit to its
  positions. A unit or a position with no households is not shown.
- **A unit's money,** earned by contact: cash at the close and for each household; wages; wage tax
  assessed and collected; support; income from ownership; what it could not buy, by need; free
  hours.
- **One sentence for each unit each period,** for example: "Paid for food and utility. Went without
  housing. Wage tax not collected."
- **Wealth by band,** measured each period, beside the law's thirds, with the line "Homes are not in
  yet."
- **What the player does.** The choice is the County Review's: one contact a period. It now has a
  target the player can see and an answer the player can read. A household that is going without
  food can be reached. If the county pays it a relief unit, the money goes into its own unit's
  purse, and the next period's count shows whether it ate.

### 7.6 Step A: proof

- At the opening every group's units add back exactly to the group as it was before units:
  households, persons, people at work, people looking, the ages, cash, each good, each need, the
  buying limits, support, and shares in each business.
- A campaign that asks for one unit for each group gives the old opening, byte for byte.
- Every period: the roster's sums against the engine's, for each unit; money conserved.
- The engine's limits on rows and the size of the organizer's status line are checked with units in.
- After the first period a unit's results rightly differ from the pooled group's. That drift is
  reported and is not an error.

### 7.7 Step B: kinds of unit

**Kinds.** A unit is a **tenant** or an **owner**. **An owner is one kind, whatever its equity.**
**[Director]** The rest of this section is the author's. **[Call]**

- **The kind is a fourth answer in a unit's identity.** An owner unit has a fifth: whether its homes
  were mortgaged at the opening. No rule reads that answer. It only says which owner units have a
  mortgage payment to make. Two owner units that differ in it are the same kind on every screen.
- **Every unit of step A exists in each form from the opening:** tenant, owner with a mortgage,
  owner without. A form with no households is empty, as collective residence is, and is not shown.
  So a household that changes its housing always has a unit to go to, and no account is made in the
  middle of a run.
- **A crew is split once more,** so that each form of a unit has its own crew and is paid its own
  wages.
- **A household that moves between two forms takes with it its share of everything the unit
  holds:** persons, people at work and looking, relief units, cash, goods, shares in businesses,
  hours, and its place in the roster. Each is split in proportion to households, by largest
  remainder. Its crew membership moves with its workers.
- **A household that buys joins the owner unit without a mortgage.**
- **The three small named groups are tenants,** whole. **[Call]** The placement of owners runs after
  them. The build refuses a campaign in which it would cut one.
- A **business that lets homes** is the engine's housing business. The households that hold shares
  in it receive "rent through a business" as part of their income from ownership.
- The **small landlord** is later work (section 11).

### 7.8 Step B: the home

**[Record needed]** A home is a record, held by an account: a unit, the housing business or the
county.

| A holding records | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Homes | A whole number |
| Lived in by the holder, let, empty | Whole numbers that add to the homes |
| Carrying value | What was paid for the homes. It changes only when a home changes hands, to the price paid |
| Assessed value | The value the county taxes. It stays with the home and does not follow the price paid |

- **At the opening the assessed value is the carrying value.** For a unit's homes it is the observed
  value, placed as section 7.15 says.
- **The business's homes** open at the value of the cheapest owned homes in the county: the mean
  value of the bottom band's owned homes. **[Call]** Designed, because the census values only homes
  their owners live in. They enter its books at the opening against its capital, as every home and
  every debt does for its holder.
- **An owner's housing need is met by the home it lives in.** It buys no Housing provision.
- **A tenancy** ties a tenant household to one home of the business. The tenant's rent is the
  purchase of Housing provision it makes today, at the posted price. A tenant that cannot pay still
  lives in the home, and its housing need is met: the service is supplied and the price is owed
  (section 7.9). If the business cannot supply the service in a period, no rent is owed for that
  period and the tenancy stands.
- **Equity is a number:** the carrying value, less what is owed on the home, less arrears. For one
  household it is the unit's total divided by its households. It is shown and no rule reads it.
- **Equity grows** as the mortgage is paid down. It falls when arrears are added.
- **No home is built or pulled down.** The homes held by units, by the housing business and by the
  county add to the opening count every period.
- **Wayne's housing business must be one business in Wayne.** The engine chooses each household's
  seller as the nearest in the state, and nobody has checked which that is for Wayne. The build
  refuses a campaign in which Wayne's tenants buy from a business outside Wayne or from more than
  one. If the check fails, the design returns to the Director.

### 7.9 Step B: what is owed

**What is paid for housing**

| Who | Pays | To | How much |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tenant | Rent | The housing business | The posted price of Housing provision. Its price rule is unchanged |
| Owner | Property tax | The county | The county's rate times the assessed value |
| Owner with a mortgage | The mortgage | The lender | A fixed part of the balance, and interest on the balance |
| Owner | Upkeep | The shops, as today | The materials every household already buys |
| The housing business | Property tax | The county | The same rate on its homes' assessed value |

- **An owner's upkeep is the materials need it has today.** **[Call]** The other bound is an upkeep
  bought from the housing business, which needs a new product in every county. Section 12 names the
  risk: the housing business loses the owners' custom.
- **Rent is the engine's price.** **[Call]** It opens at $1,450 for 28 days, Designed, and moves by
  the rule it has. The census rent for the county is shown beside it as a check.
- **The property tax rate is Derived** for each county: all real estate tax its owners paid in a
  year, divided by the value of their homes, taken to a 28-day period. It is billed every period.
- **All of it goes to the county's account.** **[Call]** That account already pays every government
  workplace in the county: the city, the schools and the county itself. The other bound is the
  county government's own share alone, which was $392.5 million in its 2024 fiscal year.
- **The business pays the tax on its homes.** **[Call]** Unpaid, it is owed with the same fees. The
  county does not foreclose on a business in this slice. The county's own homes are not taxed.
- **The mortgage.** **[Call]** Each mortgaged owner unit has one balance. Each period it pays the
  balance divided by the periods that remain, and interest on the balance. Rate 5.0% a year and 196
  periods remaining, both Designed. No new loan is made.
- **The lender** is a business that exists: the business-services business in Wayne with the most
  opening cash. **[Call]** The build refuses a campaign without one. The mortgages are on its books
  at the opening as what it is owed, against its capital. Interest is its income. It is taxed and
  paid out to the households that hold its shares, by the rules the engine has. Principal repaid is
  not income.

**The debt.** **[Director]** **[Record needed]** What is not paid is owed. A debt records the unit
that owes, the account it is owed to, its kind (rent, property tax or mortgage), the number of
households that owe it, the period it began in, and the balance. The same figure is the creditor's
claim. It is in both sets of books and the two always agree.

| Owed | To | Added when it is first owed |
|---|---|---|
| Rent | The housing business | 5% (Designed) |
| Property tax | The county | 4%, and 0.92% a period: Michigan's 1% a month, taken to 28 days (Derived) |
| Mortgage | The lender | 5% (Designed) |

### 7.10 Step B: paying

**The order.** **[Call]** **[Record needed]** A household pays in this order.

1. Food, then materials.
2. Utility.
3. **Whatever would cost it its home in this period if it stayed unpaid:** a rent debt or a tax
   debt whose clock runs out at this close.
4. This period's rent or mortgage payment.
5. Its other housing debts, the oldest first.
6. Household services and public provision.
7. The taxes: this period's property tax, then the wage tax.

- **Step 3 is the household's own priority.** It reads the household's own clocks. Because the
  county's clock is long and the landlord's is short, a household that is short pays the rent and
  lets the tax wait, until the tax is about to cost the home. That is how homes are lost in Wayne,
  and no rule says so.
- **The tax on the home comes before the wage tax,** because unpaid it becomes a debt that can cost
  the home, and an unpaid wage tax does not.
- **Whole households pay.** When a unit's cash does not cover a payment for all the households that
  owe it, as many whole households pay as the cash covers. The rest fall behind, and a debt is
  written for that number of households with that period on it.
- **A household that is behind stays behind until its debt is paid.** Cash paid on debts goes to the
  oldest first. A debt paid in full stops its clock.

### 7.11 Step B: falling behind, and what follows

| Owed | The creditor's clock | What follows |
|---|---|---|
| Rent | 2 periods (Designed) | The tenancy ends |
| Property tax | Michigan's three stages: delinquent, forfeited, foreclosed. The law's clock is 13 periods to forfeiture and 27 to foreclosure (Derived). Section 13 asks whether to shorten it | The home passes to the county |
| Mortgage | None in this slice | A debt and a fee |

- **Eviction.** When a household's rent debt is 2 periods old, its tenancy ends. The home is empty
  and stays with the housing business. The debt is written off: the business books a loss and the
  household is released. **[Call]** The other bound is a judgment that follows the household.
- **A household without a home** stays in its unit and is counted there. Its housing need goes
  unmet, and it loses 20 hours for each period without a home: the hours the engine takes today for
  an unmet unit of food. **[Call]** The hours are Designed.
- **Taking a home again.** **[Call]** A household without a home takes an empty home of the housing
  business when its unit's cash, after the period's payments, covers two periods of the rent for it:
  the first period and one in hand. When more households can than there are empty homes, the homes
  are shared among the units in proportion to those households, by largest remainder. So an eviction
  is not undone in the same period.
- **Forfeiture.** When a tax debt reaches the first clock, a fee of $175 is added for each home and
  the interest is 1.38% a period from the start: Michigan's 1.5% a month (Derived).
- **Foreclosure.** When a tax debt reaches the second clock, the home passes to the county. Every
  debt on it ends: the unpaid tax, and the mortgage, on which the lender books a loss, as in
  Michigan. The county carries the home at the tax that was owed. The households move to the tenant
  form of their unit, without a home.
- **A mortgage that is not paid** adds a debt and a fee, and nothing more in this slice.
- **A household that leaves the county.** **[Call]** A tenant's tenancy ends, and its share of the
  unit's rent debts is ended with a receipt, as a loss to the business. An owner's home stays with
  its unit, empty and still taxed, with what is owed on it. If the unit cannot carry it, the tax
  falls behind and the county's clock takes the home. So an empty house reaches the land bank the
  way it does in Wayne.

### 7.12 Step B: the county

The county administration is the actor the County Review made. It gains these.

| | |
|---|---|
| Income | The property tax, shown in its books beside the wage tax and the tax on businesses. The books today show only what the county pays out |
| Acts | It adds the law's fees to unpaid tax. It forfeits and forecloses by the clock. It offers the homes it holds for sale. It grants the exemption to a household that asks and qualifies |
| The land bank | The homes it holds and has not sold |
| Its public record | The units with unpaid tax, the households behind in each, and their stage: delinquent or forfeited. In Wayne the treasurer publishes these lists |

- **The county's limit on spending does not change.** **[Call]** It is fixed at the opening, as the
  County Review left it. So the new tax adds to the county's cash and does not raise what it spends.
  Relief is the county's cash or its limit, whichever is lower, less its first class. The tax
  therefore moves the period in which the county first runs short. The other bound is to raise the
  limit by the tax, which is a county board's act and belongs to a later slice.
- **Whether the tax on a home ranks a household for relief** is put to the Director (section 13).
- **The exemption.** **[Call]** Designed, on the model of Michigan's exemption for a household in
  poverty and of Wayne County's plan for those who are granted it. In the law a local board decides
  each year and may excuse all, three quarters, half or a quarter of the tax. In the game:
  - A household asks at the county's session. Only a household the player's organizer has reached
    can come, as in the County Review.
  - It comes when one period of the tax on its home is at least the floor of ADR301: two hours at
    the wage.
  - It qualifies when its unit's income in the closed period did not cover its unit's needs.
  - It is excused the whole tax for 13 periods.
  - While it is excused, the county's clock on its home stops. Its old tax debt stays owed, gains no
    new fee or interest, and is paid from the oldest when there is cash to spare.
  - The unit's tax debts are divided between its excused households and the rest in proportion to
    households, by largest remainder. Only the excused part's clock stops.
  - No ranking applies. The county grants it to every household that asks and qualifies.

### 7.13 Step B: buying

- **The county offers each home it holds at the tax that was owed on it.** That is the law's minimum
  bid. No price is found by a market, because the game has none for homes.
- **A tenant household can buy** when its unit's cash, after the period's payments, covers the price
  and a reserve of two periods of its needs for it. **[Call]** The reserve is Designed.
- **When more can buy than there are homes,** the homes are shared among the units in proportion to
  their households that can buy, by largest remainder.
- **A household that buys becomes an owner** (section 7.7). Its home is carried at the price it
  paid. The assessed value does not change, so the tax on a cheap home can be large beside its
  price. That is what the studies of Detroit found (Appendix B).
- **A home nobody buys** stays in the land bank and is offered again each period.
- **Who can buy** depends on the placement of section 7.15. If every tenant is in the bottom half,
  almost none will ever have the money, and the land bank only grows. The gate's report (section 9)
  counts the tenant households that could buy, period by period.

### 7.14 Step B: what a household decides, and what happens to it

Every rule decides for a household from that household's own figures (section 5). Each is run once
for a unit and its answer is a count.

**What a household decides**

| Choice | What it reads | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| What to pay first | Its unit's cash, and the clocks on its own debts | The order of section 7.10. It pays first what would cost it its home |
| Whether to buy | Its unit's cash, the price and the reserve | It buys a home from the county and becomes an owner |
| Whether to go to the county | Whether it is reached; the tax on its home; its hours | An owner asks for the exemption |
| Whether to take a home again | Its unit's cash and the rent | A household without a home becomes a housed tenant |

**What happens to a household**

| Event | Whose rule | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| A debt is written | The engine's books | The unpaid payment is owed, with its fee |
| Its tenancy ends | The housing business, by its clock | It is without a home |
| Its home is forfeited, then foreclosed | The county, by its clock | The home passes to the county |
| It is granted the exemption | The county, by its rule | The tax is excused and the clock stops |

- **A household reads only its own figures** (section 5), as in the County Review. No rule reads a
  class, a nation or a label.
- **How many act is a count.** No curve is imposed on it.

### 7.15 Step B: the opening

**The table.** **[Record needed]** One new reference table, for all 3,144 counties, from the Census
Bureau's 2024 five-year files: tenure; tenure by race of householder; tenure by income; mortgage
status; the value of owned homes, as counts and as dollars; real estate tax paid; units in the
building; rent. It is built, pinned and loaded as the household table is today. Counts and dollar
totals are totals. A median is shown and is used only as a check. No rule reads one.

**Placing the homes on Wayne's units.** **[Call]** **[Record needed]** Each step is a declared
split in whole numbers. The gate's report (section 9) prints each step's other bound beside it, on
Wayne's real figures, and the Director can choose on the figures.

1. **Who owns.** The census table of tenure by income gives the owners among households above and
   below each income line. The bands are read on that table as the county's households in order of
   income: the top tenth, the next 40% and the bottom 50%. That reading is Designed. Where a band's
   line falls inside an income bracket, the bracket's owners and tenants are split in proportion.
   That gives the owners in each band. The top tenth's owners are shared between the top 1% and the
   next 9% in proportion. Inside a band, owners are placed on units in proportion to households, by
   largest remainder. The rest are tenants. Other bound: owners from the top, so that the bottom
   half has as few as the count allows. The first reading puts tenants in every band, which is what
   the census shows and what lets a tenant with a wage buy.
2. **Who owes on the home.** Every owner in the bottom half has a mortgaged home (section 2). The
   county's other mortgaged homes, and all its homes with no mortgage, are shared among the owners
   of the top three bands in proportion to their owners. The build refuses an opening in which the
   county has more homes with no mortgage than owners in the top three bands. Other bound: mortgaged
   homes in proportion in every band, which gives the bottom half equity.
3. **What a home is worth.** The census counts of homes by value are walked from the dearest down,
   filling the bands' owners from the top. A bracket's dollars are its count times its middle value
   (Designed; the top bracket at its lower edge), scaled so that all brackets add to the census
   total for the county. Inside a band every home has one value, the band's dollars divided by its
   homes, so that a unit's households do not differ. Other bound: every band takes the county's
   whole spread of values.
4. **The business's homes** are the tenants' homes and the homes empty for rent.
5. **Opening cash.** The engine gives a household two periods of its needs. An owner's needs no
   longer hold the rent, so an owner opens with two periods of its own housing payments in its
   place: the tax and, if it has one, the mortgage payment. **[Call]**

**The rule of thirds with homes.** **[Director]** **[Record needed]** The law binds the opening.

- **Wealth** is a unit's cash and goods, the value of its shares in businesses, and the carrying
  value of its home less what it owes on it. **All household wealth in the county is the sum over
  the four bands.**
- **Homes cannot carry the whole law.** A mortgage can only lower wealth. Cash is set by need and is
  nearly the same for every household. Shares in businesses are carried at zero, and every owner
  household holds the same share. So today nothing can give the top 1% a third.
- **What each band owes on its homes.** The Federal Reserve's accounts give each wealth group's
  mortgage debt as a share of all of its real estate: 8.4% for the top 1%, 22.7% for the next 9% and
  29.5% for the next 40% (Derived from the national accounts; their use for Wayne is Designed). Each
  ratio is applied to the value of all of the band's homes, and the debt is placed on its mortgaged
  homes, never above their value. The bottom half owes the whole value of its homes. The build
  refuses a band that has debt and no mortgaged home to carry it.
- **The top two bands.** Two figures are solved together, Calibrated, so that the top 1% and the
  next 9% each hold what the next 40% hold: how Wayne's shares in businesses are divided between
  those two bands, and what the shares are worth in the books. The second adds no cash and no rule
  reads it. **The first changes who is paid.** Today the top 1% are one in ten of the owner
  households and receive a tenth of the income from ownership. Solved, they would hold a much larger
  part of each business and receive that part of its pay-out. The gate's report prints the solved
  division beside the Federal Reserve's own figure for the top 1%'s share of business wealth.
  Section 13 asks the Director to choose.
- **The build refuses the opening, naming the band,** when the bottom half's share of all wealth is
  outside the range ADR305 records for 1913 to 2024 (from −1.8% to 3.7%); when a solved figure
  is below zero; when a mortgage is larger than its home's value; when a band has debt and no
  mortgaged home; or when the census tables do not add up.
- **It then measures the finished opening from the accounts,** and refuses it when any of the top
  three bands is more than five points from a third. That cannot happen if the solving is right. The
  check is there to catch a fault in it.
- **What step A's figures say.** Without homes, Wayne's households open with $5.55 billion of cash
  and $0.57 billion of goods, and the bottom half holds $2.99 billion of that. For the bottom half
  to be under 3.7% of all wealth, the upper bands must hold about $78 billion. Whether Wayne's real
  homes, mortgages and businesses give that is not known until the table is built. So the gate of
  section 9 builds the table and works this arithmetic, as a report, before any rule of step B is
  written.

### 7.16 Step B: class reading and nation

- **A unit's livelihood profile gains its home:** whether it rents or owns, the value, what it owes,

- **MIM's words are shown beside the home,** as they are beside each source of income: "Net worth is
  means of production, in which home equity and pension funds have to be counted" (MIM). The text is
  MIM's. Its place on the screen is Designed and is marked so. **[Record needed]**
- **The words beside the bottom 50% change.** "They don't own homes" stays as the law's words. The
  view adds what the unit's own figures show.
- **What each kind did this period** is a table on the county's ledger, by band: tenants and owners,
  and for each the households that paid in full, fell behind, lost a tenancy or a home, bought, and

- **Nation.** The census counts owners and tenants by race of householder. The county's ledger shows

### 7.17 Step B: what the player sees and does

- **The first screen is one table:** the four bands down, tenants and owners across, and in each
  cell the households that paid, are behind, lost a tenancy or a home, and bought. A cell opens to
  its units. Empty units are not shown.
- **One contact a period, as today.** The organizer reaches one position. The player chooses among
  three roads: a household that can claim relief, a food run to the aid partners, or an owner who
  is behind on the tax.
- **The player can find that owner.** The county's public record lists the units with unpaid tax,
  the households behind and their stage. A position's row also says how its households stand on
  their housing: paid up, behind, forfeited, or without a home. Which of a unit's households are
  behind is not tied to a position, because the purse is shared. So a position's figure is its share
  of the unit's count, in proportion to households, by largest remainder.
- **The preview** says what the county would answer: whether the household qualifies, what tax it
  would be excused, and the period in which it would have lost the home.
- **The answer can be read the next period:** the home is off the county's clock, and the unit's
  sentence says so.
- **A unit's home panel:** rents or owns; homes; what was paid for them and what the county taxes
  them at; what is owed, to whom and since when; equity; what was due, paid and left unpaid this
  period; the clock. Each term has a line that says what it is.
- **One sentence for each unit each period,** for example: "Paid food and rent. Let household
  services go. Behind on the tax 3 periods; forfeits in 3."
- **The county's ledger:** its income by source; the homes it took, offered, sold and holds; what
  each kind did.
- **Receipts** for every payment, debt, fee, eviction, foreclosure, sale and exemption.

### 7.18 State, order in the turn, and where the rules live

- **Two compilers.** Step A's opening has one new compiler name: units, and the order of going
  without. Step B's has the next: the census table, homes and debts. Each earlier compiler gives the
  same bytes as before.
- **Homes and debts live in the engine's money book,** as a new housing book. A campaign without
  homes leaves it empty. **[Record needed]**
- **Homes and debts are in the accounts' statements.** A holder's net assets include its homes. A
  debtor's are lower by its debt and the creditor's are higher by the same figure. The engine checks
  every account's net assets at every close, so a payment or a change of holder without its entry
  stops the build.
- **Cash is not created.** Every housing payment is a transfer between two accounts that exist.
- **Order in the close.** Deliveries; public money; wages; goods; utility and rent; the housing
  payments of section 7.10 and new debts; the other services; production; the taxes; then the
  county's steps, evictions, sales, homes taken again and the moves of households between units;
  then the pay-out to owners.
- **After the close,** as today: the organizer's practice, then the session, where a household asks
  for the exemption, then the roster.
- **New records, all hashed with the turn:** the housing book (holdings, tenancies, debts, excused
  households, the land bank, the offers) and the period's housing receipts.
- **The numbers** stay in the scenario's defines file, each with its evidence class.

### 7.19 Quantities that must balance

Every period, or the build refuses:

- The units of a budget group add to the group's households, persons, people at work and people
  looking for work.
- At step A's opening, a group's units add to what the group held before units existed.
- The homes of the units, the housing business and the county add to the opening count.
- Homes lived in are never more than homes held.
- A unit's households are its owners in their homes, its housed tenants and its households without
  a home.
- The households on a unit's debts are never more than its households.
- Each debt closes as its opening figure, plus what was added, less what was paid, less what was
  ended. Each line has a receipt.
- Cash is conserved, and every account's net assets match its statements.

## 13. Questions for the Director

1. **The rule of thirds in step A.** ADR305 says the law binds the opening split of wealth "when
   positions hold accounts of their own". Step A gives them accounts and no homes. The author would
   measure the law in step A and bind it in step B, where homes can carry it (section 7.2). Is that
   accepted? The Director corrected an earlier deferral of this law.
2. **The top's two thirds.** Homes cannot give the top 1% a third. The opening can give Wayne's
   shares in businesses a value in the books and divide them between the top 1% and the next 9%, so
   that all three upper bands hold the same. That changes who receives the income from ownership:
   the top 1% receive much more of it. Or it can give the shares a value and leave them divided as
   today, one part for each owner household. Then the top tenth holds two thirds together, nobody's
   income changes, and the top 1% hold far less than a third. The author recommends the first: the
   law says the top 1% live entirely off investments, and this is what gives them the investments.
3. **Does the tax on a home rank a household for relief?** Today the county lists households by the
   wage tax they paid it. With a tax on homes, an owner pays the county more than a tenant with the
   same wage. If that tax counts, relief moves toward owners. The author recommends that it counts:
   the ruling was "defends its own, weighs by tax", and this is tax paid to the county.
4. **The county's clock.** Michigan's law takes 27 periods from unpaid tax to foreclosure, with
   forfeiture at 13. A run of thirty periods would end as the first home is taken. The author
   recommends 6 periods to forfeiture and 13 to foreclosure, marked Designed, with the law's own

## Appendix C: MIM's line on homes

The game's line is MIM's. These are MIM's own texts, in the project's library of MIM's writings.

- "The key to full rights as an Amerikan citizen is home-ownership. You can't be a settler without a
  house." (MIM Notes 60.)
- "Net worth is means of production, in which home equity and pension funds have to be counted."
  (MIM, on the myths about the white working class.)
- "Household ownership is one reason that there is not a large proletariat within U.$. borders."
  (MIM Notes 239.)
- "The bottom 90 percent of society owns 65 percent of the home ownership wealth." (MIM Notes 112,
  citing Beeghley, *The Structure of Social Stratification in the United States*, 1996.)
- "Home ownership remains a key avenue of parasitism." (MIM, in a debate on the labor aristocracy.)
- "Michigan is fifth in overall foreclosures on mortgage loans and Wayne County was first in 2006."
  (MIM, on the economics of white people in Michigan, 2007.)

MIM treats home ownership as property and as settler standing. It does not sort owners by how much
of the home they have paid for. That agrees with the Director's ruling that an owner is one kind.
