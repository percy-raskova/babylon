## 1. What this slice is for

The Director set the aim: "the goal and intention of this should be to give richness to the "NPC"s
and enhance their agency with NPCs being household units and social classes". The subject is home
ownership, because "a large portion of the labor aristocracy derives wealth from home ownership".

Today a Wayne household has nothing of its own. Appendix A gives the engine facts.

- The county's 700,591 households are four pooled budgets, and three small named ones. Every
  household of a budget shares one purse.
- The County Review counts households in positions. A position has a band and a count. It has no
  money.
- Nobody owns a home and nobody rents one. Housing is a product that every household buys from a
  business, at one price.
- Nothing can be owed. A household that cannot pay goes without, and nothing follows.

So no household can be poorer than its neighbor in the same budget, and none can decide anything
about where it lives.

This slice changes that in two steps. Each step can be played.

1. **A household unit holds its own money.** Each unit gets its own account. Its wages, its relief
   and its tax are its own. What it cannot afford, it goes without. The player can see who is going
   without, reach them, and see in the next period what changed.
2. **A household unit has a home.** It rents or it owns. A tenant pays rent. An owner pays property
   tax and, if the home is mortgaged, the mortgage. What is not paid is owed. A tenant who stays
   behind is put out. An owner who stays behind on the tax loses the home to the county, by the
   clock of Michigan's law, and the county offers it for sale. Anyone with enough money buys one.
   At the opening the homes that are let belong to the top tenth: to households that hold them
   directly, and to the property company whose shares they hold. The player's organizer can bring
   an owner to the county to ask for the exemption the law allows. An owner that qualifies, and
   keeps up the payments on its old debt, keeps the home.

A class is then visible as what its units did: how many paid, fell behind, lost a home, bought one,
or went to the county.

**Three things in the second draft differed from what the Director approved on 20 November.** The
Director ruled on each the same day.

1. **The landlord.** **[Director]** The author's playback listed four kinds of unit: tenant, owner,
   small landlord and large landlord. The second draft had only a business that lets homes, because
   the engine spreads a business's shares over every owner household of the county alike and cannot
   tell a large landlord from any other business owner. The Director ruled that the top 1% and the
   next 9% hold the rented homes both ways: through the property company, and directly, by owner
   units of those two bands. **[Call]** No new kind of unit is needed for it: such a unit holds
   homes that it lets, and is still an owner (section 7.7). That agrees with ADR208 R9: the rentier
   is a fraction of the bourgeoisie, with no new kind of node.
2. **Existing mortgages are in.** **[Director]** Direction 2 said that mortgages and lending would
   wait. The ruling "low equity evolves into equity over time" needs what is owed on the home to be
   in the books and to be paid down. Only new loans wait.
3. **The bottom half's owners open with no equity.** **[Director]** ADR305 says the bottom 50%
   "don't own homes". In Wayne about 64% of households own, so about 100,000 owner households at
   the least are in the bottom half. Under the ruling that an owner is one kind they are owners like
   any other. The law's words are read as "they hold no home equity at the opening": such a
   household owes on its home what the home is worth (section 7.15).

## 3. What is known

### What the engine holds

- **Households are counted groups.** Wayne has four large budget groups and three small named ones.
  A group has one account. The word "owner" in a group's name means a household with a share in a
  business. That share is Designed: one household in ten.
- **A position** is a group's households at one crew, with a band, the people at work, the people
  looking for work and the relief units held. The recorded opening has 111 positions over 41 crews.
- **The bands** are set once: 7,006 households in the top 1%, 63,054 in the next 9%, 280,236 in the
  next 40% and 350,295 in the bottom 50%.
- **Every money path is keyed by the account that is paid or charged**: wages, wage tax, county
  support, income from ownership and purchases. Nothing in the running game needs to know how many
  accounts a county has.
- **Housing** is a service called Housing provision. Each household buys one unit a period from one
  business fixed at the opening. The opening price is $1,450 and the price moves by a rule.
- **A business's shares** are carried at zero. They are never priced or sold. Every private
  business is a member of a company of its trade, national or regional. Each period a business pays
  its company half of the earnings it has kept, within the cash it has above its payroll. The
  company pays the households that hold a part of it, in every county where it has members. The
  parts are fixed at the opening, and every owner household of a county holds the same part.
- **The housing business in Wayne** is a private business. It opened with 27,283 people at work.
  The service needs one hour of labor for a home, so it kept 4,379 from the fourth period. Wayne
  also has a small public one.
- **The county** taxes wages and business income at 15%. It has no tax on property. Tax is collected
  at the end of the close, after a household has bought what it needs. A payer has one tax policy.
- **Nothing can be owed** except a wage that an employer has not yet paid. There is no bank.
- **When a household is short,** the engine buys goods before services, and inside each it follows
  the order of internal identifiers. So what a poor household gives up first is an accident.

### What the sources say

Appendix B gives each source.

- **Who owns in Wayne.** In the project's held census table, labelled 2023, 447,120 of 693,446
  households own their home: 64%. Of households with a white householder 77% own (279,218 of
  362,903). Of households with a Black householder 47% own (124,369 of 262,653). The slice builds
  its own pinned table from the 2024 files, and these figures are provisional until then.
- **Whose wealth homes are.** In the Federal Reserve's accounts for the second quarter of 2026 the
  next 40% hold 46.6% of the homes that households live in, and those homes are 37% of what they
  own. The bottom 50% hold 9.7% of them and 22.6% of all mortgage debt. The accounts count a rented
  home that a household holds as business wealth, and not as real estate.
- **How many owe on the home.** In the 2025 Survey of Consumer Finances 65.6% of families own their
  home and 42.0% owe on it, so about two owners in three have a mortgage.
- **How a home is lost in Wayne.** Michigan's law hands unpaid property tax to the county treasurer
  each March. A fee of 4% and interest of 1% a month are added. After one year the home is forfeited
  and the interest rises to 1.5% a month. After another year the county forecloses, and sells the
  home at auction in the autumn. Wayne County foreclosed on about 25,000 properties in 2015, more
  than 9,000 of them occupied.
- **Who lets homes.** In the whole country individual owners, alone or jointly, hold about 35% of
  the homes in rental properties. Companies of several kinds, trusts and other bodies hold the rest,
  and some of those companies are one person's own. Individuals hold about 65% of rented single
  houses and about 6% of the homes in large properties (2024). In Detroit a study of 2026, on the
  records of 2024 and 2025, counted 54,365 landlords. 93% of them owned one or two properties and
  held about three quarters of the homes it counted. Its count of homes is well above the census's.
  The study of 2022 gave the 5% who owned five or more properties a third of the rented homes, and
  the authors say their method changed.
- **Who buys what the county takes.** Among Detroit properties in the treasurer's sales of 2005 to
  2015, investors who bought in bulk made about nine purchases in ten.
- **MIM's line.** "The key to full rights as an Amerikan citizen is home-ownership. You can't be a
  settler without a house." (MIM Notes 60.) "Net worth is means of production, in which home equity
  and pension funds have to be counted." Appendix C gives the texts.

## 4. Goals, non-goals and anti-goals

**Goals**

1. Every Wayne household unit has money of its own, and every dollar a unit receives or pays can be
   read in its own account.
2. In step A the player can see who is going without, by count, reach them, and read the result in
   the next period.
3. A unit's housing situation is its own: it rents, or it owns, with a value, what it owes and its
   equity. Equity is a number that moves. A unit of the top tenth may also hold homes it lets.
4. A household's own choices follow from its own figures, and a receipt explains each: what it pays
   first, whether it buys, whether it goes to the county. What happens to it is kept apart from what
   it chose (section 7.14).
5. Arrears are really owed. A tenancy really ends. A home really changes hands, with the money and
   the debts that go with it.
6. The county gains a tax on homes in its own books, and acts on unpaid tax by the clock of
   Michigan's law.
7. A class can be read as what its units did in the period, by count.
8. The opening obeys the rule of thirds with homes in it, or the build refuses it by name.
9. Every count and every dollar adds back, at every grouping, every period. Every rule is exact and
   repeatable.
10. Each period the player still makes one choice, and from step B it has a third road: the county's
    relief, the food run, or a household's home.
11. Every rented home has a holder. At the opening it is in the top tenth: a household unit, or the
    property company whose shares the top tenth holds. A landlord is read from what a unit holds
    and what it is paid. It is never a label.
12. Every legal clock, fee and remedy is Michigan's own, kept in one record for the state, so that
    another state can differ. Where the game departs from the law, section 13 names it.

**Non-goals for this slice**

- New loans, buying on credit and land contracts.
- A sale by the owner, and a business that buys homes.
- Foreclosure by a lender.
- A landlord's own choices beyond paying and buying: setting a rent, refusing a tenant, selling a
  home, or keeping one empty.
- Rented homes held by the next 40% or the bottom 50% at the opening.
- Homes built or pulled down. The number of homes is fixed at the opening.
- Nation on a unit. Tenure by race of householder is shown as an observed table. No unit carries it
  and no rule reads it.
- Bodies of owners, of tenants or of landlords at the county, and a county board that changes the
  tax.
- Units or homes outside Wayne.
- Farm land, forest land, public land and the land ledger (section 11).

**Anti-goals: the slice has failed if it contains any of these**

- A line drawn between owners by the size of their equity.
- A unit that is called a landlord as a kind, or rented homes with no holder.
- A clock or a fee of the law changed to fit the length of a run.
- A household's money or debt that is only a display and that no payment reads.
- A rule that decides for a household from the average of its group. Section 5 says what a
  household's own figures are.
- A class label that is assigned by hand and then drives behavior.
- A home value that follows a market the game does not have.
- An individual person or a single house simulated as an object.
- A home that is taken or sold with no account that pays and no account that is paid.
- An opening bent quietly to meet the law.
- A census figure shown as if the game had measured it, or a designed figure shown as observed.
- A screen that lists every unit and every figure at once.

## 5. Rules this design must obey

- **The four counting rules** of ADR302 hold for homes, debts and money as they do for households.
- **A household's own figures.** **[Call]** **[Record needed]** A household's labor figures are its
  position's: at work, looking for work, relief units, hours. Its money and its home are its
  unit's, because the unit is the smallest thing that holds money or a home. A unit's purse is spent
  for whole households: it pays, or buys, for as many of its households as its cash covers, and the
  rest fall behind or go without. That is a declared split of one total, which is ADR302's third
  rule. A figure shown "for each household" is a rate of two of the unit's totals, which is its
  second.
- **The law of the state.** **[Record needed]** The Director ruled on the county's clock: "lets
  keep it according to the law", because "once we program in states each state has different laws
  and that will lead to different behaviors in different jurisdictions". **[Director]** The author
  reads that reason as covering every clock, fee and remedy that a state's law fixes. **[Call]**
  Such a figure is the law's own: Derived from the statute, named with its section, and never
  changed to make a run shorter. The figures are kept in one record for each state, so that another
  state can differ, and Wayne reads Michigan's. Where the law leaves a figure to a board, a court
  or a contract, the game designs it and says so. Where the game departs from the law, it says that
  too (Appendix E, section 13).
- **Article II of the Constitution.** The count of homes in a county is ground. Who holds a home is
  an overlay that play can change.
- **Article IV.** Every value carries its evidence class. A Designed value says so on screen.
- **The Aleksandrov test.** Every new object names the material thing it stands for: a household's
  purse, a house, a rent, a tax bill, a debt.
- **Amendment AM** (proposed, ADR300). No rule has an imposed curve. A rule decides for one
  household, and the answer for a unit is the count of households the rule admits.
- **ADR184.** What a body spends belongs to that body. The county forecloses and sells as the county
  administration, from its own account.
- **ADR182.** What exists is public. A unit's dollar figures are earned by contact, as in the County
  Review. Counts are public.
- **ADR098.** Only data that may be redistributed enters a committed source. The census tables are
  federal public data. Detroit's parcel file prints "No License Provided" and is not used.
- **The audit's rules for new data** (12 August, section 8): pin one vintage with its hash; carry
  withheld values as withheld; never average a rate; name the consumer before the download.
- **Determinism.** Whole numbers and exact fractions. No random draws. No order taken from an
  identifier.

### How homes enter

| | Approach | Why it was or was not chosen |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Homes as figures on today's roster. Money stays pooled and a declared rule says who falls behind | Fastest. But the unit has no money, so its choice would be the rule's |
| 2 | Each unit gets its own account, then its home | **Approved by the Director.** The unit's choices are real money |
| 3 | Households split by housing situation in every county | Real money everywhere. But the unit would lose its workplace, and Wayne's roster would be rebuilt |

### Smaller choices, with the bound not taken

- **A ledger inside the pooled account** was rejected. Every payment reads the real account, so one
  unit could still spend another's money. It would be a display.
- **A unit for every position** was rejected. Its name would change at every hire and layoff, and
  money would have to follow.
- **A home as a stock of goods** was rejected. Goods are bought, shipped and used up. A home has a
  holder, a value for tax and a household living in it.
- **A bank** was rejected for this slice. Existing mortgages need one record each and a payment.
  They need no credit.
- **A home value that follows prices** was rejected. A home is carried at what was paid for it, and
  that changes only when the home changes hands.
- **A shorter clock for the county** was rejected by the Director. The author proposed 6 and 13
  periods, so that a run of thirty periods would show a sale. The law's clock stands, and the
  recorded runs are made longer (section 9).
- **A clock counted from the first missed bill** was the author's first reading of the law's clock.
  It is shorter than the law. The Director chose the law's yearly return (section 7.11).
- **Leaving people in a foreclosed home until a buyer acts,** as Wayne's county has often done, was
  the author's pick. The Director chose that they leave at once.
- **A landlord that is only a business** was the second draft's form. The Director ruled that the
  top tenth holds the rented homes both ways (section 7.7).
- **Shares left evenly divided** among the households that hold them was rejected by the Director.
  The top tenth would hold two thirds together, and the top 1% far less than a third.

## 7. The design

Sections 7.1 to 7.6 are step A. Sections 7.7 to 7.19 are step B.

### 7.1 Step A: the unit

A **household unit** is a set of households that share one purse and that the player can follow
from period to period. It answers three questions, and the answers never change.

| Question | Answers |
|---|---|
| Which budget group? | One of the engine's household groups in Wayne |
| Which crew were its people bound to at the opening? | One workplace and one way of being paid there. Or none |
| Which band was it in at the opening? | The top 1%, the next 9%, the next 40% or the bottom 50% |

- **The numbers at work, looking for work and holding relief units are states inside a unit.** They
  move every period. The unit does not. The engine never moves a person to another workplace, so a
  hire or a layoff happens inside one unit and moves no money between units.
- **A position** is now a row inside a unit: the unit's households with the same number at work,
  looking and holding relief units. The County Review's rules run on positions as they do today.
- **One purse for a unit.** **[Call]** The households of a unit share cash although some have work
  and some do not. The engine pays one crew as one amount, and that is the floor of the grain.
- **Each unit is an account of the kind the engine already has.** **[Record needed]** No new kind of
  account is added. The unit's identity is made from its group, its opening crew and its band.
- **Wayne only.** **[Call]** The other counties keep their budgets as they are. A campaign asks for
  units in its policy file, and the build that makes them has a new compiler name (section 7.18).
  Every earlier compiler gives the same bytes as before.
- **The three small named groups** (the claimants, the aid donors and the aid partners) each stay
  one unit. The hearing, the aid mandates and the organizer name them. A named group stays one
  unit even where its households lie in two bands. Its positions keep their bands, and a figure
  shown by band divides the unit's total between them in proportion to households, by largest
  remainder. **[Call]** Corrected after approval: the first form refused a campaign whose opening
  would cut a named group, and today's Wayne has the four aid donors in two bands.
- **Collective residence** stays one unit with no households, as today.
- **Two words.** **[Call]** On screen the new object is always a "household unit" and the county's
  support is always a "relief unit". The budget groups read "with a business share" and "no business
  share", so that "owner" is free for homes.

### 7.2 Step A: the opening

The opening divides each Wayne budget group among its units. Nothing new is decided about money.

- **Bands and units come from one rule.** The build runs the County Review's own placement on the
  pooled groups. Each distinct group, crew and band among its rows is a unit.
- **A crew that spans two bands becomes two crews,** one for each unit, so that a unit's wages are
  paid to it as one amount.
- **Cash, goods, needs and support follow the counts.** The engine sets a household's opening cash,
  its pantry and its needs from its persons and households, and its support from its job-seekers.
  So the units of a group add back to today's group exactly, with no rule of their own.
- **Persons are split by a declared rule.** **[Call]** A unit's persons are its labor force, plus a
  share of the group's other adults and children in proportion to its households, by largest
  remainder. The build refuses a unit with fewer persons than households.
- **Shares in businesses are split in two stages.** First a business's shares go to the budget
  groups by households, as today. Then a group's shares go to its units by households, by largest
  remainder. The same is done for the ages the engine keeps. One stage would move each group's total
  by rounding.
- **The bands are placed before the money is wired.** **[Record needed]** Today the roster sets the
  bands after the opening has wired every account (Appendix A). Units need their bands first, so
  the opening runs the County Review's placement before it wires the money. The roster is then
  made from the rows that placement gave. It is not placed a second time: placing again on units
  would move households between bands. Corrected after approval.
- **One unit of each group keeps the group's own account:** the unit with the most households.
  **[Call]** The aid mandates, the hearing and the organizer name that account. It also keeps
  what the counts do not explain: the engine holds the aid partners' opening cash and pantry in
  their parent group's account, and they stay there. Every other unit's cash and pantry follow
  its own counts. Corrected after approval.
- **Wealth is measured, and the law binds in step B.** **[Director]** ADR305 names a measurement as
  the first task of this slice: the share of household wealth the top tenth holds, set beside the
  law's two thirds. Step A makes it. ADR305 also says the law binds the opening split of wealth
  "when positions hold accounts of their own". Step A cannot meet that. A household's only wealth in
  step A is two periods of cash and a pantry, nearly the same for every household, so the four bands
  open with about 1%, 9%, 41% and 49% of it. Giving each of the first three a third would leave the
  bottom half with almost nothing in hand in the first period. Homes, what is owed on them, and the
  businesses' shares are what can carry the law, and they are valued in step B (section 7.15). The
  Director accepted this on 20 November: "Measure now, bind with homes" (section 13).
- **What the top 1% are paid is measured too.** **[Director]** It is part of the choice "Measure
  now, bind with homes". The law's rule of who works leaves the top 1% with no wage, and today a
  household of the top 1% is paid from ownership exactly what a household of the next 9% is paid.
  So step A's first report also shows what a household of the top 1% is paid from ownership beside
  what its needs cost. It shows the same for every band. **[Call]** Step B divides the shares
  (section 7.15). If the report shows the top 1% going without in step A, the division can move
  forward by a small amendment.

### 7.3 Step A: the running game

- **Wages** are paid to the unit whose people earned them. **Wage tax** is charged to that unit.
  **County support** is paid to the unit whose households hold the relief units. **Income from
  ownership** is paid to the unit that holds the shares. None of this needs new code: each path
  already pays the account it is given.
- **A unit that is short goes without, in a declared order.** **[Call]** **[Record needed]** It pays
  for food first, then materials, then utility, then housing, then household services, then public

- **A household that leaves** is taken from its unit by the rule the County Review has today:
  from the unit's rows with nobody in the labor force. **[Record needed]** Corrected after

### 7.7 Step B: kinds of unit

**Kinds.** A unit is a **tenant** or an **owner**. **An owner is one kind, whatever its equity.**
**[Director]** The kind says how the unit's households are housed. It does not say what else the
unit holds. The rest of this section is the author's unless it is marked. **[Call]**

- **The kind is a fourth answer in a unit's identity.** An owner unit has a fifth: whether its homes
  were mortgaged at the opening. No rule reads that answer. It only says which owner units have a
  mortgage payment to make. Two owner units that differ in it are the same kind on every screen.
- **Every unit of step A exists in each form from the opening:** tenant, owner with a mortgage,
  owner without. A form with no households is empty, as collective residence is, and is not shown.
  So a household that changes its housing always has a unit to go to, and no account is made in the
  middle of a run.
- **A crew is split once more,** so that each form of a unit has its own crew and is paid its own
  wages.
- **From step B the purse is the form's.** Each form is an account. Where sections 7.8 to 7.17 say
  "its unit's cash" or "its unit's income", they mean the form the household is in. One rule reads
  the whole unit: a unit that owes the county tax in any of its forms cannot buy (section 7.13).
- **A household that moves between two forms takes with it its share of everything its form
  holds:** persons, people at work and looking, relief units, cash, goods, hours, the homes it holds
  and does not live in, its share of each debt it is behind on, and its place in the roster. Each
  is split in proportion to households, by largest remainder. One exception: a household that buys
  a home takes the reserve it was tested for, and no more of the cash (section 7.13).
- **Its wages and its part of the businesses move with it too.** **[Record needed]** The engine
  fixes each crew's parts, and each account's part of every pay-out, at the opening, and has nothing
  that changes them afterwards (Appendix A). So the housing book keeps, for each form of a unit,
  the workers and the parts of businesses its households carry, and moves them with the households.
  After the wages are paid, and after each pay-out, the forms of one unit settle between themselves,
  so that each form's wages and income from ownership are those of the workers and the parts it now
  carries. **[Call]** The other bound is to move the engine's own parts, which changes the money
  book for every county.
- **A household that buys a home to live in joins the owner form without a mortgage.**
- **The three small named groups are tenants,** whole. The placement of owners runs after them. The
  build refuses a campaign in which it would cut one.

**Landlords.** **[Director]** "the top 1% and the top 9% can also be landlords and own multiple
homes they don't live in ... especially the 1% thru property management companies". Every rented
home has a holder, in one of two ways.

- **Through the property company.** The engine's private housing business in Wayne holds part of the

- **Directly.** At the opening owner units of the top 1% and of the next 9% hold homes that their

- **The company manages every rented home, whoever holds it.** A tenant's landlord is always the
  company (section 7.8). So a tenant unit does not need to know who holds its homes.
- **Who holds how many at the opening** is section 7.15. Afterwards anyone with the cash can buy a
  home from the county (section 7.13).

### 7.8 Step B: the home

**[Record needed]** A home is a record, held by an account: a unit, the property company or the
county.

| A holding records | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Homes | A whole number |
| Lived in by the holder, let, empty | Whole numbers that add to the homes |
| Carrying value | What was paid for the homes. It changes only when a home changes hands, to the price paid |
| The county's value | The whole value at which the county taxes the home. It stays with the home and does not follow the price paid. Michigan's law taxes half of it (Appendix E) |

- **At the opening the county's value is the carrying value.** For the homes owners live in it is
  the observed value, placed as section 7.15 says.
- **A rented home** opens at the value of the cheapest owned homes in the county: the mean value of
  the bottom band's owned homes. **[Call]** Designed, because the census values only homes their
  owners live in. It is the same for a home the company holds and for one a unit holds. Homes enter
  their holder's books at the opening against its capital, as every home and every debt does.
- **An owner's housing need is met by the home it lives in.** It buys no Housing provision.
- **The property company is the landlord of every rented home.** **[Record needed]** A tenancy ties
  a tenant household to the company, and not to the holder of the home. It is a count on the tenant
  unit. Every period the let homes of all holders equal the housed tenant households.
- **The tenant's rent** is the purchase of Housing provision it makes today, at the posted price. A
  form buys it for its housed tenant households only. Owners and households without a home buy
  none. A tenant that cannot pay still lives in the home, and its housing need is met: the service
  is supplied and the price is owed (section 7.9). **[Record needed]** That is a new path in the
  engine, which today supplies a service only against cash set aside for it. The price rule must
  then count only the homes that were paid for.
- **When the company cannot supply every home,** the homes it cannot serve are shared over the
  tenant forms in proportion to their housed households, by largest remainder. No rent is owed for
  them, and the tenancies stand.
- **The company pays each holder its rent.** **[Call]** **[Record needed]** The engine check found
  that a business's pay-out cannot do this: it goes to the company of its trade, by parts fixed at
  the opening (Appendix A). So the rent is paid over by a payment of its own kind. Each period,
  after the service is supplied and the business has bought its inputs, and before any tax:
  1. The company counts the rent it received in the period from Wayne's tenants: the cash paid for
     Housing provision and the cash paid on rent debts. Rent that is owed and not paid is not
     counted.
  2. It takes off what the service cost it in the period for every let home: its labor and its
     inputs.
  3. If anything is left, it is shared over every let home, the company's own included, in
     proportion to homes, by largest remainder. The count of let homes is the one at the start of
     the close. If nothing is left, no holder is paid in that period, and nothing is carried
     forward.
  4. The company keeps the part of its own homes. From the part of each other holder it keeps a
     management part of 10% (Designed) and pays the holder the rest. The payment is an expense of
     the company and income of the holder.
  5. It never pays more than the cash it has above its payroll. A part it cannot pay is shown as not
     paid, and is not owed.
- **Rent is shared, not traced.** Because the tenancy is with the company, a holder is paid for its
  let homes out of all the rent received. When tenants fall behind, every holder's rent is lower by
  the same part. A rent debt is owed to the company, and a rent debt written off is the company's
  loss. The other bound is a tenancy kept for each holder: thousands more rows, which no rule would
  read.
- **Homes are drawn by a declared split.** When a tenancy ends, for any reason, one let home
  becomes empty. It is drawn from the holders in proportion to their let homes, by largest
  remainder. When a household takes a home to rent, the home is drawn the same way from the empty
  homes of the property company and of the units. Every empty home they hold is for rent, whatever
  it was before. The county's homes are never let.
- **The holder's rent is taxed as a business's income is.** **[Call]** The company holds back the
  county's tax on business income, 15%, from the rent it pays over, and pays it to the county. So
  the rent of a home is taxed whether the company or a household holds it. The other bound is to
  leave it untaxed.
- **Equity is a number:** the carrying value of the homes a unit holds, less what is owed on them,
  less arrears. For one household it is the unit's total divided by its households. It is shown and
  no rule reads it.
- **Equity grows** as the mortgage is paid down. It falls when arrears are added.
- **No home is built or pulled down.** The homes held by units, by the property company and by the
  county add to the opening count every period.
- **Wayne's rented homes must be served by one business, and it must serve only them.** The engine
  chooses each household's seller as the nearest in the state, and nobody has checked which that is
  for Wayne. The build refuses a campaign in which Wayne's tenants buy Housing provision from a
  business outside Wayne or from more than one, or in which that business sells to households
  outside Wayne. If the check fails, the design returns to the Director.
- **The property company belongs to a larger company.** In the engine every private business is a
  member of a company of its trade, national or regional, and its pay-out goes there (Appendix A).
  So what the property company earns on the homes it holds is paid, through that company, to the
  households that hold a part of it in every county where it has members. Wayne's top tenth
  receives its own part. The rent on homes that Wayne's units hold directly is paid to them in
  Wayne. The Director chose to keep it so (section 13). **[Director]**

**What is paid for housing**

- **An owner's upkeep is the materials need it has today.** **[Call]** The other bound is an upkeep

- **Rent is the engine's price.** **[Call]** It opens at $1,450 for 28 days, Designed, and moves by

  at most 18 mills on the taxable value, and the taxable value is at most half of the home's value

  is the law's ceiling, used as the rate (Designed). Detroit's own levy is not confirmed.
- **The housing book bills the tax,** and not the engine's tax policy: the engine allows a payer
  one policy, and a unit has its wage tax (Appendix A). A holder is billed for every home it holds:
  lived in, let or empty. The bill has its own receipt. The tax on its homes does not lower the

- **All of it goes to the county's account.** **[Call]** That account already pays every government
  workplace in the county: the city, the schools and the county itself. The other bound is the
  county government's own share alone, which was $392.5 million in its 2024 fiscal year.
- **The property company pays the tax on its homes.** Unpaid, it is owed with the same fees, and the
  county's clock runs on those homes as on any other. **[Call]** The second draft kept businesses

  law as covering this. The county's own homes are not taxed.
- **The mortgage.** **[Director]** Existing mortgages only. No new loan is made. **[Call]** for the

- **The lender** is a business that exists: the business-services business in Wayne with the most

  **[Call]** Principal repaid is not income. The lender takes no home in this slice. Michigan's law

**The debt.** **[Director]** **[Record needed]** What is not paid is owed. A debt records the

members, the period it began in, and the balance. The same figure is the creditor's claim. It is in
both sets of books and the two always agree.

- **A debt belongs to the members that fell behind together.** **[Call]** The members are whole

### 7.10 Step B: paying

**The order.** **[Call]** **[Record needed]** A household pays in this order.

1. Food, then materials.
2. Utility.
3. **Whatever would cost it a home in this period if it stayed unpaid:** a rent debt or a tax debt
   whose clock runs out at this close. The home it lives in comes first.
4. This period's rent or mortgage payment.
5. Its other housing debts, the oldest first.
6. Household services and public provision.
7. The taxes: this period's property tax on the home it lives in, then on the homes it lets, then
   on its empty homes; then the wage tax.

- **Step 3 is the household's own priority.** It reads the household's own clocks. Because the
  county's clock is long, an owner that is short pays its mortgage and its other needs and lets the
  tax wait, until the tax is about to cost the home. That is how homes are lost in Wayne, and no
  rule says so. A part of an old debt that the exemption has cut is paid at this step too
  (section 7.12).
- **The tax on a home comes before the wage tax,** because unpaid it becomes a debt that can cost
  the home, and an unpaid wage tax does not.
- **Whole households pay, and whole homes are paid for.** When a unit's cash does not cover a
  payment for all the households that owe it, as many whole households pay as the cash covers. The
  rest fall behind. They begin a debt with that period on it, or the bill is added to the debt they
  are already in (section 7.9). The households already behind are the first to miss. The tax on let
  and empty homes is paid for as many whole homes as the cash covers.
- **A household that is behind stays behind until its debt is paid.** Cash paid on debts goes to the
  oldest first. A debt paid in full stops its clock.
- **The property company pays in its own order:** its wages, as today; the holders' rent
  (section 7.8); the tax on as many of its homes as its cash above its payroll covers, its let
  homes before its empty ones; then the engine's tax on its income.

### 7.11 Step B: falling behind, and what follows

| Owed | The creditor's clock | What follows |
|---|---|---|
| Rent | 2 periods (Designed, inside the law's range: an order to leave on day 23 at the earliest, and at about day 51 when the court stays the case for help with the rent; Appendix E) | The tenancy ends |
| Property tax | Michigan's three stages, counted from the yearly return: delinquent, forfeited 13 periods later, foreclosed 27 periods after the return (Derived). **[Director]** | The home passes to the county |
| Mortgage | None in this slice | A debt and a fee |

- **Eviction.** When a household's rent debt is 2 periods old, its tenancy ends. A let home becomes
  empty (section 7.8). The debt is written off: the company books a loss and the household is
  released. **[Call]** This departs from the law, which also gives the landlord a judgment for the
  money. It is a remedy of the law that this slice leaves out (section 11).
- **A household without a home** stays in its unit and is counted there. Its housing need goes
  unmet, and it loses 20 hours for each period without a home: the hours the engine takes today for
  an unmet unit of food. **[Call]** The hours are Designed.
- **Taking a home again.** **[Call]** A household without a home takes an empty home to rent when
  its form's cash, after the period's payments, covers two periods of the rent for it: the first
  period and one in hand. The home is drawn as section 7.8 says. When more households can than
  there are empty homes, the homes are shared among the forms in proportion to those households, by
  largest remainder. A household that lost its home in this close cannot take one until the next.
- **The yearly return.** **[Director]** A game year is 13 periods. At the close of the third period
  of each game year, the tax that was billed in the year before and is still unpaid becomes
  delinquent. That is the law's 1 March, when unpaid tax is returned to the county's treasurer. A
  fee of 4% is added, and interest of 0.92% a period runs from that close (Derived, MCL 211.78a).
  The engine's year is not a calendar, so the place of the return in it is Designed: the third
  period holds the sixtieth day.
- **Forfeiture** is at the return one year later, 13 periods on. A fee of $175 is added for each
  home, and the interest is 1.38% a period, counted back to the first return: Michigan's 1.5% a
  month (Derived, MCL 211.78g).
- **Foreclosure** is 27 periods after the first return, at the close of the fourth period of the
  game year: the law's 31 March (Derived, MCL 211.78k). A debt paid in full before that close stops
  it. So the tax a household misses in its first game year takes the home at period 43.
- **What foreclosure does.** The home passes to the county. This holds for every holder: an owner,
  a unit that lets homes, and the property company. The judgment ends every claim on the home
  (MCL 211.78k): every tax debt on it; the mortgage, on which the lender books a loss; and, by the
  statute's wording, the lease. No court decision on the lease was found. The county carries the
  home at the debt that was owed on it: the tax, the fees and the interest.
- **The people in the home must leave at once.** **[Director]** "this leads to more dramatic
  events, more displacement, more opportunity to intervene so its a more interesting gameplay
  assumption". The statute gives the county possession from the next day (MCL 211.78g) and leaves
  the rest to it. So an owner's households move to the tenant form of their unit, without a home.
  When a let home is taken, as many tenant households are without a home as the debt records homes
  that were let. They are drawn from the tenant forms in proportion to their housed households, by
  largest remainder. Every home the county holds is empty. Wayne's own practice has often been to
  leave people where they were. That, and the programs that let them buy the home back, are later
  work (section 11).
- **A mortgage that is not paid** adds a debt and a fee, and nothing more in this slice.
- **A household that leaves the county.** **[Call]** A tenant's tenancy ends, and its share of the
  form's rent debts is ended with a receipt, as a loss to the company. An owner's home stays with
  its form, empty and still taxed. What the household owed on it, in tax and on the mortgage,
  becomes a debt on the home, counted in homes, with its clock unchanged. Its part of the
  businesses and the homes it let stay with the form too. If the form cannot carry them, the tax
  falls behind and the county's clock takes them. So an empty house reaches the land bank the way
  it does in Wayne.

### 7.12 Step B: the county

The county administration is the actor the County Review made. It gains these.

| | |
|---|---|
| Income | The property tax, and the tax held back from the holders' rent, shown in its books beside the wage tax and the tax on businesses. The books today show only what the county pays out |
| Acts | It adds the law's fees to unpaid tax. It forfeits and forecloses by the law's clock. It offers the homes it holds for sale. It grants the exemption to a household that asks and qualifies |
| The land bank | The homes it holds and has not sold |
| Its public record | The holders with unpaid tax, the homes behind for each, whether they are lived in, let or empty, and their stage: unpaid, delinquent or forfeited. In Wayne the treasurer publishes these lists each year |

- **The county's limit on spending does not change.** **[Call]** It is fixed at the opening, as the
  County Review left it. So the new tax adds to the county's cash and does not raise what it spends.
  Relief is the county's cash or its limit, whichever is lower, less its first class. The tax
  therefore moves the period in which the county first runs short. The other bound is to raise the
  limit by the tax, which is a county board's act and belongs to a later slice.
- **The tax on a home ranks a household for relief.** **[Director]** **[Record needed]** The
  county's list ranks a household by the tax collected from its form: the wage tax and the tax on
  the homes it holds. The Director's reason: "IRL local politicians and governments do housing
  relief and what not through the tax system and home ownership". The tax held back from a
  holder's rent counts as well. **[Call]** So relief moves toward owners, and a tenant with the
  same wage stands below them. An owner form that falls behind on the tax stands lower, as a unit
  short of its wage tax does in step A (section 7.3). The rule is ADR301's: a household is ranked
  by the tax the county collected from it. That tax now has more than one part. Each further part
  is a rate: the tax collected from the form, divided by its households. One thing follows: today
  the list gives a household with nobody at work no place, and now its place is zero only when
  every part is zero.
- **The exemption.** Michigan's law excuses the home of a household in poverty from the tax, one
  year at a time (MCL 211.7u). It also lets the county cut the old tax debt of a household so
  excused, once (MCL 211.78g). The law fixes the frame. A local board fixes the levels, and the
  game designs those (Appendix E). The second draft's form was the author's own. This one follows
  the law. **[Call]**
  - **Who can ask:** an owner household, for the home it lives in. A let home, an empty home and
    the property company cannot. That is the law's rule.
  - **Where:** at the county's session. Only a household the player's organizer has reached can
    come, as in the County Review. It comes when one period of the tax on its home is at least the
    floor of ADR301: two hours at the wage of its crew at the opening.
  - **It qualifies** when its income is at or under the federal poverty line for its persons, when
    its cash and goods are at or under $12,000, and when it holds no part of a business and no home
    but the one it lives in. The poverty line is the lowest level the law lets a board set. The
    figure is Derived, and its use as the level is Designed. The $12,000 is Detroit's level for
    what a household may own, and its use for the whole county is Designed. Income is that of the
    closed period, taken to a year. Both are rates of the form's totals.
  - **It is excused the whole tax on that home for the game year.** The bills of that year that it
    has missed are cancelled, and none is written for the rest of the year. In the next year it
    must ask again, which needs the organizer again. The law also allows three quarters, a half
    and a quarter. The game grants the whole.
  - **Its delinquent tax of earlier years is cut, once.** It is cut to 5% of the county's value of
    the home, when it is more than that. The law's figure is 10% of the taxable value of the year
    before, and the taxable value is at most half of the home's value: the game takes it at that
    ceiling (Designed). The fees and the interest are cancelled. What is left is paid in 39 equal
    parts, three years, with no interest. A part is paid at step 3 of section 7.10. That the county
    grants the cut to every household it excuses is Designed. Wayne has a plan called Pay As You
    Stay, and whether it rests on this power is not confirmed.
  - **While the household pays those parts, the county holds its home off the clock.** That hold is
    the law's for a payment plan (MCL 211.78q). Its use here is Designed. The plan runs on if the
    exemption is not asked for again.
  - **If it misses a part,** the old debt returns as it was, with its fees and interest, less the
    parts paid. No interest is counted for the periods of the hold. The law puts such a home in the
    county's next list for foreclosure, which is filed by 15 June. So the home is taken at the
    first yearly foreclosure that comes 10 periods or more after the miss. A part is missed by
    whole households: those the form's cash did not cover.
  - Only the debts on the home the household lives in are divided, between its excused members and
    the others. Only the excused part is cancelled or cut, and only its clock is held.
  - No ranking applies. The county grants it to every household that asks and qualifies.

### 7.13 Step B: buying

- **The county offers each home it took twice, as Michigan's law has it** (MCL 211.78m). The first
  offer is 6 periods after the foreclosure, at the whole debt that was owed on the home: the tax,
  the fees and the interest. That is the law's minimum bid. A home not sold is offered once more in
  the next period, at the county's opening bid of $500, because the law sets no minimum for the
  last sale. After that it stays in the land bank. The law's window for the sales is 4 to 8 periods
  after the foreclosure (Derived). The two months and the $500 are Wayne's practice, and their use
  is Designed. No price is found by bidding, because the game has no market for homes.
- **Anyone with the cash may buy.** **[Director]** The limits that follow are the author's, or the
  law's where it is named. **[Call]** A household decides from its form's own figures. The reserves
  are Designed.
  - **To live in it.** A tenant household, housed or not, buys one home when its form's cash, after
    the period's payments, covers the price and a reserve for it: two periods of an owner's needs,
    with the tax on the home. If it was housed, its tenancy ends. It takes that reserve with it to
    the owner form, and no more of the cash.
  - **To let it.** An owner household of any band buys homes when its form's cash, after the
    period's payments and after two periods of the form's own needs are set aside, covers the price
    of each and two periods of the tax on it. At the opening only the top tenth holds rented homes.
    After it, any owner with the cash can come to hold one.
  - **A household that is behind on a debt does not buy.**
  - **A unit that owes the county tax in any of its forms cannot buy.** That is the law's rule for
    bidders (Derived, MCL 211.78m).
  - The property company and other businesses do not buy in this slice.
- **The order of a sale.** Each period the county offers its lots, the oldest foreclosure first,
  and a first offer before a second. The price of a home in a lot is the lot's price divided by its
  homes. For each lot every form says how many homes it could take. If that is more than the lot
  holds, the homes are shared among the forms in proportion to what each could take, by largest
  remainder. The cash is paid before the next lot is worked. **[Call]** The other bound is the
  richest first, as at an auction.
- **A household that buys to live becomes an owner** (section 7.7). Its home is carried at the price
  it paid. The county's value does not change, so the tax on a cheap home can be large beside its
  price. That is what the studies of Detroit found (Appendix B). Michigan's law resets the taxed
  value after a sale to the assessor's figure, and not to the price (MCL 211.27a).
- **A home bought to let** is empty until a household without a home takes it (section 7.11). Its
  holder pays the tax on it from the first period.
- **No surplus arises.** Michigan's law gives the former owner what a sale brings above the minimum
  bid (MCL 211.78t). In the game no home sells above it.
- **Who buys.** Among Detroit properties in the treasurer's sales of 2005 to 2015, investors who
  bought in bulk made about nine purchases in ten (Appendix B). The gate's report (section 9)
  counts, period by period, the households that could buy to live and the homes each band could
  take.

for a unit and its answer is a count.

### 7.15 Step B: the opening

**The tables.** **[Record needed]** The census table is one new reference table, for all 3,144
counties, from the Census Bureau's 2024 five-year files: tenure; tenure by race of householder;
tenure by income; mortgage status; the value of owned homes, as counts and as dollars; real estate
tax paid; homes in the building, by tenure; rent. It is built, pinned and loaded as the household
table is today. Counts and dollar totals are totals. A median is shown and is used only as a
check. No rule reads one. Three national sources stand beside it (Appendix B): the Federal
Reserve's accounts by wealth group; the 2024 survey of rental owners; and the 2025 Survey of
Consumer Finances. The first may be copied freely. The terms of the other two are confirmed before
either is committed.

**Placing the homes on Wayne's units.** **[Call]** **[Record needed]** Each step is a declared
split in whole numbers. The gate's report (section 9) prints each step's other bound beside it, on
Wayne's real figures. The Director sees that report before any step is applied.

1. **Who owns.** The census table of tenure by income gives the owners among households above and
   below each income line. The bands are read on that table as the county's households in order of
   income: the top tenth, the next 40% and the bottom 50%. That reading is Designed. Where a band's
   line falls inside an income bracket, the bracket's owners and tenants are split in proportion.
   That gives the owners in each band. The top tenth's owners are shared between the top 1% and the
   next 9% in proportion. Inside a band, owners are placed on units in proportion to households, by
   largest remainder. The rest are tenants. Other bound: owners from the top, so that the bottom
   half has as few as the count allows. The first reading puts tenants in every band, which is what
   the census shows and what lets a tenant with a wage buy.
2. **Who owes on the home.** Every owner in the bottom half has a mortgaged home (section 2).
   **[Director]** The county's other mortgaged homes, and all its homes with no mortgage, are shared
   among the owners of the top three bands in proportion to their owners. **[Call]** The build
   refuses an opening in which the county has more homes with no mortgage than owners in the top
   three bands. Other bound: mortgaged homes in proportion in every band, which gives the bottom
   half equity.
3. **What a home is worth.** The census counts of homes by value are walked from the dearest down,
   filling the bands' owners from the top. A bracket's dollars are its count times its middle value
   (Designed; the top bracket at its lower edge), scaled so that all brackets add to the census
   total for the county. Inside a band every home has one value, the band's dollars divided by its
   homes, so that a unit's households do not differ. Other bound: every band takes the county's
   whole spread of values.
4. **Who holds the rented homes.** **[Director]** for the two ways. The numbers are the author's.
   **[Call]** The rented homes are the tenants' homes and the homes empty for rent.
   - **By the size of the building.** The census counts Wayne's rented homes by the number of homes
     in the building. The 2024 survey of rental owners gives, for the whole country, the part of
     the homes in rental properties that individual owners hold, alone or jointly, at each size of
     property: 65% of single houses, 65% in properties of two to four homes, 21% in those of 5 to
     24, and 6% in larger ones. Each part is applied to Wayne's count at that size. That gives the
     homes that households hold. The rest, mobile homes, and the homes empty for rent are the
     property company's. The parts are Derived from the survey. Their use for Wayne is Designed:
     the survey has no figure below the nation, and it counts properties where the census counts
     buildings.
   - **How sure the parts are.** They leave out the owners the survey could not name. With those
     kept in, as the Census Bureau prints them, individuals hold 60% of single houses and not 65%.
     The part for single houses rests on 377 answers and is good to about five points. A person who
     holds homes through a company of their own is counted as a company, which pulls the other way.
     So the households' part is a band and not a point. The gate's report works both readings, and
     prints Detroit's own counts of landlords beside them (Appendix B).
   - **Between the two bands.** The households' homes are shared between the top 1% and the next 9%
     in proportion to their households, so nine in ten go to the next 9%. That is Designed. No
     source held says which households hold rented homes. The 2025 Survey of Consumer Finances
     may, and the gate tabulates it if it does. Other bound: the Federal Reserve's division of
     business wealth, which gives the top 1% about six parts in ten.
   - **Inside a band,** in proportion to the households of its owner units, by largest remainder.
   - **No mortgage is placed on a let home.**
   - Other bound for the whole step: every rented home with the property company. That was the
     second draft's form.
5. **Opening cash.** The engine gives a household two periods of its needs. An owner's needs no
   longer hold the rent, so an owner opens with two periods of its own housing payments in its
   place: the tax and, if it has one, the mortgage payment. A unit that holds let homes opens with
   two periods of the tax on them as well. **[Call]**

**The rule of thirds with homes.** **[Director]** **[Record needed]** The law binds the opening.

- **Wealth** is a unit's cash and goods, the value of its parts of businesses, and the carrying
  value of the homes it holds less what it owes on them. **All household wealth in the county is the
  sum over the four bands.**
- **Homes cannot carry the whole law.** A mortgage can only lower wealth. Cash is set by need and is
  nearly the same for every household. The rented homes add to the top tenth's wealth, and they are
  too few to give each of its bands a third. Parts of businesses are carried at zero, and every
  household that holds one holds the same. So today nothing can give the top 1% a third.
- **What each band owes on its homes.** The Federal Reserve's accounts give each wealth group's
  mortgage debt as a share of the homes its households live in: 8.4% for the top 1%, 22.7% for the
  next 9% and 29.5% for the next 40% (Derived from the national accounts; their use for Wayne is
  Designed). Each ratio is applied to the value of the homes the band's households live in, and the
  debt is placed on its mortgaged homes, never above their value. The bottom half owes the whole
  value of its homes. The build refuses a band that has debt and no mortgaged home to carry it.
- **The top two bands.** **[Director]** Wayne's parts of businesses are divided between the top 1%
  and the next 9%, and given a value in the books. The two figures are solved together, Calibrated,
  so that the top 1% and the next 9% each hold what the next 40% hold. The value adds no cash and
  no rule reads it. **The division changes who is paid.** Today the top 1% are one in ten of the
  households that hold a part, and receive a tenth of the income from ownership. Solved, they hold
  a much larger part of every business, the property company among them, and receive that part of
  each pay-out. One division serves every business. **[Call]** The sketch the Director chose says
  that the property company's shares go mostly to the top 1%. The solved division decides whether
  they do. If it gives the top 1% half or less, the gate's report says so and the Director chooses.
- **The check beside it.** In the Federal Reserve's accounts for 2026 the top 1% hold 62.5% of what
  the top tenth holds in businesses outside the stock market, and 57.8% of what it holds in shares
  (Appendix B). The gate's report prints the solved division beside those two figures. The Director
  asked to see the split before it is applied, and this is where it is shown.
- **The build refuses the opening, naming the band,** when the bottom half's share of all wealth is
  outside the range ADR305 records for 1913 to 2024 (from −1.8% to 3.7%); when a solved figure
  is below zero; when a mortgage is larger than its home's value; when a band has debt and no
  mortgaged home; or when the census tables do not add up.
- **It then measures the finished opening from the accounts,** and refuses it when any of the top
  three bands is more than five points from a third. That cannot happen if the solving is right. The
  check is there to catch a fault in it.
- **What step A's figures say.** Without homes, Wayne's households open with $5.55 billion of cash
  and $0.57 billion of goods, and the bottom half holds $2.99 billion of that. For the bottom half
  to be under 3.7% of all wealth, the upper bands must hold about $78 billion. Whether Wayne's real
  homes, mortgages and businesses give that is not known until the tables are built. So the gate of
  section 9 builds them and works this arithmetic, as a report, before any rule of step B is
  written.

### 7.19 Quantities that must balance

Every period, or the build refuses:

- The units of a budget group add to the group's households, persons, people at work and people
  looking for work.
- At step A's opening, a group's units add to what the group held before units existed.
- The homes of the units, the property company and the county add to the opening count.
- Homes lived in are never more than homes held.
- The let homes of all holders equal the housed tenant households.
- The rent the property company received is what it kept, what it paid over and what it held back
  for the county, to the last unit of money.
- The parts of businesses and the workers that the forms of a unit carry add to the unit's own.
- A unit's households are its owners in their homes, its housed tenants and its households without
  a home.
- A household or a home is a member of one debt of a kind at most. The households in a form's
  debts are never more than its households, and the homes in its tax debts never more than the
  homes it holds and does not live in.
- An owner form's homes lived in equal its households. The households the county has excused are
  never more than a form's owner households.
- Each debt is the same figure in the debtor's books and the creditor's. The mortgage balances add
  to the lender's book. The land bank is the homes the county holds.
- Each debt closes as its opening figure, plus what was added, less what was paid, less what was
  ended. Each line has a receipt.
- Cash is conserved, and every account's net assets match its statements.

## 12. Known risks

- **The recorded economy already runs down.** Jobs in Wayne fall by about three fifths in the first
  three periods, and this has no ruling. Units will show it more sharply: a unit with no wage has
  only its own relief.
- **The housing business loses the owners' custom.** About 64% of households stop buying Housing
  provision. The service needs one worker for each 160 homes, so the business would keep about
  1,540 of the 27,283 people it opened with. Plan 2 measures this before the rules after it are
  built. The remedy, if one is wanted, is an upkeep bought from that business.
- **Rent may fall through a whole run.** The engine's price rule lowers the rent by a step in every
  period in which the business has spare capacity, toward a floor of a quarter of the opening rent.
  The holders' rent falls with it. The gate's first report shows the price of every period.
- **Tax and mortgage payments take cash out of households.** The property tax collects in the
  county's account above its spending limit. Interest collects in the lender's.
- **The opening may be refused on Wayne's real figures.** The gate exists for that reason.
- **Dividing the businesses' shares by band changes who is paid in Wayne.** The Director chose it
  (section 13). The top 1% receive much more of the income from ownership, and the next 9% less.
- **Buying may be rare.** A tenant needs the price and two periods of its needs in hand.
- **The law's clock is longer than today's runs.** A home is taken at period 43 and offered at
  period 49. Today's recorded runs stop at period 34, and the recorded economy has run down long
  before: jobs fall by three fifths in the first three periods, and the county is first short at
  period 29. Step B's foreclosures and sales mean little until that run-down is ruled on. The
  gate's first report says what it found.
- **The property company's earnings and the lender's leave Wayne,** because each is a member of a
  larger company (sections 7.8 and 7.9). The Director chose to keep it so. The gate's report shows
  how much leaves Wayne.
- **The housing business's crew is paid nothing from about period 28 of today's runs,** while 4,379
  people stay at work. The cause is not known. If the business is out of cash by then, the holders'
  rent and the tax on its homes stop near the period of the first foreclosures. The gate's first
  report finds the cause.
- **A holder's rent needs a new payment and a new tax basis in the engine.** Both change the money
  book's records for every campaign that has homes.
- **The households' part of the rented homes is a national figure.** No source gives it for Wayne,
  and none held says which households hold rented homes. In Wayne's tax returns of 2022, income
  from rent or royalties is on 25,120 returns of 812,790, in every income class, with all of the
  positive net income on returns of $200,000 and more. The Director's ruling places every rented
  home in the top tenth.
- **More units mean more rows.** Step B gives every unit three forms. Most are empty and are not
  shown, but the engine's row limits and the organizer's status line must be checked.
- **Moving households between units re-cuts crews in the middle of a run.** The engine fixes each
  crew's parts at the opening today. Step B needs them to move with the households (section 7.7).
- **Every recorded result of the County Review changes** in step A.
- **The top 1% may be the first to go without in step A.** It is one unit of 7,006 households,
  with no wage and no relief unit. In today's recorded runs a household with a business share is
  paid nothing from ownership in period 1, about $1,160 in period 2, about $2,180 at the highest
  (period 5), under $800 by period 10 and about $120 by period 34. At the opening's prices the
  needs of a household of one person cost $2,526 a period. Section 7.2 names the remedy: the
  division of the shares moves forward. Step A's report on the units shows it, and the Director
  decides.
- **A seller that is short serves its buyers in the order of an identifier.** When a seller
  cannot supply every buyer, the engine supplies them in the order of their orders' identifiers.
  That is so today among the budget groups. With units it can decide which unit goes without.
  Step A does not change it. Its records keep "could not pay" apart from "paid and was not
  supplied", and the gate's first report gives the size of it.
- **This branch stands on a branch that is not merged.** The County Review branch is local and has
  not been pushed.

## 13. The Director's answers

The second draft put four questions and three differences to the Director. They were asked on
20 November in two rounds, each with the choices set out and the author's pick named. These are the
answers. Where the Director added words, they are quoted.

1. **The rule of thirds in step A.** ADR305 says the law binds the opening split of wealth "when
   positions hold accounts of their own". Step A gives them accounts and no homes. **Answer:
   measure the law in step A and bind it in step B, with homes** (section 7.2). "remember that the
   top 1% and the top 9% can also be landlords and own multiple homes they don't live in, so part of
   their class interests especially the 1% thru property management companies, those can accrue
   wealth and income that way too. be sure to factor that in"
2. **The top's two thirds.** Homes cannot give the top 1% a third. **Answer: Wayne's shares in
   businesses get a value in the books and are divided between the top 1% and the next 9%**, so that
   all three upper bands hold the same. The top 1% then receive a much larger part of the income
   from ownership. The other choice kept one part for each owner household. "see previous note
   about landlords, i'll let you decide how to split this up just check it with me before you do"
3. **The tax on a home and relief.** **Answer: it counts in the county's ranking** (section 7.12).
   "yes because IRL local politicians and governments do housing relief and what not through the
   tax system and home ownership"
4. **The county's clock.** The author recommended 6 periods to forfeiture and 13 to foreclosure,
   marked Designed, because a run of thirty periods ends as the first home is taken. **Answer: the
   law's own clock, 13 and 27** (sections 5 and 7.11). "part of what makes babylon interesting is
   that once we program in states each state has different laws and that will lead to different
   behaviors in different jurisdictions so lets keep it according to the law."
5. **Landlords.** The author showed a split after the first two answers. **Answer: both ways, in
   step B.** At the opening the rented homes belong to the top tenth, part through the property
   company and part held directly by households (section 7.7).
6. **Mortgages.** **Answer: existing mortgages only.** No new loan, and the lender takes no home in
   this slice.
7. **The bottom half's owners.** **Answer: they are owners with no equity at the opening.**

**What is still checked with the Director.** The shape of the landlord split is decided. Its
numbers are not: how many rented homes are held each way, on which units, how the shares are
divided between the top 1% and the next 9%, the management part the company keeps, and the value
given to a rented home. The gate's report (section 9) works them on Wayne's real figures and goes
to the Director before any of them is applied.

**Three more answers, and the approval.** Three readers tested this draft before it went to the
Director. They found a fault in what the author had told the Director about the clock, a rule that
the law leaves open, and a fact of the engine. The Director answered each on 20 November.

8. **Where the county's clock starts.** The author had counted 13 and 27 periods from the first
   bill a household misses. Michigan's law counts them from 1 March of the next year. **Answer: the
   law's yearly return** (section 7.11). A home is then taken at about period 43 of a run, and not
   at about period 30 as the Director had been told.
9. **The people in a foreclosed home.** The law gives the county possession and leaves the rest to
   it. The author recommended that they stay until a buyer acts, as Wayne has often done.
   **Answer: they must leave at once.** "this leads to more dramatic events, more displacement,
   more opportunity to intervene so its a more interesting gameplay assumption"
10. **The larger companies.** Wayne's property company and its lender are members of companies
    whose pay-outs go to households in every county, which the sketches the Director had chosen did
    not say. **Answer: keep the larger companies.** The gate's report shows how much of their
    pay-out leaves Wayne.
11. **Approval.** The Director approved the design with these answers written in, and asked for the
    plan of step A.

**Corrected after approval, for the Director to check.** The plan's mapping of the code found
three statements of step A that the code does not bear out. Each is the author's call.

- **A named group in two bands** (section 7.1). The design refused a campaign whose opening would
  cut a named group. Today's Wayne has the four aid donors in two bands. They stay one unit, and
  their positions keep their bands.
- **The roster is made from the opening's own rows, and one unit keeps the group's account**
  (section 7.2).
- **The rule for a household that leaves stands as it is** (section 7.3). No new rule is needed,
  and a test proves it.

Two risks were added to section 12: the top 1% may be the first to go without in step A, and a
seller that is short serves its buyers in the order of an identifier.

**The calls that matter most, to check while reading.** The first is the widest: the author reads
the Director's ruling on the clock as covering every clock, fee and remedy that the law fixes

step B (7.1, 7.7); the order of going without and of paying (7.3, 7.10); tax collected last (7.3);
owners placed by the census table of tenure by income (7.15); rent that is shared and not traced
(7.8); the tax held back from a holder's rent, and that it counts in the county's list (7.8, 7.12);
an owner of any band can buy a home to let after the opening (7.13); how homes are shared when more
can buy than there are (7.13); debts kept by the members that fell behind together (7.9); the

tax goes to the county, whose spending limit does not move (7.9, 7.12).

**How the eviction clock was worked.** Rent is missed on day 0. The demand is served on day 1, and
its 7 days end on day 8. The landlord files on day 9. The trial, or a judgment by default, is on
day 12 at the earliest. The order can issue on day 23, which is 0.82 of a period. If the court
adjourns the trial by 7 to 14 days, the order issues on day 30 to 37. If the tenant has asked for
help with the rent, the court stays the case for up to 28 days, and the order issues at about
day 51, which is 1.8 periods. A longer adjournment, a motion or an appeal takes longer. The game
counts 2 periods (Designed).

**What the law leaves open, and the game designs.**

- The income and asset levels of the exemption above the federal floor, and whether part of the tax
  is excused. A local board sets them.
- Whether the county cuts an old debt, and for whom. The law says that it may.
- The dates of the two sales inside the law's window, and the opening bid at the last one.
- Whether the city or the county takes a home before the sale, and what it does with it.
- What becomes of the people in a foreclosed home. The law ends their claim. Practice has differed
  from year to year. The Director chose that they leave at once.
- The fee on late rent. No limit was found in the law, and a lease sets it. That none exists was
  not confirmed in the statute itself.
- Whether a landlord presses its judgment for unpaid rent.
- The rate and the term of a mortgage.

**Where the game departs from the law.** The tax is billed every period, where the law bills twice
a year, and the city's charges on a late bill before the return are not carried. The return is
placed in the third period of the game year, because the engine's year is not a calendar. A rent
debt is written off when a tenancy ends, where the law gives the landlord a judgment. The law's
other remedies are not in this slice: the hearing before a foreclosure and its notices
(MCL 211.78i, 211.78j), the court's hold for hardship (211.78k), the treasurer's power to hold a
home back (211.78h), payment plans apart from the exemption (211.78q), and exemptions that run for
more than a year (211.7u).

**Not confirmed.** Detroit's own school tax on homes that are not a principal residence. Whether
the court rule on evictions has changed since September 2024. Whether Wayne's Pay As You Stay rests
on the cut of the old debt, on the law's payment agreements (MCL 211.78q), or on both. The federal
poverty line for 2026 was not captured. It is captured with this record.

**The three checks made for the third draft.** A second reader tested each against its sources.
Their full results are kept with the session's notes.

- **The engine.** A business's pay-out cannot carry a holder's rent, because every private business
  pays its company, by parts fixed at the opening. So rent is paid over by a payment of its own
  kind (section 7.8). A payer has one tax policy, so the housing book bills the property tax
  (section 7.9). No part of a business can move after the opening, so the housing book carries the
  parts with the households (section 7.7). The bands are set today after the money is wired, so the
  opening must place them first (section 7.2).
- **The law.** The table above. Of the second draft's figures, the clock of 13 and 27 periods, the
  fees and the minimum bid stood. The exemption, the sale and what a foreclosure does to a lease
  were changed to the law's form. The start of the clock was then corrected to the law's yearly
  return.
- **The data.** The survey of rental owners has no figure below the nation, and no source held says
  which households hold rented homes (section 7.15). The Federal Reserve's accounts give the check
  on the division of the shares. Detroit's counts of landlords changed by a wide margin between two
  editions, so they are a check and not a rule.
